Q4
Write a paragraph in your own words on each of the following: (a) Preparation of soil (b) Sowing (c) Weeding (d) Threshing
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

(a) Preparation of Soil: It is the first and most critical step in crop production. Soil is loosened and turned over using agricultural implements like a plough or cultivator. This process is called ploughing or tilling. Loosening the soil allows roots to penetrate deep into the ground, ensures proper aeration for root respiration, and promotes the growth of earthworms and friendly microbes that decompose organic matter to add humus. Large soil clods are subsequently broken down using a leveller.

(b) Sowing: Sowing is the process of putting seeds into the soil. Before sowing, high-quality, clean, healthy, and disease-free seeds of good variety are selected. Clean seeds can be separated from damaged hollow seeds by placing them in water — good seeds sink while damaged seeds float. Sowing is carried out either manually or mechanically using a seed drill, which ensures seeds are sown at uniform distance and proper depth, protecting them from birds.

(c) Weeding: Weeding is the removal of undesirable wild plants called weeds (e.g. Amaranthus, wild oat) that grow naturally alongside cultivated crop plants. Weeds compete with crops for light, nutrients, water, and space, thereby reducing crop yield. Weeding is performed manually by uprooting or cutting weeds close to the ground using a khurpi (trowel), or chemically by spraying weedicides such as 2,4-D diluted with water before the flowering stage.

(d) Threshing: Threshing is the agricultural process of separating harvested grain seeds from the surrounding chaff or stalks. After crops are harvested and dried, stalks are beaten manually against a hard surface or processed using a mechanical thresher. Modern agricultural practices often employ a Combine machine, which performs both harvesting and threshing simultaneously in the field.

Q6
What is irrigation? Describe two methods of irrigation which conserve water.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Definition of Irrigation: The artificial supply of water to agricultural crops at appropriate time intervals for proper germination, growth, and nutrient transport is called irrigation.

Two Modern Water-Conserving Methods of Irrigation:

  • 1. Drip Irrigation System: In this system, water falls drop by drop directly near the root zone of crop plants through network of narrow pipes or tubes fitted with emitters.
    • Advantage: Minimises water evaporation and seepage loss. Water is not wasted at all.
    • Suitability: Ideal for fruit plants, gardens, trees, and water-scarce arid regions.
  • 2. Sprinkler System: Perpendicular pipes fitted with rotating nozzles at the top are joined to a main pipeline. When water flows under pressure from a pump, it escapes through the rotating nozzles, spraying water uniformly over crops like natural rain.
    • Advantage: Prevents waterlogging and ensures even distribution over uneven ground.
    • Suitability: Highly effective for uneven terrain, coffee plantations, lawn grass, and sandy soil with high infiltration rates.

Q7
If wheat is sown in the kharif season, what would happen? Discuss.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Wheat is a Rabi crop (sown in winter, November to March) that requires cool climate, moderate moisture, and mild temperatures during germination and grain development. If wheat is sown in the Kharif season (monsoon, June to October):

  • Waterlogging and Seed Rotting: Excessive rainfall during the monsoon causes waterlogging in fields, depriving wheat roots of oxygen and leading to root rot.
  • Adverse Temperature and Pest Attacks: High ambient temperature and high humidity impair seed germination, stunt plant growth, and invite severe fungal infections and pest attacks, leading to total crop failure.

Q8
Explain how soil gets affected by the continuous plantation of crops in a field.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Continuous cultivation of crops in the same field without giving the land time to fallow leads to severe soil degradation due to:

  • Nutrient Depletion: Growing crops continuously absorbs specific mineral nutrients (such as Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium) repeatedly from the topsoil, leaving the soil infertile and nutrient-deficient.
  • Decline in Soil Yield & Microbial Activity: Lack of organic matter replenishment reduces microbial decomposer activity and organic humus content, turning soil hard and unproductive.
  • Remedial Practice: Farmers must adopt crop rotation (alternating cereal crops with leguminous plants like pea/gram) or apply organic manure to restore natural soil fertility.
CBSE Marking Scheme
Depletion of essential soil nutrients (N, P, K)1 Mark
Loss of fertility & mention of remedy (crop rotation/manure)1 Mark
Q9
What are weeds? How can we control them?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Definition of Weeds: Undesirable wild plants that grow naturally alongside cultivated crop plants in agricultural fields are called weeds. Examples include Amaranthus (Chaulai), Chenopodium (Bathua), and Wild oat (Javi). They compete with main crops for sunlight, nutrients, water, and space.

Methods of Controlling Weeds (Weeding):

  • 1. Tilling before Sowing: Ploughing the field before planting crops uproots weeds, which then dry up, decompose, and mix into the soil as organic humus.
  • 2. Manual Uprooting & Cutting: Removing weeds physically by uprooting or cutting them close to the ground using a khurpi (trowel) or seed drill at regular intervals.
  • 3. Chemical Control (Weedicides): Spraying specific chemical weedicides like 2,4-D (2,4-Dichlorophenoxyacetic acid) diluted with water during the vegetative growth stage before flowering and seed formation.
CBSE Marking Scheme
Definition of weeds with example1 Mark
Any two control methods clearly explained (Manual / Tilling / Chemical)2 Marks (1 Mark each)

Q4
Can microorganisms be seen with the naked eye? If not, how can they be seen?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

No, microorganisms generally cannot be seen with the naked eye because they are extremely minute microscopic organisms (usually less than 0.1 mm in size).

How they can be observed:

  • Microorganisms like bacteria, protozoa, and micro-algae can only be observed under a high-power compound microscope or electron microscope.
  • Fungi such as bread mould (Rhizopus) growing on moist bread can be observed using a handheld magnifying glass.

Q5
What are the major groups of microorganisms?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Microorganisms are broadly classified into four major groups, alongside viruses:

  • 1. Bacteria: Single-celled prokaryotic organisms. Examples: Lactobacillus, Vibrio cholerae, Spirochetes.
  • 2. Fungi: Non-green saprophytic or parasitic eukaryotic organisms. Examples: Yeast, Penicillium, Bread mould (Rhizopus), Aspergillus.
  • 3. Protozoa: Unicellular heterotrophic animal-like protists. Examples: Amoeba, Paramecium, Plasmodium (malarial parasite).
  • 4. Algae: Simple photosynthetic plant-like aquatic organisms. Examples: Spirogyra, Chlamydomonas.

Note: Viruses (e.g. Influenza, HIV, Poliovirus) are microscopic agents that reproduce strictly inside host cells (plants, animals, bacteria).

CBSE Marking Scheme
Naming all 4 major groups with examples2 Marks (½ Mark per group)
Q6
Name the microorganisms which can fix atmospheric nitrogen in the soil.
1 Mark
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

The key biological nitrogen-fixing microorganisms (bio-fixers) are:

  • Rhizobium bacteria (symbiotic bacteria living in root nodules of leguminous plants like pea and bean).
  • Free-living bacteria such as Azotobacter and Clostridium.
  • Blue-green algae (Cyanobacteria) such as Anabaena and Nostoc.

Q7
Write 10 lines on the usefulness of microorganisms in our lives.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Microorganisms play vital beneficial roles in industry, medicine, agriculture, and environment:

  1. Curd & Cheese Formation: Lactobacillus bacteria multiply in milk to convert lactose sugar into lactic acid, setting milk into curd and cheese.
  2. Baking Industry: Yeast reproduces rapidly in bread dough, producing Carbon dioxide gas (CO₂) during respiration, making bread, cakes, and bhaturas soft and spongy.
  3. Commercial Alcohol Production: Yeast is grown on natural sugars (barley, wheat, fruit juice) to convert sugar into alcohol and wine via fermentation.
  4. Antibiotic Manufacturing: Antibiotics like Penicillin (from Penicillium chrysogenum fungus) and Streptomycin are produced from microbes to cure bacterial infections.
  5. Vaccine Production: Vaccines containing killed or weakened microbes trigger antibody production in human body, granting immunity against smallpox, polio, hepatitis, and measles.
  6. Soil Fertility Enhancement: Rhizobium bacteria and Blue-green algae fix atmospheric nitrogen into nitrogenous compounds, enriching soil fertility naturally.
  7. Environmental Cleaning (Decomposers): Saprophytic bacteria and fungi break down dead organic plant and animal waste into simple inorganic nutrients, cleaning Earth naturally.
  8. Acetic Acid (Vinegar) Production: Acetobacter bacteria are used for large-scale industrial production of acetic acid from alcohol.
  9. Sewage Treatment: Aerobic bacteria decompose organic matter present in sewage water during secondary biological treatment in water treatment plants.
  10. Human Digestive Health: Friendly bacteria residing in human intestine help synthesize Vitamin K and assist in digesting complex carbohydrates.
CBSE Marking Scheme
10 distinct valid points covering industry, agriculture, medicine & environment5 Marks (½ Mark per point)
Q8
Write a short paragraph on the harmful effects of microorganisms.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Disease-causing microorganisms are known as pathogens. Microorganisms exert several harmful impacts on human health, agriculture, and livestock:

  • Human Communicable Diseases: Microbes spread through air, water, food, or physical contact. Bacteria cause Tuberculosis, Typhoid, Cholera; Viruses cause Common cold, Polio, Chickenpox, Hepatitis B; Protozoans cause Malaria and Amoebic Dysentery; Fungi cause skin infections like Ringworm.
  • Animal Diseases: Bacillus anthracis bacteria cause fatal Anthrax disease in cattle. Foot and mouth disease in cattle is caused by a virus.
  • Plant Diseases & Crop Loss: Microbes reduce agricultural crop yields. Citrus canker is caused by bacteria, Rust of wheat by fungi, and Yellow vein mosaic of bhindi (okra) by a virus.
  • Food Spoilage & Poisoning: Bacteria and fungi decompose stored food, producing foul odors, sour tastes, and toxic substances (mycotoxins). Consuming spoiled food leads to food poisoning, severe vomiting, and dehydration.

Human communicable diseases (with pathogens & examples)1 Mark
Animal & plant diseases1 Mark
Food spoilage & food poisoning1 Mark
Q9
What are antibiotics? What precautions must be taken while taking antibiotics?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Definition of Antibiotics: Antibiotics are chemical medicines manufactured from microorganisms (fungi and bacteria) that kill or inhibit the growth of disease-causing pathogenic bacteria inside the human body. Examples: Penicillin, Streptomycin, Tetracycline, Erythromycin.

Important Precautions while taking Antibiotics:

  1. Doctor's Prescription: Antibiotics should be taken strictly under the prescription of a qualified registered doctor.
  2. Complete the Full Course: The full prescribed course must be finished even if symptoms disappear. Stopping early allows surviving bacteria to develop antibiotic resistance.
  3. Avoid Unnecessary Consumption: Taking antibiotics when not needed makes them less effective during future genuine infections and kills useful gut bacteria.
  4. Ineffective against Viral Infections: Antibiotics kill bacteria but are completely ineffective against viral infections like common cold, flu, or viral fever.
