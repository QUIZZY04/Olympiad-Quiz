Chapter 1: Exploring the Investigative World of Science

NCERT Curiosity Exercise Solutions — CBSE Word Count & Marking Scheme

Q1
Write any one question that you are curious about regarding the natural world around you. How would you go about investigating it scientifically?
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~70 words)

Question of Curiosity: Why does food cook faster in a pressure cooker than in an open vessel?

Scientific Investigation Steps:

  • 1. Observation: Notice that the same amount of food takes less time in a pressure cooker.
  • 2. Hypothesis: Increased pressure inside the cooker raises the boiling point of water above 100°C.
  • 3. Experiment: Cook equal amounts of rice in open and pressure cooker vessels, measuring time.
  • 4. Conclusion: Higher temperature due to increased pressure speeds up cooking.
CBSE Marking Scheme
A valid curiosity question from nature1 Mark
Forming a hypothesis related to the question1 Mark
Designing an investigation/experiment to test it1 Mark
Q2
What is the difference between an observation and an inference? Give one example of each from daily life.
2 Marks (30–50 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~50 words)

Observation is a direct, factual record of what is seen, heard, or measured using our senses or instruments, without interpretation. Example: The sky looks dark and cloudy.

Inference is a logical conclusion or explanation drawn from observations. Example: It is going to rain soon (inferred from the dark clouds).

CBSE Marking Scheme
Definition and example of observation1 Mark
Definition and example of inference1 Mark
Q3
List the steps of the scientific method in the correct order and briefly explain each step.
5 Marks (80–120 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~110 words)

Steps of the Scientific Method:

  • 1. Observation: Carefully observe a natural phenomenon or problem using senses or instruments and record it accurately.
  • 2. Question: Frame a specific, testable question based on the observation (e.g., "Why does this happen?").
  • 3. Hypothesis: Propose a logical, testable explanation or prediction as a possible answer to the question.
  • 4. Experiment: Design and perform a controlled experiment, changing only one variable (independent variable) while keeping others constant.
  • 5. Data Collection & Analysis: Record measurements and observations systematically; look for patterns in data.
  • 6. Conclusion: Accept or reject the hypothesis based on evidence from the experiment.
  • 7. Communication: Share findings with others so they can verify and build upon the results.
CBSE Marking Scheme
Steps 1–2 (Observation and Question)1 Mark
Step 3 (Hypothesis)1 Mark
Step 4 (Controlled Experiment)1 Mark
Step 5 (Data Analysis)1 Mark
Steps 6–7 (Conclusion and Communication)1 Mark
Q4
What is a hypothesis? Why must a hypothesis be testable? Give one example.
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~75 words)

Definition: A hypothesis is a proposed, logical, and tentative explanation or prediction made to answer a scientific question, based on prior knowledge and observations.

Why Testable: A hypothesis must be testable so that it can be confirmed or disproved through controlled experiments and observation. A hypothesis that cannot be tested remains mere speculation, not science.

Example: "Plants grow taller when watered daily compared to plants watered once a week." — This can be tested by growing identical plants under controlled conditions with different watering frequencies.

CBSE Marking Scheme
Definition of hypothesis1 Mark
Reason why it must be testable1 Mark
Correct example of a testable hypothesis1 Mark
Q5
Differentiate between qualitative and quantitative observations. Give two examples of each.
2 Marks (30–50 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~45 words)

Qualitative Observations describe characteristics using words (colour, smell, texture, shape) without numbers. Examples: The flower is red; the substance has a pungent smell.

Quantitative Observations involve measurable data expressed in numbers with units. Examples: The plant is 25 cm tall; the temperature is 37°C.

🎯 Competency Based Questions (CBQ) — Chapter 1CBSE Pattern
📝 Case Study Based
Q1. Priya noticed that the bread she left uncovered on the kitchen counter turned mouldy after two days. She wanted to investigate whether moisture affects the rate of mould growth. She set up two identical slices of bread — one kept dry in an open box, and one kept in a closed container with a wet cotton ball. After 3 days, the moist slice showed heavy mould growth while the dry slice showed none.

Based on the above passage: (a) What was Priya's hypothesis? (b) What was the independent variable in her experiment? (c) What conclusion can be drawn from the results?
(a) Hypothesis: Mould grows faster on bread kept in moist conditions compared to dry conditions.
(b) Independent Variable: The level of moisture (presence or absence of moisture).
(c) Conclusion: Moisture promotes the growth of mould on bread. The dependent variable (mould growth) was directly affected by the independent variable (moisture).
⚖️ Assertion–Reason
Q2. Assertion (A): In a scientific experiment, only one variable should be changed at a time.
Reason (R): Changing multiple variables simultaneously makes it impossible to determine which variable caused the observed effect.

Options: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
(B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
(C) A is true but R is false.  (D) A is false but R is true.
Correct Answer: (A)
Both statements are true, and R correctly explains A. In a controlled experiment, only the independent variable is changed; all other variables (controlled variables) are kept constant so that cause-and-effect relationships can be accurately identified.
🧠 HOTS (Higher Order Thinking)
Q3. A scientist performs an experiment and gets results that do NOT support her hypothesis. Should she consider the experiment a failure? Justify your answer with scientific reasoning.
Answer: No, the experiment is not a failure. In science, a rejected hypothesis is equally valuable — it eliminates one possible explanation and narrows the search for the correct answer. The scientist should review her experimental design, check for errors, reformulate the hypothesis, and repeat the investigation. Many important discoveries (e.g., discovery of penicillin) arose from unexpected results. Negative results are a valid part of the scientific process.

Chapter 2: The Invisible Living World: Beyond Our Naked Eye

NCERT Curiosity Exercise Solutions — CBSE Word Count & Marking Scheme

Q1
Name the five major groups of microorganisms and give one example of each.
2 Marks (30–50 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~45 words)

The five major groups of microorganisms are:

  1. Bacteria — e.g., Lactobacillus (curd-forming bacterium)
  2. Fungi — e.g., Penicillium (antibiotic-producing mould)
  3. Protozoa — e.g., Plasmodium (causes malaria)
  4. Algae — e.g., Spirogyra (green filamentous alga)
  5. Viruses — e.g., Influenza virus (causes flu)
CBSE Marking Scheme

Q2
Distinguish between a plant cell and a bacterial cell with respect to: (a) presence of nucleus (b) cell wall material (c) size.
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~65 words)

(a) Nucleus: Plant cells have a well-defined true nucleus enclosed by a nuclear membrane (eukaryotic). Bacterial cells lack a true nucleus — their genetic material (DNA) is found in an undefined region called the nucleoid (prokaryotic).

(b) Cell Wall: Plant cell walls are made of cellulose. Bacterial cell walls are made of peptidoglycan (murein).

(c) Size: Plant cells are much larger (10–100 µm). Bacteria are extremely small (0.1–10 µm).

CBSE Marking Scheme
Nucleus difference (true nucleus vs. nucleoid)1 Mark
Cell wall material (cellulose vs. peptidoglycan)1 Mark

Q3
In Aanandi's experiment, she placed yeast and sugar solution in a test tube and attached a balloon over its mouth. (a) What happened to the balloon after some time? (b) Why did this happen? (c) What happens if the gas produced is passed through lime water?
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~70 words)

(a) The balloon gradually inflated (swelled up) after some time.

(b) Yeast is a fungus that carries out fermentation — it breaks down sugar (glucose) into alcohol and carbon dioxide (CO₂) gas under anaerobic conditions. The CO₂ gas produced gets trapped in the balloon and inflates it.

(c) When the CO₂ gas is bubbled through lime water [Ca(OH)₂], it reacts with it to form calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), turning the lime water milky white. This confirms the presence of CO₂.

CBSE Marking Scheme

Lime water turns milky due to CO₂1 Mark
Q4
List any four beneficial roles of microorganisms in our daily lives.
2 Marks (30–50 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~45 words)

Four beneficial roles of microorganisms:

  1. Lactobacillus bacteria convert milk into curd through fermentation.
  2. Yeast is used in baking (bread, cakes) by producing CO₂ that makes the dough rise.
  3. Rhizobium bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen in soil, enriching its fertility.
  4. Penicillium fungi produce life-saving antibiotics like Penicillin.

Q5
Why are viruses not classified as living organisms, yet they can cause diseases in living beings? Explain.
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~75 words)

Why Viruses are not fully living: Viruses are considered on the borderline of living and non-living. They have no cells, no cytoplasm, no metabolism, and cannot carry out life processes (nutrition, respiration, excretion) on their own. Outside a host, they are inert particles.

Yet they cause diseases because: When viruses enter a living cell, they hijack the host cell's machinery (ribosomes, enzymes) to replicate themselves, producing thousands of copies that destroy host cells, causing symptoms of disease (e.g., COVID-19, Influenza, Chickenpox).

CBSE Marking Scheme
No cells, no metabolism — acellular nature of viruses1 Mark
Hijacking host cell machinery for replication1 Mark

🎯 Competency Based Questions (CBQ) — Chapter 2CBSE Pattern
📝 Case Study Based
Q1. Ravi observed that curd forms faster on hot summer days than in winter. His teacher told him that the bacteria responsible for forming curd are sensitive to temperature. Ravi set up an investigation: he placed milk with a spoonful of curd in containers at 10°C, 25°C, and 45°C and observed after 6 hours.

(a) Name the bacterium responsible for curd formation. (b) At which temperature would curd form best, and why? (c) What does this experiment show about the nature of microorganisms?
(a) Lactobacillus bacteria are responsible for curd formation.
(b) Curd would form best at 25°C–37°C (warm temperature), as Lactobacillus grows optimally at warm temperatures, fermenting milk lactose into lactic acid, which coagulates the milk protein (casein).
(c) The experiment shows that microorganism activity is highly sensitive to environmental conditions, especially temperature — too cold slows their metabolism; too hot kills them.
⚖️ Assertion–Reason
Q2. Assertion (A): Antibiotics are effective against bacterial infections but not against viral infections.
Reason (R): Antibiotics work by targeting structures specific to bacterial cells (like peptidoglycan cell walls), which viruses do not possess.

(A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
(B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
(C) A is true but R is false.  (D) A is false but R is true.
Correct Answer: (A)
Both A and R are true. Antibiotics interfere with bacterial-specific structures and processes (cell wall synthesis, protein synthesis in bacterial ribosomes). Since viruses are acellular and use host machinery, antibiotics have no target to act on in viruses, making them completely ineffective against viral diseases.
🧠 HOTS
Q3. Doctors warn against excessive or unnecessary use of antibiotics. Why? What is "antibiotic resistance" and why is it a global health threat?
Answer: When antibiotics are overused, bacteria that have natural mutations making them resistant survive and multiply, passing on resistance genes. This produces "superbugs" that cannot be killed by standard antibiotics. Antibiotic resistance is a global health threat because infections that were once easily treatable (like TB, pneumonia) may become fatal if resistance spreads widely, and developing new antibiotics is a slow, expensive process.

Chapter 3: Health: The Ultimate Treasure

NCERT Curiosity Exercise Solutions — CBSE Word Count & Marking Scheme

Q1
What is the difference between communicable and non-communicable diseases? Give two examples of each.
2 Marks (30–50 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~45 words)

Communicable diseases are caused by pathogens (bacteria, viruses) and can spread from one person to another through air, water, contact, or vectors. Examples: Tuberculosis, Cholera.

Non-communicable diseases are caused by genetic factors, lifestyle, or environmental reasons and do not spread from person to person. Examples: Diabetes, Heart disease.

CBSE Marking Scheme
Communicable diseases definition + 2 examples1 Mark
Non-communicable diseases definition + 2 examples1 Mark
Q2
How does vaccination help protect us from diseases? Explain with the concept of immunity.
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~75 words)

Vaccination involves introducing a weakened or killed pathogen (or its proteins) into the body through injection or oral doses. This stimulates the body's immune system to produce specific antibodies without causing the actual disease.

These antibodies remain in memory cells. When the same pathogen attacks in the future, the immune system recognises it rapidly and destroys it before it can cause illness. This protection is called acquired immunity. Examples: Polio vaccine, BCG (TB), COVID-19 vaccine.

CBSE Marking Scheme
Introduction of weakened/killed pathogen via vaccine1 Mark
Immune system produces antibodies — memory cells1 Mark
Rapid recognition & destruction on future exposure1 Mark
Q3
Explain why completing a full course of antibiotics is important, even if you feel better before finishing.
2 Marks (30–50 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~45 words)

When we stop antibiotics early, the weaker bacteria are killed first, but some stronger, resistant bacteria survive. These remaining bacteria multiply and cause the infection to return — now with a strain that is resistant to the antibiotic. Completing the full course ensures all bacteria, including resistant ones, are eliminated.

CBSE Marking Scheme
Stronger/resistant bacteria survive if course is stopped early1 Mark
Full course eliminates all bacteria, preventing resistance1 Mark
Q4
List five personal hygiene practices that help prevent the spread of communicable diseases.
2 Marks (30–50 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~40 words)

Five hygiene practices to prevent communicable diseases:

  1. Washing hands with soap before meals and after using the toilet.
  2. Drinking clean, boiled, or purified water.
  3. Covering mouth and nose while coughing or sneezing.
  4. Eating freshly cooked, covered food to prevent contamination.
  5. Getting vaccinated as per the immunisation schedule.

A person can be free of disease but still be unhealthy due to chronic stress or social isolation.

Chapter 4: Electricity: Magnetic and Heating Effects

NCERT Curiosity Exercise Solutions — CBSE Word Count & Marking Scheme

Q1
What is the heating effect of electric current? Name three household appliances that work on this principle.
2 Marks (30–50 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~40 words)

Heating Effect: When electric current flows through a conductor (wire), the moving electrons collide with atoms in the conductor, producing heat energy. This is the heating effect of electric current (Joule's heating effect).

Appliances: Electric iron, electric kettle, electric toaster (also: room heater, electric bulb filament).

CBSE Marking Scheme
Definition of heating effect of current1 Mark
Three correct household appliances1 Mark
Q2
Explain how an electromagnet is different from a permanent magnet. Give one practical application of an electromagnet.
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~70 words)

Permanent Magnet: A permanent magnet has a constant, fixed magnetic field that cannot be switched on or off. It is made of magnetised steel or alloys (e.g., bar magnet, horseshoe magnet).

Electromagnet: An electromagnet is made by winding an insulated copper coil around an iron core and passing electric current through it. Its magnetic property exists only when current flows — the magnetism can be turned on/off by switching the current. Its strength can also be varied.

Application: Electric bells — the electromagnet attracts an iron strip to ring the bell; used in cranes at scrap yards to lift heavy metal objects; MRI machines in hospitals.

CBSE Marking Scheme
Permanent magnet — fixed, always-on magnetic field1 Mark
Electromagnet — coil + iron core + current; switchable1 Mark
Practical application with explanation1 Mark
Q3
What is the role of a fuse in an electric circuit? What happens if a fuse with a higher rating than required is used?
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~75 words)

Role of Fuse: A fuse is a short piece of thin wire (made of a low melting-point alloy like tin-lead) connected in series in an electric circuit. It acts as a safety device. When excess current (due to short circuit or overload) passes through the circuit, the fuse wire heats up rapidly and melts, breaking the circuit and preventing damage to appliances and fire hazards.

Effect of Higher Rating Fuse: If a fuse with a higher rating than required is used, it will not melt even when dangerous excess current flows. This defeats the purpose of the fuse, allowing the excess current to damage appliances, melt wires, and potentially cause short circuits or electrical fires.

CBSE Marking Scheme
Fuse definition — low melting point wire, series connection1 Mark
Melts on excess current — breaks circuit, safety device1 Mark
Higher rating fuse — won't melt, risk of fire and damage1 Mark
Q4
State Oersted's discovery. What does it tell us about the relationship between electricity and magnetism?
2 Marks (30–50 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~45 words)

Oersted's Discovery (1820): Hans Christian Oersted discovered that when electric current flows through a wire, it creates a magnetic field around the wire. He observed that a compass needle placed near a current-carrying wire deflected from its normal North-South direction.

Relationship: Oersted's discovery established that electricity and magnetism are interrelated — moving electric charges (current) produce a magnetic effect, forming the basis of electromagnetism.

CBSE Marking Scheme
Current-carrying wire creates magnetic field — Oersted's finding1 Mark
Electricity and magnetism are interrelated (electromagnetism)1 Mark
🎯 Competency Based Questions (CBQ) — Chapter 4CBSE Pattern
📝 Case Study Based
Q1. Meera was making an electromagnet using a nail, copper wire, and a battery. She noticed that when she increased the number of coils of wire around the nail, the electromagnet could lift more paper clips than before.

(a) What does increasing the number of coils do to the strength of the electromagnet? (b) Name two other ways to increase the strength of an electromagnet. (c) Why is iron used as the core instead of copper?
(a) Increasing the number of coils (turns) increases the strength of the electromagnet — more coils produce a stronger magnetic field.
(b) (i) Increasing the electric current through the coil. (ii) Using a soft iron core instead of a non-magnetic material.
(c) Iron is used because it is a soft magnetic material — it is easily magnetised when current flows and easily loses its magnetism when current stops, making the electromagnet switchable. Copper is non-magnetic.
⚖️ Assertion–Reason
Q2. Assertion (A): Nichrome wire is used as the heating element in electric appliances like toasters and room heaters.
Reason (R): Nichrome has a high electrical resistance and a high melting point, making it suitable for generating and withstanding high heat.

(A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.  (B) Both true but R is not correct explanation.  (C) A true, R false.  (D) A false, R true.
Correct Answer: (A)
Both A and R are true. Nichrome (nickel-chromium alloy) has high resistivity — it converts significant electrical energy into heat (Joule's law: H = I²Rt). Its very high melting point (~1400°C) ensures it does not melt at operating temperatures, making it ideal for heating elements.
🧠 HOTS
Q3. An electric bell stops ringing when the iron strip inside touches the electromagnet. Explain the mechanism by which the bell keeps ringing automatically in a circuit.
Answer: When current flows, the electromagnet attracts the iron strip (armature), which strikes the bell gong and rings it. However, when the armature moves toward the magnet, it breaks the circuit at the contact screw, stopping the current. With no current, the electromagnet loses its magnetism and the spring pulls the armature back to its original position, completing the circuit again. Current flows once more and the cycle repeats rapidly, causing the bell to ring continuously.

Chapter 5: Exploring Forces

NCERT Curiosity Exercise Solutions — CBSE Word Count & Marking Scheme

Q1
Distinguish between contact forces and non-contact forces. Give two examples of each.
2 Marks (30–50 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~45 words)

Contact Forces act only when two objects are physically touching each other. Examples: Muscular force (pushing a table), Friction (between shoe and floor).

Non-contact Forces can act on objects without physical contact, even across empty space. Examples: Gravitational force (Earth pulling the moon), Magnetic force (magnet attracting iron filings).

Q2
What is friction? State two advantages and two disadvantages of friction in everyday life.
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~70 words)

Friction is a force that opposes the relative motion (or tendency to motion) between two surfaces in contact. It acts in the direction opposite to the direction of motion.

Advantages:

  • Enables walking — without friction between shoes and ground, we would slip.
  • Brakes on vehicles work due to friction between brake pads and wheels.

Disadvantages:

  • Causes wear and tear of machinery parts, tyres, and shoe soles.
  • Generates unwanted heat in engines, reducing their efficiency.

Q3
Explain the difference between mass and weight. A student weighs 50 kg on Earth. What would be her weight on the Moon? (g on Earth = 10 m/s², g on Moon = 1.6 m/s²)
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~70 words)

Mass: The amount of matter in an object. It remains constant everywhere (SI unit: kilogram, kg).

Weight: The gravitational force with which a planet pulls an object. Weight = Mass × g. It varies from planet to planet (SI unit: Newton, N).

Calculation:

Weight on Earth = 50 × 10 = 500 N

Weight on Moon = 50 × 1.6 = 80 N

Her weight on the Moon would be 80 N — about one-sixth of her weight on Earth.

Chapter 6: Pressure, Winds, Storms, and Cyclones

NCERT Curiosity Exercise Solutions — CBSE Word Count & Marking Scheme

Q1
Define pressure. State how pressure changes with area when force is constant. Give one practical example.
2 Marks (30–50 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~45 words)

Pressure is defined as the force acting perpendicularly per unit area of a surface. Formula: Pressure = Force / Area (SI unit: Pascal, Pa).

Inverse Relation: When force is constant, pressure is inversely proportional to area — smaller area means greater pressure.

Example: A sharp knife cuts easily because the small area of the blade concentrates force, producing high pressure on the object being cut.

CBSE Marking Scheme
Definition and formula (P = F/A)1 Mark
Inverse relationship + correct example1 Mark
Q2
Explain how unequal heating of the Earth's surface causes winds to blow.
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~75 words)

The Sun heats the Earth's surface unequally — land heats up faster than water, and equatorial regions receive more heat than polar regions.

When a region heats up, the air above it becomes warm, expands, becomes lighter, and rises. This creates a region of low pressure at the surface.

In cooler adjacent regions, the air is denser and heavier, creating high pressure. Air always moves from high pressure to low pressure regions — this movement of air is called wind. The greater the pressure difference, the stronger the wind.

CBSE Marking Scheme
Unequal heating — warm air rises, creating low pressure1 Mark
Cool regions have high pressure1 Mark
Wind blows from high pressure to low pressure1 Mark
Q3
How are cyclones formed? What safety measures should people in coastal areas follow during a cyclone warning?
5 Marks (80–120 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~110 words)

Formation of Cyclones: Cyclones form over warm tropical ocean waters (above 26°C). The warm, moist air over the ocean rises rapidly, creating a large zone of very low pressure at the surface. Surrounding cooler air rushes in to fill this low-pressure zone. Due to Earth's rotation (Coriolis effect), this rushing air begins to rotate in a spiral (anticlockwise in Northern Hemisphere). As the rotating winds gain speed and the system intensifies, it forms a cyclone with a calm centre called the eye. Cyclones bring extremely strong winds, torrential rain, and storm surges to coastal areas.

Safety Measures:

  • Evacuate to designated cyclone shelters immediately on receiving warnings.
  • Stock up on emergency supplies — drinking water, food, medicines, torches.
  • Stay indoors, away from windows and doors during the storm.
  • Do not venture near the sea or rivers during and after the cyclone.
  • Switch off all electrical appliances and gas connections.

Chapter 7: Particulate Nature of Matter

NCERT Curiosity Exercise Solutions — CBSE Word Count & Marking Scheme

Q1
State the kinetic theory of matter. How does it explain the different states of matter — solid, liquid, and gas?
5 Marks (80–120 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~110 words)

Kinetic Theory of Matter: All matter is made up of tiny particles (atoms or molecules) that are in constant, random motion. The energy of these particles determines the state of matter.

Solids: Particles are tightly packed in fixed positions. They vibrate in place but do not move freely. Strong intermolecular forces maintain a definite shape and volume.

Liquids: Particles are close together but can slide past each other. They have moderate kinetic energy. Liquids have a definite volume but take the shape of their container.

Gases: Particles are far apart with very weak intermolecular forces and very high kinetic energy. Gas particles move rapidly and randomly in all directions, filling any container completely — no definite shape or volume.

Temperature increases the kinetic energy of particles, causing matter to change from solid → liquid → gas.

CBSE Marking Scheme
Statement of kinetic theory (particles in constant motion)1 Mark
Solid — tightly packed, vibrate in place, definite shape1 Mark
Liquid — close, can slide, definite volume, no fixed shape1 Mark
Gas — far apart, high energy, no definite shape or volume1 Mark
Temperature increases kinetic energy → state changes1 Mark
Q2
What is diffusion? How does the particulate nature of matter explain the process of diffusion?
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~70 words)

Diffusion is the spontaneous mixing and spreading of particles of one substance into another due to their random motion, moving from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration, until uniform distribution is achieved.

Explanation using particulate nature: Since all matter consists of constantly moving particles with spaces between them, particles of one substance can move into the intermolecular spaces of another. For example, when a perfume bottle is opened in a room, fragrance molecules rapidly diffuse through air particles, spreading the scent throughout the room without any external force.

Explanation using inter-particle spaces and motion1 Mark
Correct example (perfume/gas diffusion)1 Mark
Q3
Why do solids have a fixed shape but liquids and gases do not? Use the particulate model to explain.
2 Marks (30–50 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~45 words)

Solids have a fixed shape because their particles are held tightly together by strong intermolecular forces, allowing only vibration in fixed positions — particles cannot flow past each other.

Liquids and Gases have no fixed shape because their particles have weaker intermolecular forces and enough kinetic energy to slide past each other (liquids) or move freely in all directions (gases), taking the shape of their container.

Chapter 8: Nature of Matter: Elements, Compounds, and Mixtures

NCERT Curiosity Exercise Solutions — CBSE Word Count & Marking Scheme

Q1
Differentiate between elements, compounds, and mixtures with one example each.
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~70 words)

Element: A pure substance made of only one kind of atom that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means. Example: Oxygen (O₂) — consists only of oxygen atoms.

Compound: A pure substance formed when two or more elements chemically combine in a fixed ratio. Its properties are entirely different from its constituent elements. Example: Water (H₂O) — hydrogen and oxygen combine in 2:1 ratio.

Mixture: A combination of two or more substances mixed together in any ratio without a chemical reaction. Components retain their original properties and can be separated physically. Example: Saltwater — salt and water mixed together.

How are compounds different from mixtures? Give three points of difference.

  • 1. Formation: Compounds are formed by a chemical reaction between elements in a fixed ratio; Mixtures are formed by simply physically mixing substances in any ratio.

Q2
What is solubility? State any three factors that affect the solubility of a substance.
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~70 words)

Solubility: The maximum amount of a solute (in grams) that can dissolve in 100 g of solvent at a given temperature to form a saturated solution.

Three Factors Affecting Solubility:

  • 1. Temperature: Solubility of most solid solutes increases with increasing temperature (e.g., more sugar dissolves in hot water).
  • 2. Nature of Solute and Solvent: "Like dissolves like" — polar solvents (water) dissolve polar/ionic solutes (salt, sugar); non-polar solvents (oil) dissolve non-polar solutes.
  • 3. Pressure (for gases): Solubility of a gas in a liquid increases with increasing pressure (Henry's Law — explains fizzy drinks under pressure).

How would you separate a mixture of sand, salt, and iron filings? Describe the method step by step.

✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~75 words)

Separation Method (Step by Step):

Q1
State the laws of reflection of light. Give one practical application of a plane mirror.
3 Marks (50–80 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~70 words)

Laws of Reflection:

  • 1. First Law: The angle of incidence (∠i) is always equal to the angle of reflection (∠r). Both angles are measured from the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence.

Application of Plane Mirror: Used in periscopes (to see around corners and over obstacles in submarines), dressing mirrors, solar reflectors, and kaleidoscopes.

Q2
Compare concave and convex mirrors. Name one practical use of each.
2 Marks (30–50 Words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Word Count: ~45 words)

Concave Mirror: Inward-curved (converging) mirror. Converges light rays at the focus. Produces real, inverted, magnified images when object is beyond focus; virtual, erect, magnified image when object is close. Use: In torches, headlights, solar cookers, dentist's mirror.
