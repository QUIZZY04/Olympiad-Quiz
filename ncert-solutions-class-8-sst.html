        Q2
        What is the problem with the periodisation of Indian history that James Mill offers?
        3 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        James Mill, a Scottish economist and political philosopher, published a massive three-volume work, A History of British India (1817), in which he divided Indian history into three distinct periods: Hindu, Muslim, and British. The severe problems with this periodisation include:

        • Religious Bias & Over-simplification: Mill categorized entire historical eras solely on the basis of the religion of the ruling monarchs. This ignores the fact that a variety of faiths existed simultaneously across India in all periods.
        • Eurocentric & Colonial Mindset: Mill believed that all Asian societies were at a lower level of civilization than Europe. He falsely portrayed pre-British India as a dark age dominated by religious intolerance, caste taboos, and superstitious practices.
        • Neglect of Socio-Economic Diversity: Subdividing history by ruler religion completely overlooks the vibrant socio-economic transformations, local trade networks, agricultural developments, and ordinary people's lives during these periods.

        Q3
        Why did the British preserve official documents?
        3 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        The British imperial administration placed immense value on written records and documentation. They preserved official records meticulously due to the following reasons:

        • Administrative Continuity & Accountability: The British believed that every policy, instruction, plan, decision, agreement, and investigation had to be clearly written down. This enabled future administrators to study, debate, and track past administrative precedents easily.
        • Creation of Institutional Memory: Preserved documents served as permanent reference materials for administrative queries, legal disputes, land revenue calculations, and border demarcation.
        • Specialized Record Rooms: To safeguard these documents, the British established record rooms attached to all administrative institutions — from Collectorates and Commissionerates to Law Courts and Secretariats. Furthermore, specialized institutions like Archives and Museums were established.

        Q4
        How will the information historians get from old newspapers be different from that found in police reports?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        Historians analyze multiple primary sources to reconstruct an unbiased picture of the past. Information extracted from old newspapers differs significantly from police reports in perspective, purpose, content, and subjectivity:

        1. Perspective & Viewpoint: Police reports are official state records reflecting the perspective of colonial authorities. They tend to portray public protests, peasant rebellions, or worker strikes as unlawful disruptions, riots, or threats to law and order. In contrast, independent newspapers captured public sentiments, nationalist views, and common grievances.
        2. Target Audience & Purpose: Police reports were written for confidential administrative reviews, criminal prosecutions, and internal government surveillance. Newspaper reports were produced for public readership, aimed at shaping public opinion, critiquing government policies, and highlighting social issues.
        3. Scope of Coverage: Police reports strictly documented crimes, revolutionary activities, tax evasions, and law enforcement operations. Old newspapers provided a broader cultural and socio-economic context, including trade updates, artistic movements, public debates, editorials, and regional news.
        4. Objectivity vs. Bias: While police reports frequently minimized administrative failures and exaggerated police achievements, newspaper reporting could be influenced by editor preferences or political censorship under British Press Acts. Thus, historians must cross-examine both sources to extract accurate historical truth.

        Q5
        Can you think of examples of surveys in India today? What can a historian derive from such surveys?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        Modern Survey Examples in India:

        • Government Census & Educational Surveys: The Indian Government conducts the National Decennial Census and surveys like UDISE (Unified District Information System for Education) and NSSO (National Sample Survey Office) to gather empirical data on literacy rates, school enrollments, drop-out rates, and youth demographics.
        • Commercial Market Surveys: Commercial enterprises, such as toy manufacturers or tech companies, utilize consumer research surveys, online feedback forms, focus groups, and digital analytics to evaluate children's preferences, purchasing power, and trending interests.

        What Historians Derive from Modern Surveys:

        1. Demographic Patterns: Future historians can trace population growth rates, gender ratios, urbanization levels, and literacy trends across different Indian states.
        2. Socio-Economic Conditions: Educational surveys reveal regional disparities, government expenditure effectiveness, and educational equity across gender and social classes.
        3. Cultural Trends & Consumer Behavior: Commercial surveys provide insights into popular culture, changing lifestyles, technological adoption, and economic priorities of 21st-century Indian youth.

        Q5
        What were the causes of the Battle of Plassey (1757)?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        The Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757) was a pivotal turning point in Indian history. The major causes included:

        1. Misuse of Trade Privileges (Dastaks): East India Company officials routinely misused duty-free trade passes (dastaks) meant for company trade to carry on illegal private trade without paying local taxes to the Nawab of Bengal.
        2. Fortification of Calcutta without Permission: The Company began fortifying Fort William in Calcutta without the approval of Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah, directly challenging the Nawab's sovereign authority.
        3. Sheltering Government Fugitives: Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah was infuriated when the Company provided asylum to Krishna Das, a political fugitive who had absconded with state funds.
        4. Nawab's Attack on Kasimbazar & Fort William: When the Company refused to stop fortification or pay taxes, Siraj-ud-Daulah captured the English factory at Kasimbazar and occupied Fort William in Calcutta.
        5. Conspiracy with Mir Jafar: Lord Robert Clive hatched a secret conspiracy with Mir Jafar (the Nawab's Commander-in-Chief), promising to make him Nawab of Bengal in exchange for betraying Siraj-ud-Daulah on the battlefield.

        Q6
        Explain the System of Subsidiary Alliance introduced by Lord Wellesley.
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        The Subsidiary Alliance System was an aggressive diplomatic and expansionist tool used by Governor-General Lord Wellesley (1798–1805) to bring Indian princely states under British paramountcy:

        1. Disarmament of Princely Forces: An Indian ruler entering into a Subsidiary Alliance was forced to disband his independent standing army.
        2. Stationing of British Troops: The ruler had to maintain British troops within his territory, ostensibly for protection against foreign invasions and internal rebellions.
        3. Subsidiary Maintenance Payments: The ruler was required to pay fixed annual cash subsidies to the British for maintaining these troops. If a ruler failed to make payments on time, a portion of his territory was ceded to the British as penalty (e.g., Nawab of Awadh lost over half his territory in 1801).
        4. Appointment of a British Resident: A British Resident was posted at the ruler's court, who progressively interfered in internal state administration, appointments, and governance.
        5. Surrender of Foreign Relations: The state could not enter into alliances or wage war with any other power without prior British consent.

        Q7
        In what ways was the administration of the Company different from that of Indian rulers?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        The East India Company introduced a bureaucratic and centralized colonial administrative structure that differed fundamentally from traditional Indian monarchical administration:

        1. Administrative Division into Presidencies: Company territories were divided into three major Presidencies — Bengal, Madras, and Bombay — each ruled by a Governor, with ultimate executive authority vested in the Governor-General. In contrast, Indian empires were divided into Subahs, Sarkars, and Parganas under Nawabs, Subahdars, or local Rajas.
        2. Separation of Judicial and Executive Powers: Warren Hastings established a structured dual court system in 18th century — Civil Court (Diwani Adalat) presided over by British Collectors, and Criminal Court (Faujdari Adalat) supervised by Qazis and Muftis under Collector supervision. Indian rulers personally held supreme judicial, executive, and military authority.
        3. Standardization of Laws & Codification: The British codified Hindu laws (by N.B. Halhed in 1775) and Islamic laws (by 1778) to create uniform legal codes enforced by written statutes. Traditional Indian justice relied heavily on customary law, local panchayats, and royal decrees.
        4. Role of the Collector: The British Collector became the supreme district administrator responsible for revenue collection and law enforcement, backed by police and courts. Traditional Indian administration relied on zamindars, jagirdars, and local chieftains.
        📋 CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown:
        Administrative Units (Presidencies vs Subahs)1.5 Marks

        Q3
        Describe the main features of the Permanent Settlement introduced in 1793.
        3 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        Lord Cornwallis introduced the Permanent Settlement in Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa in 1793 with the following key features:

        • Recognition of Rajas as Zamindars: Local rajas and taluqdars were recognized as zamindars responsible for collecting land revenue from peasants.

        Q6
        Why were ryots reluctant to grow indigo under the Ryoti system?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        Peasants (ryots) fiercely resisted indigo cultivation due to exploitative terms enforced by European planters:

        1. Unfair Low Prices: Planters paid extremely low prices for harvested indigo, keeping ryots trapped in perpetual debt cycles.
        2. Soil Degradation: Indigo had deep roots that exhausted soil fertility rapidly, rendering fields unfit for rice cultivation.
        3. Forced Land Usage: Planters insisted that indigo be grown on the most fertile soils where peasants traditionally grew staple food grains like rice.

        Analyze why the 'Blue Rebellion' of 1859 broke out in Bengal and state its ultimate outcome.

        Blue Rebellion (1859): Thousands of Bengal ryots refused to grow indigo, attacked indigo factories, and boycotted planters. The British government formed the Indigo Commission (1860), which declared planters guilty of coercion and allowed ryots to refuse indigo contracts. Consequently, indigo production collapsed in Bengal and shifted to Bihar.

        What were the major changes introduced by the British after the Revolt of 1857?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        Following the suppression of the 1857 Revolt, the British Parliament passed the Government of India Act 1858, introducing major administrative shifts:

        1. Transfer of Power: Governance of India was transferred from East India Company to the British Crown, represented by the Secretary of State for India and Governor-General (renamed Viceroy).
