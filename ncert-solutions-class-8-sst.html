        Q2
        What is the problem with the periodisation of Indian history that James Mill offers?
        3 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        James Mill, a Scottish economist and political philosopher, published a massive three-volume work, A History of British India (1817), in which he divided Indian history into three distinct periods: Hindu, Muslim, and British. The severe problems with this periodisation include:

        • Religious Bias & Over-simplification: Mill categorized entire historical eras solely on the basis of the religion of the ruling monarchs. This ignores the fact that a variety of faiths existed simultaneously across India in all periods.
        • Eurocentric & Colonial Mindset: Mill believed that all Asian societies were at a lower level of civilization than Europe. He falsely portrayed pre-British India as a dark age dominated by religious intolerance, caste taboos, and superstitious practices.
        • Neglect of Socio-Economic Diversity: Subdividing history by ruler religion completely overlooks the vibrant socio-economic transformations, local trade networks, agricultural developments, and ordinary people's lives during these periods.

        Q3
        Why did the British preserve official documents?
        3 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        The British imperial administration placed immense value on written records and documentation. They preserved official records meticulously due to the following reasons:

        • Administrative Continuity & Accountability: The British believed that every policy, instruction, plan, decision, agreement, and investigation had to be clearly written down. This enabled future administrators to study, debate, and track past administrative precedents easily.
        • Creation of Institutional Memory: Preserved documents served as permanent reference materials for administrative queries, legal disputes, land revenue calculations, and border demarcation.
        • Specialized Record Rooms: To safeguard these documents, the British established record rooms attached to all administrative institutions — from Collectorates and Commissionerates to Law Courts and Secretariats. Furthermore, specialized institutions like Archives and Museums were established.

        Q4
        How will the information historians get from old newspapers be different from that found in police reports?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        Historians analyze multiple primary sources to reconstruct an unbiased picture of the past. Information extracted from old newspapers differs significantly from police reports in perspective, purpose, content, and subjectivity:

        1. Perspective & Viewpoint: Police reports are official state records reflecting the perspective of colonial authorities. They tend to portray public protests, peasant rebellions, or worker strikes as unlawful disruptions, riots, or threats to law and order. In contrast, independent newspapers captured public sentiments, nationalist views, and common grievances.
        2. Target Audience & Purpose: Police reports were written for confidential administrative reviews, criminal prosecutions, and internal government surveillance. Newspaper reports were produced for public readership, aimed at shaping public opinion, critiquing government policies, and highlighting social issues.
        3. Scope of Coverage: Police reports strictly documented crimes, revolutionary activities, tax evasions, and law enforcement operations. Old newspapers provided a broader cultural and socio-economic context, including trade updates, artistic movements, public debates, editorials, and regional news.
        4. Objectivity vs. Bias: While police reports frequently minimized administrative failures and exaggerated police achievements, newspaper reporting could be influenced by editor preferences or political censorship under British Press Acts. Thus, historians must cross-examine both sources to extract accurate historical truth.

        Q5
        Can you think of examples of surveys in India today? What can a historian derive from such surveys?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        Modern Survey Examples in India:

        • Government Census & Educational Surveys: The Indian Government conducts the National Decennial Census and surveys like UDISE (Unified District Information System for Education) and NSSO (National Sample Survey Office) to gather empirical data on literacy rates, school enrollments, drop-out rates, and youth demographics.
        • Commercial Market Surveys: Commercial enterprises, such as toy manufacturers or tech companies, utilize consumer research surveys, online feedback forms, focus groups, and digital analytics to evaluate children's preferences, purchasing power, and trending interests.

        What Historians Derive from Modern Surveys:

        1. Demographic Patterns: Future historians can trace population growth rates, gender ratios, urbanization levels, and literacy trends across different Indian states.
        2. Socio-Economic Conditions: Educational surveys reveal regional disparities, government expenditure effectiveness, and educational equity across gender and social classes.
        3. Cultural Trends & Consumer Behavior: Commercial surveys provide insights into popular culture, changing lifestyles, technological adoption, and economic priorities of 21st-century Indian youth.

        Q5
        What were the causes of the Battle of Plassey (1757)?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        The Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757) was a pivotal turning point in Indian history. The major causes included:

        1. Misuse of Trade Privileges (Dastaks): East India Company officials routinely misused duty-free trade passes (dastaks) meant for company trade to carry on illegal private trade without paying local taxes to the Nawab of Bengal.
        2. Fortification of Calcutta without Permission: The Company began fortifying Fort William in Calcutta without the approval of Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah, directly challenging the Nawab's sovereign authority.
        3. Sheltering Government Fugitives: Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah was infuriated when the Company provided asylum to Krishna Das, a political fugitive who had absconded with state funds.
        4. Nawab's Attack on Kasimbazar & Fort William: When the Company refused to stop fortification or pay taxes, Siraj-ud-Daulah captured the English factory at Kasimbazar and occupied Fort William in Calcutta.
        5. Conspiracy with Mir Jafar: Lord Robert Clive hatched a secret conspiracy with Mir Jafar (the Nawab's Commander-in-Chief), promising to make him Nawab of Bengal in exchange for betraying Siraj-ud-Daulah on the battlefield.

        Q6
        Explain the System of Subsidiary Alliance introduced by Lord Wellesley.
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        The Subsidiary Alliance System was an aggressive diplomatic and expansionist tool used by Governor-General Lord Wellesley (1798–1805) to bring Indian princely states under British paramountcy:

        1. Disarmament of Princely Forces: An Indian ruler entering into a Subsidiary Alliance was forced to disband his independent standing army.
        2. Stationing of British Troops: The ruler had to maintain British troops within his territory, ostensibly for protection against foreign invasions and internal rebellions.
        3. Subsidiary Maintenance Payments: The ruler was required to pay fixed annual cash subsidies to the British for maintaining these troops. If a ruler failed to make payments on time, a portion of his territory was ceded to the British as penalty (e.g., Nawab of Awadh lost over half his territory in 1801).
        4. Appointment of a British Resident: A British Resident was posted at the ruler's court, who progressively interfered in internal state administration, appointments, and governance.
        5. Surrender of Foreign Relations: The state could not enter into alliances or wage war with any other power without prior British consent.

        Q7
        In what ways was the administration of the Company different from that of Indian rulers?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        The East India Company introduced a bureaucratic and centralized colonial administrative structure that differed fundamentally from traditional Indian monarchical administration:

        1. Administrative Division into Presidencies: Company territories were divided into three major Presidencies — Bengal, Madras, and Bombay — each ruled by a Governor, with ultimate executive authority vested in the Governor-General. In contrast, Indian empires were divided into Subahs, Sarkars, and Parganas under Nawabs, Subahdars, or local Rajas.
        2. Separation of Judicial and Executive Powers: Warren Hastings established a structured dual court system in 18th century — Civil Court (Diwani Adalat) presided over by British Collectors, and Criminal Court (Faujdari Adalat) supervised by Qazis and Muftis under Collector supervision. Indian rulers personally held supreme judicial, executive, and military authority.
        3. Standardization of Laws & Codification: The British codified Hindu laws (by N.B. Halhed in 1775) and Islamic laws (by 1778) to create uniform legal codes enforced by written statutes. Traditional Indian justice relied heavily on customary law, local panchayats, and royal decrees.
        4. Role of the Collector: The British Collector became the supreme district administrator responsible for revenue collection and law enforcement, backed by police and courts. Traditional Indian administration relied on zamindars, jagirdars, and local chieftains.

        Q3
        Describe the main features of the Permanent Settlement introduced in 1793.
        3 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        Lord Cornwallis introduced the Permanent Settlement in Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa in 1793. It had the following key features:

        1. Zamindars Recognised as Owners: Local rajas, nawabs, and taluqdars were recognised as permanent owners (zamindars) of the land under the British. They were made responsible for collecting land revenue from cultivating peasants and paying a fixed share to the Company.
        2. Revenue Fixed in Perpetuity: The revenue amount to be paid by each zamindar to the East India Company was fixed permanently and could never be increased in the future, regardless of how productive the land became. This guaranteed the Company a steady and predictable revenue income.
        3. Sunset Law — Timely Payment or Land Loss: If a zamindar failed to pay revenue by the sunset of a fixed date, his land rights could be auctioned off to a new zamindar without any appeal.
        4. Peasants Reduced to Tenants: Peasants lost their customary hereditary rights over land and were reduced to the status of mere tenants on their ancestral fields, vulnerable to eviction and rack-renting by zamindars. No security of tenure was guaranteed.
        5. Revenue Farming Replaced: The old revenue-farming system was abolished, and zamindars became permanent, hereditary revenue collectors, encouraged to invest in land improvement to maximise agricultural productivity.

        Consequence: While the Company got guaranteed revenue, many zamindars who could not modernise agriculture lost their lands. The peasants bore the harshest burden, as zamindars extracted maximum rent to pay the Company's fixed demand.

        Q5
        Give two problems which arose with the new Munro (Ryotwari) system of fixing revenue.
        3 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        The Ryotwari System introduced by Thomas Munro in Madras Presidency (and by Elphinstone in Bombay) created two major problems:

        1. Excessively High Revenue Demands: Revenue officials conducted biased surveys of soil quality and fixed unrealistically high revenue rates — sometimes 50–100% of the estimated produce. Officials were pressured to maximise Company collections. When rains failed and crops were poor, peasants still had to pay the same high revenue. Unable to pay, thousands of peasants abandoned their villages, fled to jungles, or surrendered to moneylenders. Entire tracts of land were left uncultivated due to mass exodus of farmers.
        2. Coercive Revenue Collection Practices: When peasants failed to pay, colonial revenue officials and their subordinates used harsh methods — public humiliation, physical torture (standing in the sun), cattle seizure, and crop confiscation — to extract dues. Moneylenders (sahukars) lent money at interest rates of 25–50%, and when peasants defaulted, they seized their land, bullocks, and household goods. This created a cycle of permanent peasant indebtedness that worsened across generations.

        Overall Impact: Both these problems triggered periodic agrarian crises, including famines and peasant revolts (e.g., the Deccan Riots of 1875), exposing the exploitative character of British revenue extraction under the Ryotwari system.

        Q6
        Why were ryots reluctant to grow indigo under the Ryoti system?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        Ryots (peasants) were deeply reluctant to grow indigo under the Ryoti system for the following compelling reasons:

        1. Unfair, Artificially Low Prices: European planters compelled ryots to sign satta (contracts) in advance — before the sowing season — agreeing to grow indigo at prices fixed by the planters. The advance price paid was deliberately kept at a fraction of the actual market value of indigo. After receiving the advance, ryots were legally bound by the contract, leaving them with no choice but to sell at ruinous prices regardless of market fluctuations.
        2. Soil Exhaustion — Loss of Fertility: Indigo is a deep-rooted plant that rapidly drains soil of its nutrients. After an indigo harvest, the land remained infertile for several subsequent seasons and required expensive rehabilitation. Rice, which was the primary staple crop, could no longer be grown on indigo-exhausted fields — threatening food security directly.
        3. Occupation of Best Fertile Fields: Planters insisted — often by force — that indigo be cultivated on the most productive, well-irrigated, riverine fields, which peasants needed for growing paddy (rice) to feed their families. Peasants were left with inferior, stony, or flood-prone land for food crops.
        4. No Debt Relief: The money advance given by planters was deliberately small. Yet it created a legal debt bondage — planters could sue defaulting ryots in court under the British legal system. Generation after generation of ryot families remained trapped in indigo debt cycles, unable to refuse cultivation for fear of legal action and police harassment.
        5. Violence and Intimidation: Planters employed lathial (armed strongmen) who intimidated, beat, and threatened ryots who refused indigo cultivation or tried to grow food crops instead.

        Result: These exploitative conditions triggered the famous Blue Rebellion (Nil Bidroha) of 1859–60 in Bengal, where thousands of ryots simultaneously refused to grow indigo.

        Q4
        What was Birsa's vision of a golden age? Why do you think such a vision appealed to the people of the region?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        Birsa Munda envisioned the establishment of a Satyug (Golden Age / Age of Truth) — a new world order where the Munda community would reclaim its dignity, land, and self-governance:

        1. Munda Raj — Self-Rule: Birsa called for the expulsion of dikus (British officials, moneylenders, traders) and the establishment of a Munda Kingdom (Munda Raj) governed by their own laws and traditions. He believed the Mundas would recover all their ancestral lands seized by outsiders.
        2. Restoration of Ancestral Land Rights: The Munda community had historically practiced khuntkatti — joint family land ownership — but colonial land settlements transferred these communal rights to zamindars and contractors. Birsa's Golden Age envisioned the reversal of this dispossession and the return of forested ancestral lands to the Munda people.
        3. Socio-Religious Purification: Birsa declared himself a divine messenger (Dharti Abba — Father of the Earth). He called on Mundas to give up alcohol, abandon witchcraft beliefs, and maintain internal purity. He synthesised elements of Christianity and tribal religion into a new faith centered on one God.
        4. Harmony with Nature: The envisioned Golden Age included planting trees, building embankments, and living an honest, hard-working agricultural life in harmony with forests — the tribal ancestral home.

        Why This Vision Appealed to People:

        • Tribals had suffered catastrophic land dispossession, forced begar (unpaid labour), and brutal forest restrictions under British rule.
        • Birsa's message offered spiritual hope, restored cultural pride, and a practical political programme — making it deeply resonant among Mundas suffering grinding poverty.
        • His charismatic leadership and claims of divine power attracted thousands who believed he could deliver miraculous relief.

        Q5
        How did the British rule affect tribal chiefs?
        3 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        Before British rule, tribal chiefs enjoyed administrative autonomy and police powers. Under British rule:

        Q1
        Answer the following questions briefly:
        (i) Why are resources distributed unequally over the earth?
        (ii) What is resource conservation?
        (iii) Why are human resources important?
        (iv) What is sustainable development?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        (i) Why are resources distributed unequally over the earth?

        Resources are distributed unequally because their formation depends on complex physical and geological factors that vary enormously across the globe:

        • Terrain and Geology: Mineral resources (coal, iron ore, petroleum) exist only where specific geological conditions occurred millions of years ago. For example, petroleum is found mainly in sedimentary rock basins of West Asia, while coal is concentrated in former swampy forests of Europe and India.
        • Climate: Tropical rainforests provide timber and biodiversity, while deserts and polar regions are resource-poor. Monsoon regions support rich agricultural resources.
        • Altitude: High mountainous regions may have valuable minerals but harsh terrain limits agriculture and settlement.

        (ii) What is Resource Conservation?

        Resource conservation means using natural resources carefully, judiciously, and at a rate that allows natural regeneration — so that future generations inherit a resource-rich planet. It includes reducing waste, recycling materials, and switching to renewable alternatives.

        (iii) Why are Human Resources Important?

        Human beings possess unique knowledge, technical skills, creativity, and organisational ability that convert raw natural materials into useful products and services. Without human intelligence and innovation, natural resources like coal, iron, and petroleum would remain unexploited. Educated and skilled people are therefore the most productive resource a country can develop.

        (iv) What is Sustainable Development?

        Sustainable development means development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland Commission, 1987). It balances economic growth, environmental protection, and social equity — ensuring that resource exploitation today does not create resource scarcity tomorrow.

        Q1
        Why does a democratic country need a Constitution?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        A democratic country requires a Constitution for the following fundamental reasons:

        1. Establishes Foundational Rules and Values: A Constitution sets out the basic rules upon which all other laws and government decisions are based. It defines the nature, structure, and objectives of the state — ensuring that future governments cannot arbitrarily change the fundamental character of the country.
        2. Prevents Tyranny of the Majority: In a democracy, elected majorities could potentially use their power to oppress minorities (religious, linguistic, ethnic). A Constitution places enforceable limits on what even a popularly elected majority government can do. For example, the Indian Constitution protects minority rights through Fundamental Rights that Parliament cannot simply vote away.
        3. Protects Citizens from Arbitrary State Power: Without constitutional safeguards, government officials could arrest, imprison, or punish citizens without due process. The Constitution guarantees rights like Right to Life and Personal Liberty (Article 21), Right to be Heard, and Protection Against Self-Incrimination.
        4. Defines Separation of Powers: The Constitution distributes power among the Legislature (makes laws), Executive (implements laws), and Judiciary (interprets laws and resolves disputes) — preventing dangerous concentration of power in one branch.
        5. Guarantees Fundamental Rights: It enshrines non-negotiable rights (Right to Equality, Freedom of Speech, Protection from Exploitation, Right to Education) that no government can take away through ordinary legislation.
        6. Enables Peaceful Transfer of Power: By defining elections, terms of office, and transition procedures, the Constitution ensures democratic governance is renewed peacefully without coups or violence.

        What are the key features of the Indian Constitution?
        5 Marks

        The Indian Constitution embodies several distinctive key features:

        Q2
        Why should the Government be responsible for providing public facilities?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        The Government must be primarily responsible for providing public facilities for the following compelling reasons:

        1. Market Failure — Private Profit Motive: Private companies exist to maximise profit for shareholders. Essential services like safe drinking water, electricity, sanitation, and healthcare — if left entirely to private market forces — would be priced far beyond the reach of the poor majority. A private water company in Chennai charges Rs 500–1000 per tanker, while a slum family cannot afford even Rs 50.
        2. Universal Access as a Right — Article 21: The Indian Constitution guarantees the Right to Life (Article 21), which the Supreme Court has interpreted to include the right to safe drinking water, education, and a clean environment. If these are treated as commercial products available only to those who can pay, constitutional rights become meaningless for the poor.
        3. Non-Excludability and Non-Rivalry — Public Goods: Many public facilities (clean air, flood protection, street lighting) are public goods — no one can be excluded from using them, and one person's use does not reduce availability for others. Private firms cannot profitably provide such goods, making government provision essential.
        4. Economies of Scale: Governments can build large-scale water treatment plants, power grids, and road networks at far lower per-unit costs than multiple small private providers. This efficiency benefits all citizens through lower service costs.
        5. Social Equity and Inclusive Development: Without government-provided public facilities, inequality deepens — the rich access quality private services while the poor go without. Government provision ensures all citizens — regardless of income, caste, or location — receive minimum essential services, promoting social justice.

        Civics Chapter 8: Law and Social Justice

        NCERT Solutions for Class 8 Civics Chapter 8 - Law and Social Justice

        Q1
        Why do we need laws to protect workers, consumers, and producers?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        Laws are essential to protect the interests of workers, consumers, and producers in a market economy for the following reasons:

        1. Preventing Exploitation of Workers: In an unregulated market, employers — especially large corporations — have vastly more power than individual workers. Without laws, employers could pay starvation wages, impose dangerous 14–16 hour workdays, employ children, deny maternity leave, and dismiss workers arbitrarily. Laws like the Minimum Wages Act, Factories Act, Child Labour (Prohibition) Act, and Maternity Benefit Act create a legal floor of protections.
        2. Protecting Consumer Health and Safety: Without regulations, producers could adulterate food, sell unsafe medicines, provide deceptive product information, and deny refunds. The Consumer Protection Act (2019), Bureau of Indian Standards, and FSSAI (Food Safety and Standards Authority of India) set mandatory quality standards and give consumers legal recourse against fraud.
        3. Environmental Protection: Without laws, industries would discharge toxic waste into rivers, emit pollutants into the air, and deplete natural resources for short-term profit — destroying public health and ecological systems. Environmental laws (Environment Protection Act 1986, Water Pollution Act, Forest Conservation Act) make polluters legally liable.
        4. Ensuring Fair Competition: Laws prevent monopolistic practices and cartel arrangements where powerful producers could eliminate competition and dictate inflated prices. The Competition Commission of India regulates unfair trade practices.
        5. Building Market Trust: When consumers trust that products meet standards, workers know their rights, and businesses follow transparent rules, markets function more efficiently — enabling sustainable economic growth that benefits all.

        Q3
        What were the major enforcement failures in the Bhopal Gas Tragedy?
        5 Marks
        💡 Complete Answer (CBSE Marking Scheme Aligned):

        The Bhopal Gas Tragedy (December 2–3, 1984) — the world's worst industrial disaster — revealed catastrophic failures in safety enforcement at multiple levels:

        1. Plant Safety Equipment Disabled to Save Costs: Union Carbide Corporation's Bhopal plant had allowed several critical safety systems to fall into disrepair or be deliberately shut off — including the refrigeration unit (that would have kept MIC gas cool and stable), the gas scrubber (that should have neutralised leaking gas with caustic soda), and the flare tower (designed to burn off escaping gas). These were turned off to cut operational expenses.
        2. Government Inspection Failure: State and central government inspectors had repeatedly visited the plant but failed to enforce safety standards. The plant had been built in a heavily populated residential area of Bhopal in violation of zoning norms. Inspectors were either under-trained or compromised — no serious corrective action was ever taken despite known safety violations.
        3. Inadequate Compensation to Victims: Over 500,000 people were exposed to the toxic methyl isocyanate (MIC) gas. Approximately 15,000–20,000 people eventually died and hundreds of thousands were permanently disabled with lung damage, blindness, and neurological disorders. Yet the total compensation paid by Union Carbide was a mere USD 470 million — a fraction of what US courts would have awarded to American victims under similar circumstances. The inequality of compensation based on nationality was deeply unjust.
        4. Regulatory Double Standards: Safety regulations enforced in Union Carbide's US plants were far stricter than those maintained in Bhopal. The 'race to the bottom' in safety standards by multinational corporations operating in developing countries was exposed as systematic exploitation.
        5. Rehabilitation Failure: Even decades later, toxic chemical waste at the Bhopal plant site has not been fully cleaned up, continuing to contaminate groundwater and affecting subsequent generations.
