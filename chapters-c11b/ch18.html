Question 1
NCERT Exercise
Briefly describe the structure of the following:
(a) Brain
(b) Eye
(c) Ear
[6 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
(a) Structure of the Human Brain:
Protected inside the cranial cavity and surrounded by three cranial meninges: outer dura mater, delicate middle arachnoid, and highly vascular inner pia mater. Divided into three major anatomical parts:
  • 1. Forebrain (Prosencephalon): Comprises cerebrum, thalamus, and hypothalamus. The cerebrum forms the bulk of the brain, divided into right and left cerebral hemispheres interconnected by a thick nerve tract called the corpus callosum. The cerebral cortex (grey matter) houses motor, sensory, and association areas. The thalamus acts as a major sensory relay station. The hypothalamus at its base regulates body temperature, hunger, thirst, circadian rhythms, and pituitary hormone synthesis.
  • 2. Midbrain (Mesencephalon): Located between thalamus and pons. Contains the cerebral aqueduct (canal of Sylvius) and dorsal four optic/auditory lobes called corpora quadrigemina.
  • 3. Hindbrain (Rhombencephalon): Comprises pons (interconnecting tracts), cerebellum (convoluted surface coordinating muscular posture and balance), and medulla oblongata (vital reflex center controlling respiration, cardiovascular reflexes, and gastric secretions). Medulla connects inferiorly with the spinal cord.
(b) Structure of the Human Eye:
Spherical organ located in the bony orbit of the skull. Its wall consists of three concentric tunics:
  • 1. Fibrous Tunic (Outer): Posterior opaque white sclera and anterior transparent, avascular cornea which admits and refracts light rays.
  • 2. Vascular Tunic / Uvea (Middle): Highly vascular, bluish choroid layer. Anteriorly thickens into the ciliary body (contains ciliary muscles controlling lens curvature) and projects forward as the pigmented, visible iris with a central variable aperture called the pupil. The crystalline biconvex transparent lens is held in place by suspensory ligaments.
  • 3. Neural Tunic / Retina (Inner): Contains three neural cell layers: photoreceptors (outer), bipolar cells (middle), and ganglion cells (inner). Photoreceptors include rods (rhodopsin pigment, twilight/scotopic vision) and cones (photopic bright-light & color vision). Lateral to the optic disc (blind spot) lies the macula lutea with its central depression, the fovea centralis (site of highest visual resolution, containing exclusively packed cones).
(c) Structure of the Human Ear:
Performs dual sensory modalities: hearing (phonoreception) and equilibrium (statoreception). Composed of three divisions:
  • 1. External Ear: Consists of pinna (collects sound waves), external auditory canal (lined with wax/ceruminous glands), and the vibrating tympanic membrane (eardrum).
  • 2. Middle Ear (Tympanic Cavity): Air-filled chamber housing three articulated ear ossicles: malleus (hammer) attached to eardrum, incus (anvil), and stapes (stirrup) attached to the oval window (fenestra ovalis). Connected to the pharynx via the Eustachian tube to equalize pressure.
  • 3. Inner Ear (Labyrinth): Consists of bony labyrinth enclosing fluid-filled membranous labyrinth (endolymph inside, perilymph outside). Formed of the cochlea (hearing organ housing the basilar membrane with the Organ of Corti containing sensory hair cells) and the vestibular apparatus (3 semicircular canals and otolith organ — utricle & saccule — maintaining dynamic and static equilibrium).

Question 3
NCERT Exercise
Explain the following processes:
(a) Polarisation of the membrane of a nerve fibre
(b) Depolarisation of the membrane of a nerve fibre
(c) Conduction of a nerve impulse along an axon
(d) Transmission of a nerve impulse across a chemical synapse
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
(a) Polarisation of the Nerve Membrane (Resting Potential):
In an unstimulated resting neuron, the inside of the axonal membrane is negatively charged relative to the outside. This resting membrane potential (approximately $-70\text{ mV}$) is generated and maintained by:
  • Differential permeability: the resting membrane is significantly more permeable to $K^+$ ions than to $Na^+$ ions and completely impermeable to large negatively charged intracellular proteins.
  • The active $Na^+/K^+$ ATPase pump, which consumes 1 ATP to transport $3\text{ Na}^+$ outward into the ECF in exchange for bringing $2\text{ K}^+$ inward into the axoplasm, creating an electrochemical polarity.
(b) Depolarisation of the Nerve Membrane (Action Potential Generation):
When a stimulus reaching threshold voltage ($-55\text{ mV}$) is applied at a point on the polarized axonal membrane:
  • Voltage-gated $Na^+$ channels immediately open, causing a massive influx of $Na^+$ into the axoplasm down its steep electrical and concentration gradient.
  • This influx rapidly overwhelms the negative charge, reversing the membrane polarity to approximately $+30\text{ mV}$ (inside becomes positive, outside negative). This transient electrical reversal is termed depolarisation or the action potential.
(c) Conduction of a Nerve Impulse Along an Axon:
At the depolarised site (site A), the inner surface is positive while at the adjacent resting site (site B), the inner surface is negative:
  • A local electrical circuit forms: current flows on the inner axonal surface from site A to site B, and on the outer surface from site B to site A.
  • This current flow depolarises site B to its threshold, triggering an action potential at site B. Meanwhile, site A repolarises as voltage-gated $K^+$ channels open and $K^+$ rushes outward.
  • This self-propagating wave of sequential depolarisation travels smoothly along unmyelinated axons, or rapidly jumps from node to node (saltatory conduction) in myelinated axons.
(d) Transmission of a Nerve Impulse Across a Chemical Synapse:
  1. When the action potential arrives at the pre-synaptic axon terminal bulb, it opens voltage-gated $Ca^{2+}$ channels, causing an influx of $Ca^{2+}$ ions from the synaptic cleft into the bulb.
  2. The $Ca^{2+}$ influx stimulates synaptic vesicles containing neurotransmitters (such as acetylcholine) to migrate and fuse with the pre-synaptic membrane.
  3. Neurotransmitters are released into the narrow fluid-filled synaptic cleft (~20 nm) via exocytosis.
  4. Neurotransmitter molecules diffuse across the cleft and bind to specific ligand-gated receptors on the post-synaptic membrane, opening ion channels that generate an Excitatory Post-Synaptic Potential (EPSP) or action potential in the post-synaptic neuron.

Question 5
NCERT Exercise
Write short notes on the following:
(a) Neural coordination
(b) Forebrain
(c) Midbrain
(d) Hindbrain
(e) Retina
(f) Ear ossicles
(g) Cochlea
(h) Organ of Corti
(i) Synapse
[9 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Concise Scientific Explanations of Key Neurobiological Concepts:

  • (a) Neural Coordination: The physiological process through which the nervous system integrates and synchronizes the activities of multiple interdependent organs via rapid, point-to-point electrical nerve impulses, maintaining homeostatic equilibrium.
  • (b) Forebrain: Composed of the cerebrum, thalamus, and hypothalamus. Center for conscious thought, memory, voluntary muscle actions, speech, thermoregulation, and endocrine regulation.
  • (c) Midbrain: Located between the diencephalon and pons. Houses the cerebral aqueduct and four dorsal rounded optic/auditory reflex swelling lobes termed the corpora quadrigemina.
  • (d) Hindbrain: Composed of the pons (fibre bridges), cerebellum (intricate convoluted tree of life / arbor vitae coordinating posture and fine motor skills), and medulla oblongata (vital involuntary control of cardiac rhythm, breathing rate, and swallowing).
  • (e) Retina: Innermost photosensitive neural layer of the eyeball containing three layers of cells: outer photoreceptors (rods with rhodopsin for dim light; cones with photopsin for sharp color vision), middle bipolar cells, and inner ganglion cells forming the optic nerve.
  • (f) Ear Ossicles: Three miniature articulating bones in the middle ear — Malleus (hammer), Incus (anvil), and Stapes (stirrup) — that mechanically amplify sound wave vibrations by approximately 20 times and transmit them from the eardrum to the oval window.
  • (g) Cochlea: The coiled, snail-shell-shaped auditory portion of the inner ear labyrinth, divided into three longitudinal chambers: upper scala vestibuli, middle scala media (containing endolymph), and lower scala tympani.
  • (h) Organ of Corti: The sensory receptor organ of hearing resting on the basilar membrane within the scala media of the cochlea, bearing rows of sensory hair cells whose apical stereocilia bend against the overlying tectorial membrane to generate auditory action potentials.
  • (i) Synapse: The specialized anatomical junction between the terminal bulb of a pre-synaptic neuron and the dendrite/membrane of a post-synaptic neuron, through which electrical signals are transmitted via chemical neurotransmitters or direct gap junctions.

Question 6
NCERT Exercise
Give a brief account of the mechanism of synaptic transmission.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Mechanism of Synaptic Transmission Across a Chemical Synapse:

  1. Arrival of Action Potential: When a wave of depolarization (action potential) reaches the pre-synaptic axon terminal bulb, it depolarises the pre-synaptic membrane.
  2. Calcium Influx: The depolarisation triggers the opening of voltage-gated $Ca^{2+}$ channels. Extracellular calcium ions ($Ca^{2+}$) rush into the pre-synaptic knob down their steep gradient.
  3. Exocytosis of Neurotransmitter: The elevated intracellular $Ca^{2+}$ induces synaptic vesicles containing acetylcholine (ACh) to move toward, fuse with the pre-synaptic membrane, and release neurotransmitter molecules into the fluid-filled synaptic cleft (~20 nm) via exocytosis.
  4. Binding to Post-Synaptic Receptors: The neurotransmitter diffuses across the narrow cleft and binds specifically to ligand-gated chemoreceptors on the post-synaptic membrane.
  5. Post-Synaptic Potential Generation: Receptor binding opens ion channels, allowing an influx of $Na^+$ ions, which depolarises the post-synaptic membrane and generates a fresh action potential.
  6. Termination: Acetylcholine is rapidly hydrolyzed by the enzyme acetylcholinesterase (AChE) into acetate and choline, resetting the receptor for subsequent signals.

Question 7
NCERT Exercise
Explain the role of $Na^+$ in the generation of action potential.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Critical Role of Sodium Ions ($Na^+$) in Action Potential Generation:

  • 1. Resting State Exclusion: At resting potential ($-70\text{ mV}$), the axonal membrane is nearly impermeable to $Na^+$, and $Na^+/K^+$ pumps actively keep $[Na^+]$ tenfold higher outside the axon than inside.
  • 2. Threshold Stimulus: When a stimulus depolarises the membrane to the threshold potential ($-55\text{ mV}$), voltage-gated $Na^+$ activation gates snap open.
  • 3. Explosive $Na^+$ Influx: Driven by both a concentration gradient (high outside to low inside) and an electrical gradient (attracted to negative interior), positively charged $Na^+$ ions rush into the axoplasm in a rapid surge.
  • 4. Polarity Reversal (Depolarisation): This influx cancels the internal negative charge and drives the membrane potential up to approximately $+30\text{ mV}$. This sudden spike represents the action potential.
  • 5. Channel Inactivation: Within a millisecond, the inactivation gates of $Na^+$ channels close, stopping further sodium entry and allowing $K^+$ efflux to repolarize the membrane.

Question 8
NCERT Exercise
Differentiate between:
(a) Myelinated and non-myelinated axons
(b) Dendrites and axons
(c) Thalamus and Hypothalamus
(d) Cerebrum and Cerebellum
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Comparative Analysis of Nervous System Structures:

(a) Myelinated vs. Non-Myelinated Axons:
• Myelinated Axons: Ensheathed with lipid-rich myelin sheath produced by Schwann cells (PNS) or oligodendrocytes (CNS), interrupted at periodic gaps called Nodes of Ranvier. Exhibits ultra-fast saltatory conduction (up to 120 m/s); found in cranial and spinal nerves.
• Non-Myelinated Axons: Enclosed by Schwann cells that do not form a multi-layered myelin wrapping; lack Nodes of Ranvier. Exhibits slow, continuous wave conduction (1–2 m/s); common in autonomic and somatic neural systems.

(b) Dendrites vs. Axons:
• Dendrites: Short, highly branched tapering cytoplasmic processes containing Nissl's granules; conduct impulses centripetally (towards the soma).
• Axon: Single, long, cylindrical process lacking Nissl's granules; originates at the axon hillock and conducts impulses centrifugally (away from the soma) to terminal synaptic knobs.

(c) Thalamus vs. Hypothalamus:
• Thalamus: Large egg-shaped sensory/motor relay station wrapped by the cerebrum that filters and channels all sensory inputs (except olfaction) to appropriate cortical areas.
• Hypothalamus: Located at the base of the thalamus; contains autonomic centers for body temperature regulation, hunger, thirst, circadian clock, and synthesizes neurohormones controlling pituitary secretion.

(d) Cerebrum vs. Cerebellum:
• Cerebrum: Largest part of the forebrain; center of memory, conscious thought, sensory interpretation, reasoning, and voluntary motor output.
• Cerebellum: Part of the hindbrain; features a highly folded convoluted surface (arbor vitae) that unconsciously coordinates voluntary muscle movements, maintaining posture, balance, and equilibrium.

Question 10
NCERT Exercise
Define the following:
(a) Synapse
(b) Resting membrane potential
(c) Action potential
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Standard Neurobiological Definitions:

  • (a) Synapse: An anatomically specialized functional junction between an axon terminal of one neuron and the dendrite, soma, or effector cell of another, separated by a narrow microscopic gap (synaptic cleft) across which nerve impulses are transmitted via chemical neurotransmitters or electrical gap junctions.
  • (b) Resting Membrane Potential (RMP): The steady electrical potential difference across the plasma membrane of an unexcited, non-conducting neuron (typically $-70\text{ mV}$), wherein the inside of the cell is negatively charged relative to the outside due to unequal distribution of $Na^+$ and $K^+$ ions maintained by $Na^+/K^+$ ATPase pumps.
  • (c) Action Potential (Nerve Impulse): A rapid, all-or-none electrical wave of depolarisation and repolarisation (reversing membrane potential from $-70\text{ mV}$ to $+30\text{ mV}$ and back) that travels autonomously along an excitable plasma membrane in response to a threshold stimulus.

Question 11
NCERT Exercise
Give a brief account of the functions of the following:
(a) Corpus callosum
(b) Hypothalamus
(c) Amygdala
(d) Semicircular canals
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Key Physiological Functions:

  • (a) Corpus Callosum: A wide, arched tract of commissural myelinated nerve fibers connecting the left and right cerebral hemispheres. It enables bidirectional inter-hemispheric communication, data sharing, cognitive synchronization, and memory integration between the two halves of the brain.
  • (b) Hypothalamus: Master regulator of the autonomic nervous system and neuroendocrine axis:
    • Controls autonomic centers for heart rate, blood pressure, thermoregulation, hunger, thirst, and satiety.
    • Synthesizes releasing and inhibiting hormones that control the anterior pituitary, as well as oxytocin and vasopressin.
    • Part of the limbic system controlling emotional responses and sexual drive.
  • (c) Amygdala: An almond-shaped nucleus within the temporal lobe, forming an integral part of the limbic system. It processes fear, aggression, emotional memory, and threat detection, coordinating defensive autonomic behavioral responses.
  • (d) Semicircular Canals: Three fluid-filled bony/membranous rings oriented at mutual right angles in the inner ear. The sensory crista ampullaris in their dilated ampullae detects angular acceleration and rotational head movement, maintaining dynamic bodily equilibrium and gaze stabilization.

Question 12
NCERT Exercise
Distinguish between:
(a) Afferent neurons and efferent neurons
(b) Impulse conduction in a myelinated nerve fibre and unmyelinated nerve fibre
(c) Aqueous humor and vitreous humor
(d) Blind spot and yellow spot (macula lutea)
(e) Cranial nerves and spinal nerves
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Key Physiological and Anatomical Distinctions:

  • (a) Afferent Neurons vs. Efferent Neurons:
    • Afferent (Sensory) Neurons: Transmit sensory impulses from peripheral sensory receptors towards the CNS.
    • Efferent (Motor) Neurons: Transmit regulatory motor commands away from the CNS to peripheral effector organs (muscles and glands).
  • (b) Conduction in Myelinated vs. Unmyelinated Nerve Fibre:
    • Myelinated Fibre: Impulse jumps from one Node of Ranvier to the next (saltatory conduction), yielding extremely high conduction velocity (~100–120 m/s) with low ATP expenditure.
    • Unmyelinated Fibre: Impulse travels continuously down the entire axonal length (continuous propagation), yielding slower velocity (~1–2 m/s) with higher metabolic energy cost.
  • (c) Aqueous Humor vs. Vitreous Humor:
    • Aqueous Humor: Thin, clear, watery fluid filling the anterior chamber between cornea and lens, continually produced by ciliary processes and drained into Canal of Schlemm to nourish avascular lens and cornea.
    • Vitreous Humor: Transparent, gelatinous, non-renewing jelly filling the posterior cavity between lens and retina, providing intraocular pressure to keep retina flat against choroid.
  • (d) Blind Spot vs. Yellow Spot (Macula Lutea / Fovea):
    • Blind Spot (Optic Disc): Region medial to the posterior pole where the optic nerve exits and central retinal vessels enter; completely devoid of photoreceptors (zero visual perception).
    • Yellow Spot (Fovea Centralis): Thinned-out pit on the optical axis lateral to the blind spot, containing only densely packed cones with 1:1 ganglion synapses; site of maximum visual acuity and sharpest color resolution.
  • (e) Cranial Nerves vs. Spinal Nerves:
    • Cranial Nerves: 12 pairs of peripheral nerves originating directly from the brain/brainstem, innervating predominantly head, neck, and thoracic/abdominal viscera (vagus nerve).
    • Spinal Nerves: 31 pairs of mixed peripheral nerves arising segmentally from the spinal cord, innervating the trunk and upper/lower limbs.
