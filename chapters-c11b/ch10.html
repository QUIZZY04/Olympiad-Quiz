Question 1
NCERT Exercise
What is the average cell cycle span for a mammalian cell?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Average Cell Cycle Duration:
The average cell cycle duration for a typical proliferating human/mammalian somatic cell in culture is approximately 24 hours.

Temporal Distribution Across Phases:
  • Interphase (>95% of cycle): Lasts for roughly 23 hours:
    • $G_1$ Phase (Gap 1): ~8–10 hours
    • $S$ Phase (Synthesis): ~6–8 hours
    • $G_2$ Phase (Gap 2): ~4–5 hours
  • M-Phase (Mitosis + Cytokinesis): The actual dramatic division phase lasts for only about 1 hour (<5% of total cell cycle duration).
Comparative Note: Yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) completes a cell cycle in just 90 minutes.

Question 2
NCERT Exercise
Distinguish cytokinesis from karyokinesis.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Differences Between Karyokinesis and Cytokinesis:

Parameter Karyokinesis Cytokinesis
1. Definition Division of the cell's nucleus into two genetically identical daughter nuclei. Division of the cell's cytoplasm, partitioning organelles into two separate daughter cells.
2. Timing in Cell Cycle Precedes cytokinesis (occurs first during M-phase). Follows karyokinesis (initiates during late anaphase / telophase).
3. Sequential Stages Divided into four sequential stages: Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, and Telophase. A continuous single mechanical event; no sub-phases.
4. Mechanism Involves microtubular spindle apparatus, condensation of chromatin, and sister chromatid segregation. Occurs via cleavage furrow (centripetal in animal cells) or cell plate formation via phragmoplast (centrifugal in plant cells).
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Definition: nuclear division (karyokinesis) vs cytoplasmic division (cytokinesis)1 Mark
Order of occurrence and karyokinesis sub-phases (P, M, A, T)1 Mark
Mechanism: spindle-mediated chromatid separation vs cleavage furrow / cell plate1 Mark
Question 3
NCERT Exercise
Describe the events taking place during interphase.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Key Biochemical & Morphological Events of Interphase ("Resting / Preparation Phase"):
Interphase is a period of intense metabolic activity during which the cell grows, replicates its genome, and prepares for division. It is subdivided into three successive phases:

1. $G_1$ Phase (Gap 1 / Post-Mitotic Growth Phase):
  • The cell is metabolically exceptionally active and continuously grows in size.
  • Intense synthesis of RNA, structural proteins, and enzymes required for DNA replication (DNA polymerases, ligases, tubulin).
  • Replication of cellular organelles (mitochondria, ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum).
  • DNA content remains constant at 2C; chromosome number remains 2n.
2. $S$ Phase (Synthesis Phase):
  • DNA Replication: The quantity of nuclear DNA is accurately doubled from 2C to 4C per cell ($2C \rightarrow 4C$).
  • Critical Chromosome Rule: Chromosome number is not changed; it remains strictly diploid ($2n$). Each chromosome now consists of two identical sister chromatids joined at a single centromere.
  • In animal cells, the centrosome (centriole pair) duplicates in the cytoplasm.
3. $G_2$ Phase (Gap 2 / Pre-Mitotic Phase):
  • Proteins essential for the assembly of the mitotic spindle apparatus (such as tubulin) are actively synthesized.
  • Cell continues to enlarge, synthesize ATP reserves, and checks for DNA replication errors (G2/M DNA damage checkpoint) before entering mitosis.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
G1 phase: intense metabolic growth, RNA/protein synthesis, organelle duplication1.5 Marks
S phase: DNA doubling from 2C to 4C without changing chromosome number (2n) & centriole duplication1.5 Marks
G2 phase: tubulin spindle protein synthesis, ATP accumulation, and mitotic readiness1 Mark
Question 4
NCERT Exercise
What is $G_0$ (quiescent phase) of cell cycle?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Scientific Concept of $G_0$ (Quiescent / Resting) Phase:

1. Definition:
The $G_0$ phase is an inactive, quiescent stage of the cell cycle where cells exit the active proliferative cell cycle at the $G_1$ checkpoint and suspend division, without undergoing cell death.

2. Metabolic and Proliferative Characteristics:
  • Cells in $G_0$ remain metabolically active and functional, performing their differentiated physiological duties, but they no longer proliferate or replicate DNA unless specifically stimulated.
  • They can re-enter the $G_1$ phase and resume active cell division upon receiving appropriate mitogenic signals (growth factors, tissue injury).
3. Examples:
  • Permanent $G_0$: Fully differentiated mature human heart muscle cells (cardiomyocytes) and neurons, which never divide in adult life.
  • Facultative / Conditional $G_0$: Hepatocytes (liver cells) and fibroblasts remain in $G_0$ under normal conditions, but swiftly enter $G_1$ and proliferate to regenerate tissue following surgical resection or injury.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Definition of G0 as exit from G1 into quiescent non-dividing state1 Mark
Metabolic status: cells remain fully active and differentiated but cease proliferation1 Mark
Examples: Permanent (neurons, cardiac cells) vs Reversible (liver cells upon injury)1 Mark
Question 5
NCERT Exercise
Why is mitosis called equational division?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Reason Mitosis is Designated as Equational Division:

Mitosis is termed equational division because the number of chromosomes, their genetic composition, and their ploidy in the resulting daughter cells remain strictly equal and identical to that of the parent cell:

  • If a diploid parent cell has 46 chromosomes ($2n = 46$), each of the two daughter cells produced following mitotic division also receives exactly 46 chromosomes ($2n = 46$).
  • Mechanism of Fidelity:
    1. During the 'S' phase of interphase, the DNA content of each chromosome is duplicated (forming two genetically identical sister chromatids attached at a single centromere).
    2. During anaphase of mitosis, the centromere of each chromosome splits longitudinally, and the two sister chromatids are pulled apart to opposite poles as independent daughter chromosomes.
    3. Consequently, each daughter nucleus receives an identical, complete set of genetic instructions without any numerical reduction or alteration of alleles (unlike meiosis where chromosome number is halved from $2n$ to $n$).
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Clear statement: chromosome number in daughter cells remains identical to parent cell (2n to 2n)1 Mark
Mechanism: S-phase replication followed by equational centromere splitting in anaphase1 Mark
Question 6
NCERT Exercise
Name the stage of cell cycle at which one of the following events occur:
(i) Chromosomes are moved to spindle equator.
(ii) Centromere splits and chromatids separate.
(iii) Pairing between homologous chromosomes takes place.
(iv) Crossing over between homologous chromosomes takes place.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Accurate Identification of Stages:

Event Exact Stage of Cell Division Biochemical / Cellular Basis
(i) Chromosomes moved to spindle equator Metaphase (of Mitosis / Metaphase I or II) Kinetochore spindle microtubules pull chromosomes into alignment along the metaphase plate (congression).

Question 7
NCERT Exercise
Describe the following: (a) synapsis (b) bivalent (c) chiasmata. Draw a diagram to illustrate your answer.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Botanical & Cytological Definitions of Meiotic Phenomena:

(a) Synapsis:
The intimate, highly specific, longitudinal point-by-point pairing of maternal and paternal homologous chromosomes during the zygotene sub-stage of Prophase I of meiosis. It is mediated by the assembly of a tripartite proteinaceous ladder-like framework called the synaptonemal complex.

(b) Bivalent (or Tetrad):
The complex formed by a pair of synapsed homologous chromosomes. Since it consists of two distinct paired chromosomes, it is called a bivalent; because each chromosome has already duplicated into two chromatids, the pair exhibits four chromatids, termed a tetrad (clearly visible in pachytene).

(c) Chiasmata:
The distinct X-shaped points of physical attachment between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes, first visible during the diplotene stage when the synaptonemal complex dissolves and paired homologues begin to repel each other. Chiasmata represent the physical cytological sites where previous genetic crossing over occurred in pachytene.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Synapsis: homologous pairing via synaptonemal complex in zygotene1 Mark
Bivalent / Tetrad: pair of synapsed chromosomes showing 4 chromatids1 Mark
Chiasmata: X-shaped sites of crossing over visible in diplotene1 Mark
Question 8
NCERT Exercise
How does cytokinesis in plant cells differ from that in animal cells?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Differences Between Cytokinesis in Plant Cells and Animal Cells:

Parameter Cytokinesis in Plant Cells Cytokinesis in Animal Cells
1. Mechanism of Division Occurs by Cell Plate formation. Occurs by Cleavage Furrow constriction.
2. Direction of Progression Centrifugal: Starts at the center of the cell and grows outward towards the lateral parent cell walls. Centripetal: Initiates at the plasma membrane periphery as a shallow furrow and deepens inwards toward the center.
3. Structural Role Golgi-derived vesicles and spindle remnants coalesce at the equator to form a phragmoplast, which matures into the calcium pectate middle lamella. A contractile ring composed of actin and myosin microfilaments constricts beneath the plasma membrane to pinch the cell in two.
4. Influence of Cell Wall Necessitated by the presence of a rigid, inextensible outer cellulosic cell wall that cannot invaginate. Facilitated by the flexible, elastic outer plasma membrane (cell wall is absent).
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Plant: Cell plate method, centrifugal direction, phragmoplast/middle lamella1.5 Marks
Animal: Cleavage furrow, centripetal direction, contractile actin ring1.5 Marks
Question 9
NCERT Exercise
Find examples where the four stages of mitosis are suitable for observation.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Ideal Biological Materials for Observing the Four Stages of Mitosis:

1. Onion Root Tip Meristem (Allium cepa):
The classic, most universally used material in school and university laboratories for observing all four stages of mitosis because:
  • Cells in the sub-apical growing zone are actively dividing with no chloroplasts to obstruct vision.
  • Chromosomes are large, distinct, relatively few in number ($2n = 16$), and stain intensely with acetocarmine or Feulgen reagent.
2. Stage-Specific Optimal Observational Markers:
  • Prophase: Observable in root tip squashes: chromatin condenses into distinct entangled threads; nucleolus disappears.
  • Metaphase: Best observed in root tips treated briefly with colchicine (arrests spindle assembly). Chromosomes are most condensed, shortest, thickest, and aligned at the equatorial plate — ideal for karyotyping, morphology, and counting chromosome number.
  • Anaphase: Clearly displays centromere splitting and the characteristic V, L, J, or i shapes of migrating chromatids.
  • Telophase & Cytokinesis: Observable in growing shoot/root tips showing cell plate formation by phragmoplast.
3. Other Examples: Shoot apical meristems, bone marrow aspirates in animals, and blastula stages of fish/frog embryos.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Naming Onion root tip (Allium cepa) as the prime biological material1 Mark
Suitability of metaphase for counting/morphology & anaphase for centromere shape observation1 Mark
Question 10
NCERT Exercise
Can there be mitosis without DNA replication in 'S' phase?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Answer: No, normal mitosis cannot occur without DNA replication in the 'S' phase.

Biological & Genetic Justification:
  • The fundamental evolutionary mandate of mitosis is equational division: to deliver a complete, uncompromised, identical complement of genetic information to both daughter cells.
  • DNA replication during the 'S' phase creates two identical sister chromatids per chromosome ($2C \rightarrow 4C$).
  • If a cell skipped the S phase and attempted mitosis with unreplicated DNA ($2C$), when centromeres split in anaphase, each daughter cell would receive only an incomplete, fragmented half-set of genetic material ($1C$). Such cells would suffer massive gene deletions, aneuploidy, and instantaneous cell death.
  • Stringent biochemical cell cycle surveillance mechanisms (the $G_2/M$ DNA replication checkpoint) detect un-replicated DNA and activate checkpoint kinases ($ATM/ATR, Chk1$) to inhibit Cyclin B-CDK1 (MPF), halting the cell cycle and preventing entry into mitosis until replication is 100% complete.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Clear answer: No, normal mitosis cannot occur without S-phase DNA replication1 Mark
Explanation of severe genetic loss / aneuploidy & G2/M checkpoint block1 Mark
Question 11
NCERT Exercise
Can there be DNA replication without cell division?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Answer: Yes, DNA replication can occur without subsequent cell division.

Biological Phenomena and Examples:

1. Endomitosis / Endoreduplication:
A phenomenon where chromosomes repeatedly replicate and duplicate their DNA content within the nucleus without undergoing nuclear membrane breakdown or cell division. This leads to polyploidy (multiple sets of chromosomes, e.g., $4n, 8n, 16n$).
  • Examples: Tapetal nutritive cells in angiosperm anthers, human liver cells (hepatocytes), and bone marrow megakaryocytes (can reach up to $64n$).
2. Polytene Chromosomes (Giant Salivary Gland Chromosomes):
In the salivary glands of dipteran flies (such as Drosophila melanogaster and Chironomus), DNA replicates up to 10 rounds (1,024 strands) without chromatid separation or cell division, forming colossal banded polytene chromosomes.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Affirmative answer: Yes, DNA replication can occur without division1 Mark
Explanation of endoreduplication/polyploidy with examples (tapetum, hepatocytes, polytene chromosomes)1 Mark
Question 12
NCERT Exercise
Analyse the events of prophase I of meiosis.
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
In-Depth Analysis of Prophase I of Meiosis I:
Prophase I is exceptionally prolonged, intricate, and crucial because it is the phase where homologous genetic recombination occurs. It is divided into five consecutive sub-stages:

1. Leptotene (Thin-Thread Stage):
  • Chromatin fibers progressively condense and coil into long, slender, visible chromosomes.
  • Chromosomes often orient their telomeres towards one side of the nuclear envelope in a "bouquet" arrangement.
2. Zygotene (Paired-Thread Stage):
  • Homologous chromosomes (one paternal, one maternal) recognize each other and pair point-for-point in an event called synapsis.
  • Stabilized by the formation of a proteinaceous ribonucleoprotein structure called the synaptonemal complex.
  • The paired complex is called a bivalent.
3. Pachytene (Thick-Thread Stage):
  • Bivalents are clearly visible as four-chromatid tetrads.
  • Crossing Over: Non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes exchange genetic segments at recombination nodules. The reaction is enzyme-mediated, catalyzed by recombinase. Produces novel genetic variations.
4. Diplotene (Two-Thread Stage):
  • The synaptonemal complex undergoes dissolution. Paired homologous chromosomes repel each other, except at points of crossing over.
  • These persistent X-shaped crossing-over junction points are called chiasmata. (Can remain arrested for years, e.g., in human oocytes as dictyotene).
5. Diakinesis (Moving-Apart Stage):
  • Marked by terminalisation of chiasmata (chiasmata shift toward chromosome tips like a zipper).
  • Meiotic spindle begins to assemble; nucleolus completely disappears, and the nuclear envelope disintegrates.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
1. Leptotene: chromosome condensation and bouquet formation1 Mark
2. Zygotene: synapsis, synaptonemal complex, bivalent formation1 Mark
3. Pachytene: tetrads, crossing over at recombination nodules with recombinase1 Mark
4. Diplotene: dissolution of complex, appearance of X-shaped chiasmata1 Mark
5. Diakinesis: terminalisation of chiasmata, loss of nucleolus and nuclear envelope1 Mark
Question 13
NCERT Exercise
Discuss with your teacher: (a) haploid insects and lower plants where cell division occurs (b) some haploid cells in higher plants where cell division occurs.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
(a) Haploid Insects and Lower Plants Where Cell Division Occurs:
  • Male Honeybees (Drones) — Arrhenotoky: In the honeybee (Apis mellifera), males are haploid ($n = 16$). They develop parthenogenetically from unfertilized eggs. All their somatic tissues grow by mitosis. Drones produce sperm by specialized mitosis (without meiosis).
  • Lower Plants (Algae & Bryophytes): The entire dominant, vegetative, multi-cellular plant body of algae (e.g., Volvox, Spirogyra) and bryophytes (e.g., Marchantia, Funaria) is haploid ($n$). It undergoes millions of mitotic cell divisions to produce haploid leafy shoots and gametes.
(b) Haploid Cells in Higher Plants (Gymnosperms & Angiosperms) Where Cell Division Occurs:
  • Pollen Grain (Microgametophyte): The haploid microspore ($n$) divides mitotically into a vegetative cell and a generative cell. The generative cell further undergoes mitosis to yield two haploid male gametes.
  • Embryo Sac (Female Gametophyte): The functional haploid megaspore ($n$) undergoes three successive rounds of free nuclear mitotic divisions to form the 8-nucleate, 7-celled female gametophyte (egg cell, synergids, antipodals).
  • Gymnosperm Endosperm: Arises directly from mitotic division of the haploid megaspore prior to fertilization (haploid endosperm).

(a) Male honeybees (drones) and haploid gametophytes of algae/bryophytes1.5 Marks

Question 14
NCERT Exercise
Can there be mitosis without cell division? What would be the consequence?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Answer: Yes, karyokinesis (nuclear division) can occur repeatedly without being accompanied by cytokinesis (cell division).

Biological Consequence:
The failure of cytokinesis following nuclear division results in the formation of a multinucleate mass of protoplasm called a syncytium (in animals) or a coenocyte (in plants and fungi).

Classic Biological Examples:
  • Liquid Endosperm of Coconut: The primary endosperm nucleus undergoes hundreds of successive free nuclear mitotic divisions without cytokinesis, creating the milky fluid consisting of thousands of free nuclei (liquid coconut water).
  • Slime Molds (Myxomycetes): During the vegetative phase, plasmodium forms a large, creeping, multinucleate mass of protoplasm.
  • Skeletal Muscle Fibres: Multinucleate syncytial fibers formed by cell fusion.
  • Coenocytic Fungi & Algae: Rhizopus (Phycomycetes) and Vaucheria possess continuous tubular hyphae filled with multinucleate cytoplasm.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Affirmative confirmation: karyokinesis without cytokinesis is common1 Mark
Consequence: formation of multinucleate condition (syncytium / coenocyte)1 Mark
Classic examples: liquid endosperm of coconut, slime molds, skeletal muscle1 Mark
Question 15
NCERT Exercise
What is the significance of meiosis?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Evolutionary and Biological Significance of Meiosis:

1. Maintenance of Species Chromosome Number (Conservation of Karyotype):
Meiosis reduces the chromosome number by half from diploid ($2n$) to haploid ($n$) during gametogenesis in sexually reproducing organisms. When two haploid gametes fuse during fertilization (syngamy: $n + n = 2n$), the species-specific diploid chromosome number is precisely restored across generations, preventing doubling of chromosome numbers.

2. Generation of Genetic Diversity and Variations:
Meiosis is the primary engine of genetic novelty through two distinct mechanisms:
  • Crossing Over (Pachytene of Prophase I): Physical exchange of non-sister chromatid segments breaks linkages and creates recombinant allele combinations.
  • Independent Assortment (Metaphase / Anaphase I): Random orientation and maternal/paternal segregation of bivalents at the equator yields $2^n$ unique gametic combinations ($2^{23} \approx 8.4$ million combinations in humans).
3. Raw Material for Natural Selection and Organic Evolution:
The unique genetic combinations generated by meiosis confer phenotypic variability, enabling populations to adapt to fluctuating environments and providing the raw material upon which natural selection acts.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Conservation of species-specific chromosome number across generations via gametic halving1 Mark
Creation of genetic diversity via crossing over and independent assortment1 Mark
Role of variations in natural selection and organic evolution1 Mark
