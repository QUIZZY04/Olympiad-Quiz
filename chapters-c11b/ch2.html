Question 1
NCERT Exercise
Discuss how classification systems have undergone several changes over a period of time.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Chronological Evolution of Classification Systems:
Over centuries, biological classification has evolved progressively from simplistic utilitarian schemes to phylogenetically grounded molecular frameworks:

1. Two-Kingdom Classification (Carolus Linnaeus):
Linnaeus grouped all known organisms into two kingdoms: Plantae and Animalia. While simple, it suffered from severe limitations:
  • It did not distinguish between eukaryotes and prokaryotes (bacteria and cyanobacteria were placed under Plantae).
  • It grouped unicellular organisms (e.g., Chlamydomonas, Amoeba) with complex multicellular organisms (e.g., spirogyra, metazoans).
  • It failed to separate photosynthetic organisms (green plants) from heterotrophic, absorptive non-photosynthetic organisms (fungi).
2. Three-Kingdom Classification (Ernst Haeckel, 1866):
Haeckel separated unicellular microscopic organisms lacking tissue differentiation into Kingdom Protista, leaving multicellular plants in Plantae and multicellular animals in Animalia.

3. Four-Kingdom Classification (Herbert Copeland, 1956):
Copeland created Kingdom Monera to house all prokaryotic organisms (lacking nuclear membranes and membrane-bound organelles), distinguishing them from eukaryotic Protista.

4. Five-Kingdom Classification (R.H. Whittaker, 1969):
Whittaker resolved the ambiguity of fungi by creating Kingdom Fungi based on chitinous cell walls and saprotrophic absorptive nutrition. His five criteria were: (i) Cell structure (prokaryotic vs. eukaryotic), (ii) Thallus / body organisation, (iii) Mode of nutrition, (iv) Reproduction, and (v) Phylogenetic relationships.

5. Three-Domain System (Carl Woese, 1990):
Divided Monera into two distinct domains: Archaea and Bacteria, based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing and unique cell membrane lipid architecture, placing all eukaryotes in Domain Eukarya.

Question 2
NCERT Exercise
State two economically important uses of: (a) heterotrophic bacteria (b) archaebacteria.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
(a) Two Economically Important Uses of Heterotrophic Bacteria:
Heterotrophic bacteria are the most abundant decomposers in nature and have extensive industrial and agricultural utility:
  • Dairy & Fermentation Industry: Lactobacillus (Lactic Acid Bacteria / LAB) ferment lactose into lactic acid, causing curdling of milk to form curd, yoghurt, and cheese.
  • Production of Antibiotics & Nitrogen Fixation: Species of Streptomyces synthesize vital clinical antibiotics (e.g., streptomycin, tetracycline), while symbiotic bacteria like Rhizobium leguminosarum fix atmospheric nitrogen in legume root nodules, enriching soil fertility.
(b) Two Economically Important Uses of Archaebacteria:
Archaebacteria thrive under extreme environmental niches due to their unique branched branched-chain ether-linked pseudomurein walls:
  • Biogas (Methane) Production: Methanogens (e.g., Methanobacterium) present in the anaerobic rumen of ruminant animals (cows, buffaloes) ferment cellulosic plant matter, generating biogas (methane) from animal dung (gobar gas plants).
  • Thermostable Enzymes in Biotechnology: Thermus aquaticus (a thermophilic bacterium closely allied with extreme conditions) provides Taq DNA polymerase, an enzyme capable of retaining catalytic fidelity at 95°C during automated Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) DNA amplification.

Question 3
NCERT Exercise
What is the nature of cell-walls in diatoms?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Distinctive Nature and Structural Composition of Diatom Cell Walls:
Diatoms (Chrysophytes) possess a uniquely engineered cell wall structure known as a frustule:
  • Two Overlapping Halves (Soap-Box Architecture): The cell wall forms two thin, overlapping shells that fit tightly together like the lid and base of a soapbox. The upper outer valve is termed the epitheca, and the smaller lower valve is termed the hypotheca.
  • Silica Impregnation (Indestructible Nature): The walls are heavily embedded with hydrated amorphous silica ($SiO_2 \cdot nH_2O$). Because silica is chemically inert and highly resistant to acids and enzymatic degradation, diatom cell walls are virtually indestructible.
  • Formation of Diatomaceous Earth (Kieselguhr): When diatoms die, their organic protoplasm decomposes, but their siliceous frustules deposit on ocean beds over millions of years, forming enormous geologic deposits called diatomaceous earth.
  • Economic Applications: Being gritty and porous, diatomaceous earth is extensively utilized in filtration of oils, syrups, and antibiotic broths, polishing metals, and manufacturing sound-proof heat insulators.

Question 4
NCERT Exercise
Find out what do the terms 'algal bloom' and 'red-tides' signify.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Algal Bloom:
An algal bloom is the rapid, explosive proliferation and excessive accumulation of microscopic phytoplankton—predominantly cyanobacteria (blue-green algae like Microcystis, Anabaena, Oscillatoria) and green algae—in nutrient-enriched water bodies (eutrophic lakes and slow-moving rivers).
  • Cause: High concentration of agricultural runoff containing nitrates and phosphates (cultural eutrophication).
  • Consequences: The dense layer of algae blocks sunlight penetration, depleting dissolved oxygen (DO) as dead algae are decomposed by aerobic bacteria, leading to massive fish mortality and production of noxious toxins (cyanotoxins).
2. Red Tides:
Red tides refer to a specific marine phenomenon where coastal ocean waters appear reddish, brown, or pink due to the explosive multiplication and bloom of red-pigmented dinoflagellates (such as Gonyaulax polyedra and Gymnodinium).
  • Toxin Secretion (Saxitoxin): These blooming dinoflagellates release potent neurotoxins, notably saxitoxin, into the water column.
  • Biological Hazard: The toxin accumulates in filter-feeding shellfish (clams, oysters, mussels). While harmless to the molluscs, consumption of contaminated shellfish by marine mammals, birds, or humans causes severe, often lethal Paralytic Shellfish Poisoning (PSP).

Definition of algal bloom, nutrient enrichment (eutrophication), and oxygen depletion1.5 Marks

Question 5
NCERT Exercise
How are viroids different from viruses?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Comparative Differences Between Viroids and Viruses:

Parameter Viroids (Discovered by T.O. Diener, 1971) Viruses (Ivanowsky, Beijerinck, Stanley)
1. Protein Coat (Capsid) Completely absent (consist of naked RNA). Present (genetic material is enclosed within a protein coat called capsid made of capsomeres).
2. Genetic Material Only single-stranded RNA (ssRNA). Either DNA or RNA (single-stranded or double-stranded), never both.
3. Molecular Weight RNA has very low molecular weight. Nucleic acid has relatively high molecular weight.
4. Size Much smaller than viruses (sub-viral infectious agents). Larger than viroids (ranging from 20 to 300 nm).
5. Host Specificity & Examples Exclusively infect higher plants (e.g., Potato Spindle Tuber Viroid - PSTVd). Infect plants, animals, fungi, and bacteria (e.g., TMV, Influenza, HIV, Bacteriophages).

Question 6
NCERT Exercise
Describe briefly the four major groups of Protozoa.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
The Four Major Morphological & Locomotory Groups of Protozoa:
Protozoans are unicellular, heterotrophic, eukaryotic microorganisms belonging to Kingdom Protista. They are classified into four groups based on their locomotory organelles:

1. Amoeboid Protozoans:
  • Habitat & Locomotion: Inhabit freshwater, seawater, or moist soil. Move and capture prey by extending false feet called pseudopodia (blunt cytoplasmic projections), e.g., Amoeba.
  • Special Features: Marine forms possess silica shells on their surface. Parasitic forms like Entamoeba histolytica cause amoebic dysentery in humans.
2. Flagellated Protozoans:
  • Locomotion & Lifestyle: Possess one or more whip-like flagella for active swimming. They are either free-living or endoparasitic.
  • Pathogenic Example: Trypanosoma gambiense is transmitted by the tsetse fly (Glossina) and causes fatal Human African Trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness).
3. Ciliated Protozoans:
  • Locomotion & Ingestion: Aquatic, actively moving organisms possessing thousands of coordinated hair-like cilia beating rhythmically in metachronal waves, e.g., Paramoecium.
  • Anatomical Specialisation: Feature an oral cavity (gullet) opening to the outside. The coordinated movement of cilia steers water laden with food particles directly into the gullet. Possess nuclear dualism (macronucleus and micronucleus).
4. Sporozoans:
  • Characteristics: Endoparasitic protozoans lacking specialized locomotory organelles (cilia, flagella, or pseudopodia). Characterized by having an infectious spore-like stage in their life cycle.
  • Pathogenic Example: Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium falciparum, the causative agents of malaria, which cycles between female Anopheles mosquitoes and human hosts.

Question 7
NCERT Exercise
Plants are autotrophic. Can you think of some plants that are partially heterotrophic?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Partially Heterotrophic Plants in Kingdom Plantae:
While the vast majority of angiosperms are photoautotrophic (possessing chlorophyll $a$ and $b$ to synthesize sugars via photosynthesis), certain specialized plants are partially heterotrophic because they supplement their nitrogen and mineral requirements by consuming insects or parasitizing other plants:

1. Insectivorous (Carnivorous) Plants:
These plants grow in waterlogged, acidic bogs and nitrate-deficient soils. They have functional green leaves and carry out photosynthesis to produce carbohydrates, but trap and digest small insects to acquire vital nitrogenous compounds (amino acids):
  • Pitcher Plant (Nepenthes): The leaf lamina is modified into a tubular pitcher filled with digestive enzymes and closed by a lid. Insects falling into the pitcher slip on waxy walls and are digested.
  • Venus Flytrap (Dionaea muscipula): Leaf blades are modified into two sensitive hinged lobes with trigger hairs that snap shut within milliseconds when stimulated by an insect.
  • Bladderwort (Utricularia): Aquatic plant with tiny segmented leaf-bladders operating under negative hydrostatic pressure to suck in aquatic micro-invertebrates.
  • Sundew (Drosera): Leaves bear glandular tentacles secreting glistening, sticky mucilage containing proteolytic enzymes.
2. Parasitic Plants:
  • Total Stem Parasite (Cuscuta / Dodder / Amarbel): Lacks chlorophyll and functional roots; twines around host branches and sends adventitious roots called haustoria directly into host xylem and phloem to extract water, minerals, and readymade sugars.
  • Partial Parasites (e.g., Viscum / Mistletoe): Possess green leaves to photosynthesize, but embed haustoria into host xylem to absorb water and inorganic minerals.

Explanation of why insectivorous plants trap insects (nitrogen-deficient habitats)1 Mark

Question 8
NCERT Exercise
What do the terms phycobiont and mycobiont signify?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Significance of Phycobiont and Mycobiont in Lichen Symbiosis:
Lichens represent a classic, obligate, mutualistic symbiotic partnership between an autotrophic algal component and a heterotrophic fungal component:

1. Phycobiont (Photobiont):
  • Definition: The photosynthetic, algal or cyanobacterial partner in the lichen thallus (typically green algae like Trebouxia or blue-green algae like Nostoc).
  • Function & Significance: Synthesizes organic carbohydrates through photosynthesis and supplies readymade nourishment to the fungal partner. When cyanobacteria are present, they also actively fix atmospheric nitrogen.
2. Mycobiont:
  • Definition: The non-photosynthetic fungal partner in the lichen thallus (predominantly Ascomycetes and rarely Basidiomycetes).
  • Function & Significance: Forms the structural body (thallus) that encloses and protects the delicate algal cells from desiccation, mechanical injury, and intense solar radiation. Its dense hyphal network anchors the lichen to substrates (rocks, bark) via rhizines and absorbs water and mineral nutrients from the atmosphere and substrate, channeling them to the phycobiont.
Key Ecological Note: Neither partner can survive independently in extreme alpine, arctic, or desert environments. Lichens are also highly sensitive bio-indicators of atmospheric sulfur dioxide ($SO_2$) pollution ("pollution indicators").

Question 9
NCERT Exercise
Give a comparative account of the classes of Kingdom Fungi under the following: (i) mode of nutrition (ii) mode of reproduction.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Comparative Analysis of the Four Classes of Kingdom Fungi:

Class & Common Name Mycelium Type (i) Mode of Nutrition (ii) Mode of Reproduction (Asexual & Sexual)
1. Phycomycetes
(Algal Fungi)		 Aseptate and coenocytic (multinucleate). Obligate parasites on plants, saprotrophs on decaying wood or in aquatic moist habitats. Asexual: Zoospores (motile) or aplanospores (non-motile) formed endogenously in sporangia.
Sexual: Zygospore formed by isogamous or oogamous fusion of gametes. (e.g., Mucor, Rhizopus, Albugo).
2. Ascomycetes
(Sac Fungi)		 Branched and septate. Saprotrophic, decomposers, parasitic, or coprophilous (growing on dung). Asexual: Conidia produced exogenously on special hyphae called conidiophores.
Sexual: Ascospores produced endogenously in sac-like asci arranged in fruiting bodies called ascocarps. (e.g., Penicillium, Aspergillus, Neurospora).
3. Basidiomycetes
(Club Fungi)		 Branched and septate (with clamp connections). Saprotrophic on soil, logs, and tree stumps, or internal parasites causing rusts and smuts. Asexual: Asexual spores generally not found; vegetative fragmentation common.
Sexual: Sex organs absent; somatogamy occurs. Basidiospores produced exogenously on a basidium inside basidiocarps. (e.g., Agaricus, Ustilago, Puccinia).
4. Deuteromycetes
(Fungi Imperfecti)		 Branched and septate. Largely saprophytes or parasites; many are crucial decomposers of leaf litter helping mineral cycling. Asexual: Reproduce exclusively by asexual conidia.
Sexual: Sexual (perfect) stage is completely unknown or absent. If discovered, moved to Ascomycetes or Basidiomycetes. (e.g., Alternaria, Colletotrichum, Trichoderma).

Ascomycetes: septate, coprophilous/saprophytic, exogenous conidia & endogenous ascospores1 Mark

Question 10
NCERT Exercise
What are the characteristic features of Euglenoids?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Key Characteristic Features of Euglenoids:
Euglenoids (e.g., Euglena viridis) are distinctive unicellular, flagellated protists primarily found in stagnant freshwater bodies:
  • Absence of Cell Wall (Pellicle): Instead of a cellulosic cell wall, their body is bounded by a protein-rich flexible membrane layer called the pellicle, which permits active changes in cell shape (metaboly or euglenoid movement).
  • Heterokont Flagella: They bear two unequal flagella inserted in an anterior invagination (reservoir): one long, active, locomotory flagellum extending outward, and one short, non-emergent flagellum.
  • Mixotrophic Nutrition: They exhibit a remarkable dual mode of nutrition:
    • In the presence of sunlight, they are photoautotrophic, possessing chlorophyll $a$ and $b$ identical to those in higher plants.
    • When deprived of sunlight in dark or nutrient-rich waters, they become heterotrophic predators, capturing and ingesting smaller organisms (saprotrophic / phagotrophic).
  • Photosensitive Stigma (Eye-Spot): An orange-red pigmented carotenoid eye-spot (stigma) located near the reservoir detects light intensity and directs phototactic movement toward optimal light.
  • Reserve Food: Store carbohydrates in the form of unique $\beta$-1,3-glucan granules called paramylon (paramylum).
  • Osmoregulation: Possess an anterior contractile vacuole to expel excess water entering hypotonic freshwater environments.

Proteinaceous pellicle replacing cell wall and providing body flexibility0.5 Mark

Question 11
NCERT Exercise
Give a brief account of viruses with respect to their structure and nature of genetic material. Also name four common viral diseases.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Comprehensive Account of Viral Structure and Genetic Material:
Viruses are non-cellular, sub-microscopic, obligate intracellular parasites that remain inert crystalline nucleoprotein structures outside living host cells:

1. Structural Organization:
  • Core Genome (Nucleic Acid): Located internally, containing the infectious genetic blueprint. A single virus contains either DNA or RNA, never both simultaneously.
  • Protein Coat (Capsid): The protective shell surrounding the nucleic acid, composed of small repeating protein subunits called capsomeres. Capsomeres are arranged geometrically in helical (e.g., Tobacco Mosaic Virus) or polyhedral / icosahedral forms (e.g., Adenovirus, Poliovirus).
  • Viral Envelope: Some animal viruses (e.g., Influenza, HIV, SARS-CoV-2) possess an outer lipid-bilayer envelope derived from host membranes, studded with viral glycoproteins (spikes) facilitating host attachment.
2. Nature of Viral Genetic Material:
  • Plant Viruses: Generally possess single-stranded RNA (ssRNA), e.g., Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV).
  • Bacteriophages (Bacterial Viruses): Usually possess double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), e.g., T4 bacteriophage.
  • Animal Viruses: May possess single-stranded RNA (ssRNA - Influenza, Rabies, Polio, Retroviruses like HIV), double-stranded RNA (dsRNA - Reovirus), or double-stranded DNA (dsDNA - Poxvirus, Herpesvirus, Hepatitis B).
3. Four Common Viral Diseases:
  • In Humans: (i) Influenza (Flu), (ii) AIDS (Acquired Immuno Deficiency Syndrome caused by HIV), (iii) Smallpox / Chickenpox, (iv) Poliomyelitis (Polio), (v) Rabies.
  • In Plants: Tobacco mosaic disease, Cucumber mosaic, Leaf curling and yellow vein mosaic in Bhindi.

Structure: Capsid made of capsomeres (helical/polyhedral) protecting internal genetic core1.5 Marks
Genome nature: DNA or RNA (never both); ssRNA in plant viruses, dsDNA in bacteriophages, diverse in animals1.5 Marks
Naming four common viral diseases in humans or plants1 Mark
Question 12
NCERT Exercise
Organise a discussion in your class on the topic - Are viruses living or non-living?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Scientific Debate: Are Viruses Living or Non-Living Entities?
Biologists describe viruses as entities existing at the "threshold of life" or biological twilight zone, because they exhibit a dual personality:

A. Arguments and Characteristics Supporting that Viruses are Non-Living:
  • Lack of Cellular Organization: They are non-cellular (acellular); they have no cytoplasm, cell organelles (mitochondria, ribosomes, ER), or cell membrane.
  • Metabolic Inertia: Outside a host cell, they possess no metabolic activity, respiratory machinery, or autonomous enzyme systems for generating ATP.
  • Crystallizability: Like non-living chemical minerals, purified virus particles can be crystallized and stored indefinitely in bottles without losing infectious potential (first demonstrated by W.M. Stanley in 1935 for TMV).
  • No Independent Growth: They do not show independent growth or division outside host living cytoplasm.
B. Arguments and Characteristics Supporting that Viruses are Living:
  • Genetic Material: They possess definite genetic material (either DNA or RNA) that encodes specific viral structural and functional proteins.
  • Replication & Reproduction: Inside living host cells, they take over the host cell's metabolic machinery to replicate their genome and assemble thousands of viral progeny.
  • Mutation & Evolution: Their genetic material undergoes spontaneous mutations and genetic recombination, evolving rapidly to evade host immune defenses (e.g., antigen drift in Influenza and HIV).
  • Infectivity & Specificity: They exhibit strict host specificity and cause infectious, transmissible diseases in specific host organisms.
Scientific Conclusion:
Viruses are best classified as obligate intracellular molecular parasites representing a vital evolutionary link between complex non-living prebiotic macromolecular assemblies and cellular living organisms.

Listing non-living attributes: acellular, no metabolism/respiration, crystallizable1.5 Marks
