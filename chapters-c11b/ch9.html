Question 1
NCERT Exercise
What are macromolecules? Give examples.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Scientific Definition of Macromolecules:
Biomolecules found in living tissues are divided into two distinct analytical fractions when ground in trichloroacetic acid ($Cl_3C-COOH$):
  • Acid-Soluble Pool (Filtrate / Biomicromolecules): Small organic molecules with molecular weights ranging strictly between 18 and 800 Daltons (Da) (e.g., simple sugars, amino acids, nucleotides).
  • Acid-Insoluble Fraction (Retentate / Biomolecules): Large, polymeric compounds characterized by high molecular weights exceeding 10,000 Daltons. These are defined as biomacromolecules.
The Four Major Classes of Macromolecules in Living Systems:
  1. Proteins: Heteropolymers of $\alpha$-amino acids joined by peptide bonds (e.g., Collagen, Hemoglobin, Insulin, RuBisCO).
  2. Polysaccharides: Complex polymers of monosaccharides linked by glycosidic bonds (e.g., Cellulose, Starch, Glycogen, Chitin).
  3. Nucleic Acids: Long polynucleotide chains linked by 3'-5' phosphodiester bonds (e.g., Deoxyribonucleic acid / DNA, Ribonucleic acid / RNA).
  4. Note on Lipids: Lipids have molecular weights under 800 Da, but form macromolecular aggregate vesicles in the acid-insoluble retentate due to hydrophobic interactions; hence, they are not strictly chemical macromolecules.

Question 2
NCERT Exercise
Illustrate a glycosidic, peptide and a phospho-diester bond.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Biochemical Nature and Formation of Key Biopolymer Linkages:

1. Peptide Bond (in Polypeptides / Proteins):
  • Formed when the carboxyl group ($-COOH$) of one $\alpha$-amino acid reacts with the amino group ($-NH_2$) of the succeeding amino acid with the elimination of a water molecule (condensation dehydration reaction).
  • $$\text{R}_1\text{-CH(NH}_2\text{)-COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{N-CH(R}_2\text{)-COOH} \xrightarrow{-\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{R}_1\text{-CH(NH}_2\text{)-}\mathbf{CO-NH}\text{-CH(R}_2\text{)-COOH}$$
  • The planar linkage $\mathbf{-CO-NH-}$ is the peptide bond.
2. Glycosidic Bond (in Polysaccharides):
  • Formed between two individual monosaccharide rings when the anomeric hydroxyl ($-OH$) group of one sugar condenses with a hydroxyl group of an adjacent sugar with the loss of one water molecule.
  • $$\text{C}_6\text{H}_{11}\text{O}_5\text{-OH} + \text{HO-C}_6\text{H}_{11}\text{O}_5 \xrightarrow{-\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_{11}\text{O}_5\mathbf{-O-}\text{C}_6\text{H}_{11}\text{O}_5$$
  • The ether linkage $\mathbf{-C-O-C-}$ is the glycosidic bond (e.g., $\alpha$-1,4-linkage in maltose/amylose; $\beta$-1,4 in cellulose).
3. Phosphodiester Bond (in Nucleic Acids — DNA/RNA):
  • A central phosphate group ($-\text{PO}_4^{3-}$) forms two ester bonds simultaneously: one with the 3'-OH of the pentose sugar of one nucleotide, and the other with the 5'-OH of the sugar of the adjacent nucleotide.
  • $$\text{Sugar}_1\text{-3'-O-PO}_2^-\text{-O-5'-Sugar}_2$$
  • This repeating $\mathbf{3'-5'\text{ phosphodiester linkage}}$ creates the continuous sugar-phosphate backbone of nucleic acids.

Question 3
NCERT Exercise
What is meant by tertiary structure of proteins?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition and Significance of Tertiary ($3^\circ$) Protein Structure:
The tertiary structure of a protein refers to the overall three-dimensional spatial conformation achieved when a secondary polypeptide chain (with $\alpha$-helices and $\beta$-pleated sheets) folds extensively upon itself like a hollow woolen ball, producing a compact globular shape.

1. Non-Covalent and Covalent Stabilizing Forces:
Tertiary conformation is maintained by diverse chemical interactions among amino acid $R$-side chains:
  • Hydrophobic Interactions: Non-polar aliphatic and aromatic side chains (leucine, valine, phenylalanine) cluster internally away from surrounding aqueous cytosol.
  • Hydrogen Bonds: Formed between polar uncharged side chains.
  • Ionic (Electrostatic) Bonds / Salt Bridges: Formed between positively charged basic groups (lysine, arginine) and negatively charged acidic groups (aspartate, glutamate).
  • Disulfide Bonds ($-S-S-$): Strong covalent linkages formed between two cysteine residues via oxidation of sulfhydryl ($-SH$) groups.
2. Biological Significance:
Tertiary structure is absolutely mandatory for biological activity. Folding brings distant amino acid residues together in 3D space to sculpt geometric pockets called active sites, enabling enzymes to bind specific substrates with high catalytic efficiency. Denaturation destroys the $3^\circ$ conformation, causing irreversible loss of enzymatic activity.

Question 5
NCERT Exercise
Proteins have primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure. Discuss each.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
The Four Hierarchical Levels of Protein Structural Organization (Linderstrøm-Lang):

1. Primary Structure ($1^\circ$):
  • The linear sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain encoded by mRNA codons.
  • Positional information: The first amino acid is at the N-terminus (amino terminal), and the last amino acid is at the C-terminus (carboxyl terminal).
  • Maintained strictly by covalent peptide bonds. Any mutation changing the primary sequence can alter protein function (e.g., Sickle-cell anemia where Glu → Val at position 6 of $\beta$-globin).
2. Secondary Structure ($2^\circ$):
  • Local folding of the polypeptide backbone into regular periodic spatial motifs stabilized by hydrogen bonds between the peptide $C=O$ and $N-H$ groups:
    • $\alpha$-Helix: Polypeptide chain is coiled into a right-handed spiral. Intrachain H-bonds form between the $C=O$ of residue $n$ and $N-H$ of residue $n+4$ (e.g., $\alpha$-keratin in hair/wool).
    • $\beta$-Pleated Sheet: Polypeptide segments run parallel or antiparallel, joined by interchain or intrachain hydrogen bonds (e.g., silk fibroin).
3. Tertiary Structure ($3^\circ$):
  • Complex three-dimensional folding of secondary structure into a compact globular architecture.
  • Stabilized by hydrophobic interactions, ionic bonds, van der Waals forces, and covalent disulfide ($-S-S-$) bridges. Essential for enzymatic catalytic active site pockets (e.g., myoglobin, lysozyme).
4. Quaternary Structure ($4^\circ$):
  • Applies to multimeric proteins composed of two or more independent polypeptide chains (called subunits or protomers).
  • Subunits fit together in a precise geometric spatial arrangement. Classic Example: Human Adult Hemoglobin ($HbA$), which is a tetramer composed of two identical $\alpha$-subunits (141 amino acids each) and two identical $\beta$-subunits (146 amino acids each) — ($2\alpha + 2\beta$).

Question 6

Find out how much cellulose is made by plants annually.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Global Annual Biosynthesis of Cellulose:
Cellulose is the primary structural homopolymer of $\beta$-D-glucose units ($[C_6H_{10}O_5]_n$) in plant cell walls and is the most abundant organic macromolecule on Earth:

  • Globally, terrestrial and aquatic photosynthetic vegetation synthesizes an estimated $10^{11}$ tonnes (approximately 100 billion tonnes or $100 \times 10^9$ metric tons) of cellulose annually.
  • This colossal quantity represents roughly 50% to 60% of all carbon fixed on Earth through photosynthesis each year.
  • Cotton fibers are nearly pure cellulose (~90%), whereas dry wood contains roughly 40% to 50% cellulose along with lignin and hemicellulose.

Question 7
NCERT Exercise
Describe the important properties of enzymes.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Key Biochemical Properties of Enzymes (Biocatalysts):

1. Proteinaceous Nature:
Almost all enzymes are globular proteins possessing unique tertiary ($3^\circ$) conformations (exceptions are catalytic RNA molecules called ribozymes, e.g., 23S rRNA).

2. Unprecedented Catalytic Efficiency:
Enzymes accelerate chemical reaction rates by immense factors (ranging from $10^6$ to $10^{12}$ times). For instance, carbonic anhydrase hydrates 600,000 $CO_2$ molecules per second (turnover number = $36 \times 10^6$ per min).

3. Specificity of Action:
Enzymes display absolute or stereo-chemical substrate specificity: an enzyme typically catalyzes only one specific reaction or acts on one specific chemical bond (Lock-and-Key or Induced-Fit model).

4. Unaltered at the End of Reaction:
Enzymes participate transiently in reactions by forming an unstable enzyme-substrate complex ($ES$), but are released completely chemically unchanged at reaction completion to catalyze new cycles.

5. Lowering of Activation Energy:
Enzymes do not alter the overall free energy change ($\Delta G$) or equilibrium constant ($K_{eq}$) of a reaction; they function strictly by lowering the activation energy barrier ($E_a$).

6. Sensitivity to Temperature and pH:
Exhibit optimum catalytic activity at specific physiological temperature (37°C in humans) and pH. High temperatures cause thermal denaturation, while low temperatures cause reversible inactivation.

Question 8
NCERT Exercise
Explain the Watson-Crick model of DNA double helix.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
The B-DNA Double Helix Model (James Watson & Francis Crick, 1953):
Based on Rosalind Franklin & Maurice Wilkins' X-ray diffraction data and Erwin Chargaff's rules, the B-form of DNA exhibits the following hallmark features:

  • 1. Two Polynucleotide Chains: DNA consists of two complementary helical polynucleotide chains wound around a common central imaginary axis in a right-handed spiral.
  • 2. Antiparallel Polarity: The two strands run in opposite directions: one strand has $5' \rightarrow 3'$ polarity, and the complementary strand has $3' \rightarrow 5'$ polarity.
  • 3. Sugar-Phosphate Backbone & Inward Bases: The hydrophilic backbone is on the outside composed of alternating deoxyribose sugars and phosphate groups linked by $3'-5'$ phosphodiester bonds. The hydrophobic nitrogenous bases project inward perpendicular to the main axis.
  • 4. Complementary Base Pairing (Chargaff's Rule):
    • Adenine (A) always pairs with Thymine (T) via two hydrogen bonds ($A = T$).
    • Guanine (G) always pairs with Cytosine (C) via three hydrogen bonds ($G \equiv C$).
    • Consequently, a purine always pairs with a pyrimidine, maintaining a uniform constant helix diameter of 2.0 nm (20 Å).
  • 5. Helical Dimensions:
    • Pitch of the helix (one complete $360^\circ$ turn) = 3.4 nm (34 Å).
    • There are approximately 10 base pairs (bp) per helical turn.
    • Distance between two adjacent base pairs = 0.34 nm (3.4 Å).
  • 6. Base Stacking Stability: The plane of one base pair stacks over the other in the double helix, conferring thermodynamic hydrophobic stability.

Question 9
NCERT Exercise
What are co-factors? Describe the different types of co-factors with examples (prosthetic groups, co-enzymes, metal ions).
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition of Co-factors:
Many enzymes are simple proteins, but numerous complex enzymes (called holoenzymes) require non-protein chemical components called co-factors to achieve catalytic activity. The proteinaceous portion of the enzyme is called the apoenzyme: $$\text{Holoenzyme (Active)} = \text{Apoenzyme (Inactive Protein)} + \text{Co-factor (Non-protein)}$$ If the co-factor is removed, the catalytic activity of the enzyme is completely lost.

Three Distinct Categories of Co-factors:

1. Prosthetic Groups:
  • Non-protein organic compounds that are tightly and permanently bound to the apoenzyme by covalent or strong non-covalent forces.
  • Example: Heme is the iron-porphyrin prosthetic group tightly attached to the active sites of catalase and peroxidase, which catalyze the decomposition of toxic hydrogen peroxide ($H_2O_2 \rightarrow H_2O + \frac{1}{2}O_2$).
2. Co-enzymes:
  • Non-protein organic compounds that are loosely and transiently bound to the apoenzyme, typically associating only during the course of catalysis. They function as transient carriers of electrons or chemical groups.
  • Most co-enzymes are derived from water-soluble vitamins:
    • NAD (Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide) and NADP contain vitamin niacin.
    • FAD and FMN contain vitamin riboflavin ($B_2$).
    • Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) contains vitamin thiamine ($B_1$).
3. Metal Ions (Co-factor Activators):
  • Inorganic metal cations that form coordination bonds with amino acid side chains at the active site and simultaneously with the substrate.
  • Examples:
    • $Zn^{2+}$: Essential co-factor for carboxypeptidase, carbonic anhydrase, and alcohol dehydrogenase.
    • $Mg^{2+}$: Required by hexokinase, phosphofructokinase, and DNA/RNA polymerases.
    • $Fe^{2+} / Fe^{3+}$: Component of cytochromes in electron transport chain.

Question 10
NCERT Exercise
Describe the competitive inhibition of enzymes with an example (malonate inhibiting succinate dehydrogenase).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Mechanism of Competitive Inhibition of Enzymes:

1. Molecular Principle:
  • Occurs when an inhibitor molecule closely resembles the natural substrate in its three-dimensional chemical and steric structure.
  • Due to structural similarity, the competitive inhibitor competes directly with the substrate for binding to the active site of the enzyme.
  • When the inhibitor binds to the active site, it forms an inactive Enzyme-Inhibitor ($EI$) complex, preventing substrate binding and reducing the reaction velocity.
  • Reversibility: Competitive inhibition can be completely reversed by significantly increasing the concentration of the natural substrate.
  • Kinetic Effects: $V_{max}$ remains unchanged, while $K_m$ (Michaelis constant) increases.
2. Classic Textbook Example — Inhibition of Succinate Dehydrogenase:
  • In the Krebs cycle, the enzyme succinate dehydrogenase oxidizes succinate into fumarate.
  • Malonate ($HOOC-CH_2-COOH$) has a 3-carbon dicarboxylic structure closely mimicking the 4-carbon substrate succinate ($HOOC-CH_2-CH_2-COOH$).
  • Malonate binds tightly to the active site of succinate dehydrogenase. Because malonate cannot be dehydrogenated, catalysis halts, blocking the Krebs cycle.
  • Clinical Application: Sulfa drugs (sulfanilamide) competitively inhibit the bacterial enzyme that incorporates para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) into folic acid, halting bacterial multiplication.

Question 12
NCERT Exercise
Why are lipids not strictly considered macromolecules?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Scientific Rationale Why Lipids Are Not Strictly Macromolecules:

1. Low Molecular Weight (<800 Daltons):
Biomacromolecules (such as proteins, nucleic acids, and polysaccharides) are characterized by having molecular weights exceeding 10,000 Daltons. In sharp contrast, individual lipid molecules (fatty acids, triglycerides, and phospholipids like lecithin) have small molecular weights that never exceed 800 Daltons.

2. Non-Polymeric Nature:
True macromolecules are polymers formed by the repetitive covalent bonding of monomeric sub-units (amino acids form proteins, nucleotides form DNA, glucose forms starch). Lipids are not polymers; a triglyceride is merely a single condensation ester of one glycerol molecule with three fatty acids.

3. Reason for Presence in the Acid-Insoluble Fraction:
When living tissue is homogenized in trichloroacetic acid, cell membranes and organelle envelopes (plasma membrane, ER, mitochondria) are disrupted into fragments. Being hydrophobic and water-insoluble, these lipid fragments spontaneously aggregate into spherical vesicles (micelles / liposomes). Because these large aggregates do not dissolve in aqueous acid, they fail to pass through the filter cloth and remain in the acid-insoluble retentate alongside true macromolecules.

Conclusion: Structurally and biochemically, lipids belong to the biomicromolecules, but behaviorally they fractionate with the acid-insoluble macromolecular pool.

Question 13
NCERT Exercise
What is an active site of an enzyme? How does enzyme action lower activation energy?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. What is an Active Site?
An active site is a specific crevice, pocket, or cleft on the surface of an enzyme created by the folding of its tertiary ($3^\circ$) polypeptide structure, lined with specific catalytic amino acid side chains. It is the designated site to which substrate molecules bind with high geometric and chemical complementarity to form an unstable intermediate Enzyme-Substrate ($ES$) complex.

2. How Enzyme Action Lowers Activation Energy ($E_a$):
Activation energy is the minimum amount of energy required by reactant molecules to overcome the energy barrier and reach an unstable, high-energy transition state to convert into products.
Enzymes drastically lower this barrier through several physical and chemical mechanisms:
  • Proximity and Orientation: Holds substrate molecules in the exact optimal spatial alignment for reactive bonds to collide.
  • Substrate Strain (Induced Fit): Binding induces conformational changes that stretch and distort covalent bonds in the substrate, making them fragile and easier to cleave.
  • Micro-environmental Catalysis: Provides specialized local environments (e.g., acidic/basic residues, hydrophobic pockets, metal ion coordination) that stabilize the transition state.
Consequently, a vastly larger proportion of reactant molecules possess sufficient thermal energy to achieve the lowered transition state, accelerating reaction rates millions of times without raising temperature.

Question 14
NCERT Exercise
Explain the effect of temperature and pH on enzyme activity.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Impact of Temperature and pH on Enzymatic Catalytic Velocity:

1. Effect of Temperature:
  • Bell-Shaped Curve: Enzyme activity plotted against temperature displays a characteristic bell-shaped curve with a peak at an optimum temperature (typically 35°C–40°C in humans).
  • Low Temperature (Below Optimum): At near-freezing temperatures, the enzyme is reversibly inactivated due to low kinetic energy; enzyme-substrate collisions are infrequent, but tertiary structure remains intact.
  • Temperature Coefficient ($Q_{10}$): For every 10°C rise in temperature up to optimum, reaction rate approximately doubles ($Q_{10} \approx 2$).
  • High Temperature (Above Optimum >45°C–50°C): Thermal energy disrupts weak hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and ionic bridges maintaining the tertiary structure. The enzyme undergoes irreversible denaturation, destroying the active site. (Exception: Thermophilic bacteria like Thermus aquaticus possess heat-stable enzymes surviving at 95°C).
2. Effect of pH:
  • Optimum pH: Every enzyme exhibits maximum catalytic efficiency at a specific optimum pH (e.g., Pepsin at pH 1.5–2.0, Salivary Amylase at pH 6.8, Trypsin at pH 8.0).
  • Mechanism of pH Sensitivity: Deviations from optimum pH alter the ionization states of acidic ($-COO^-$) and basic ($-NH_3^+$) amino acid side chains at the active site and substrate, preventing proper binding. Extreme acidic or alkaline pH causes protein denaturation.

Question 15
NCERT Exercise
What are nucleic acids? Mention their components and functions.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition of Nucleic Acids:
Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are linear unbranched biopolymers of nucleotides (polynucleotides) that serve as the universal repository, replicator, and translator of hereditary genetic information in living systems.

Three Fundamental Chemical Components of a Nucleotide:
  1. Nitrogenous Base:
    • Purines (double-ringed): Adenine (A) and Guanine (G).
    • Pyrimidines (single-ringed): Cytosine (C), Thymine (T) (in DNA only), and Uracil (U) (in RNA only).
  2. Pentose Sugar (5-Carbon):
    • $eta$-D-2-deoxyribose in DNA ($C_5H_{10}O_4$, lacking 2'-OH).
    • $eta$-D-ribose in RNA ($C_5H_{10}O_5$, with 2'-OH).
  3. Phosphate Group: Phosphoric acid ($H_3PO_4$) linking sugars via $3'-5'$ phosphodiester bonds.
Biological Functions of Nucleic Acids:
  • DNA: Stores long-term genetic instructions; directs cellular protein synthesis through transcription; ensures fidelity of inheritance via semi-conservative replication.
  • RNA: Directs protein synthesis via translation (mRNA templates, tRNA amino acid adapters, rRNA peptidyl transferase). Some RNAs act as catalytic ribozymes.
