Question 1
NCERT Exercise
What is the basis of classification of algae?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Scientific Basis of Classification of Algae into Three Major Classes:
Algae are classified into three primary classes—Chlorophyceae (green algae), Phaeophyceae (brown algae), and Rhodophyceae (red algae)—based primarily on the following fundamental criteria:

Classification Criteria Chlorophyceae (Green) Phaeophyceae (Brown) Rhodophyceae (Red)
1. Major Photosynthetic Pigments Chlorophyll $a$, $b$ Chlorophyll $a$, $c$, and xanthophyll carotenoid fucoxanthin Chlorophyll $a$, $d$, and phycobiliprotein r-phycoerythrin
2. Stored Food Material True Starch (stored in pyrenoids) Complex carbohydrates: Laminarin or Mannitol Floridean Starch (structurally similar to amylopectin and glycogen)
3. Cell Wall Composition Inner cellulose, outer pectose layer Cellulose covered externally by a gelatinous coating of algin Cellulose, pectin, and sulfated phycocolloids (agar, carrageen)
4. Flagellar Number & Position of Insertion 2–8, equal, apical 2, unequal, lateral Completely absent in all life stages

Question 2
NCERT Exercise
When and where does reduction division take place in the life cycle of a liverwort, a moss, a fern, a gymnosperm and an angiosperm?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Site and Timing of Reduction Division (Meiosis) Across Plant Groups:

1. Liverwort (e.g., Marchantia):
  • Where: Inside the capsule of the diploid sporophyte.
  • When: During sporogenesis, when diploid spore mother cells (SMCs) undergo meiosis to form haploid spores ($n$) which are dispersed with the help of hygroscopic elaters.
2. Moss (e.g., Funaria, Polytrichum):
  • Where: Inside the spore sac within the capsule region of the sporophyte (surrounding the columella).
  • When: During spore formation, spore mother cells undergo meiosis to yield haploid spores released via peristomial teeth.
3. Fern (e.g., Dryopteris, Pteris):
  • Where: Inside the sporangia borne in clusters called sori on the ventral surface of fertile leaves (sporophylls).
  • When: Diploid spore mother cells undergo meiosis to produce haploid spores, which germinate into the independent monoecious gametophyte (prothallus).
4. Gymnosperm (e.g., Pinus, Cycas):
  • Where & When: Occurs in two distinct sporangia:
    • Microsporangia (pollen sacs): Diploid microspore mother cells undergo meiosis to form haploid microspores (pollen grains).
    • Megasporangia (nucellus of ovule): A single diploid megaspore mother cell (MMC) undergoes meiosis to produce a linear tetrad of 4 haploid megaspores, of which 3 degenerate and 1 forms the functional female gametophyte with archegonia.
5. Angiosperm (Flowering Plants):
  • Where & When:
    • Anther (Microsporangium): Microspore mother cells (pollen mother cells / PMC) undergo meiosis to generate haploid microspores (pollen grains).
    • Ovule (Megasporangium): Megaspore mother cell inside the nucellus undergoes meiosis to produce 4 haploid megaspores (one functional megaspore forms the 7-celled, 8-nucleate female embryo sac).

Question 3
NCERT Exercise
Name three groups of plants that bear archegonia. Briefly describe the life cycle of any one of them.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Three Groups of Plants Bearing Archegonia (Archegoniatae):
The female sex organ called the archegonium (flask-shaped multicellular organ consisting of a neck and a venter enclosing an egg cell) is present in:
  1. Bryophyta (Liverworts and Mosses)
  2. Pteridophyta (Ferns, Horsetails, Club mosses)
  3. Gymnosperms (Conifers, Cycads, Ginkgo)
(Note: Archegonia are completely absent in Angiosperms, where they are replaced by the reduced embryo sac).

Brief Life Cycle of Bryophyta (e.g., Funaria / Moss):
  • Dominant Phase (Gametophyte - n): The conspicuous, free-living, independent phase is the haploid gametophyte consisting of leafy shoots anchored to soil by multicellular rhizoids.
  • Sex Organs: Formed at the apices of leafy stems:
    • Male organ: Antheridium (club-shaped, producing biflagellated, motile antherozoids).
    • Female organ: Archegonium (flask-shaped, with neck canal cells and a venter containing a non-motile egg).
  • Fertilization (Zooidogamy): Water is essential for reproduction. Antherozoids swim through a thin film of water towards the chemotactic stimulus of the archegonium. Syngamy produces a diploid zygote ($2n$).
  • Dependent Sporophyte Phase (2n): The zygote does not undergo immediate meiosis; it develops into a multicellular diploid sporophyte consisting of a foot, seta, and capsule, which remains permanently attached to and nutritionally dependent on the green gametophyte.
  • Spore Dispersal & Germination: Spore mother cells in the capsule undergo meiosis to form haploid spores. Spores germinate into a juvenile creeping, green, filamentous stage called the protonema, which later develops leafy gametophore buds.

Mention the ploidy of the following: protonemal cell of a moss; primary endosperm nucleus in dicot, leaf cell of a moss; prothallus cell of a fern; gemma cell in Marchantia; meristem cell of monocot, ovum of a liverwort, and zygote of a fern.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Exact Ploidy Levels of the Given Plant Cells:

Cell / Structure Ploidy Level Biological Justification
1. Protonemal cell of a moss Haploid ($n$) Directly develops from the mitotic germination of a meiotically produced haploid spore.
2. Primary Endosperm Nucleus (PEN) in dicot Triploid ($3n$) Result of triple fusion: 1 haploid male gamete ($n$) + 2 haploid polar nuclei ($n+n$).
3. Leaf cell of a moss Haploid ($n$) Forms part of the main vegetative body of the gametophyte ($n$).
4. Prothallus cell of a fern Haploid ($n$) The prothallus is the independent gametophytic phase grown from a haploid spore.
5. Gemma cell in Marchantia Haploid ($n$) Asexual vegetative propagule produced on the haploid gametophytic thallus.
6. Meristem cell of monocot Diploid ($2n$) Actively dividing somatic vegetative tissue of the dominant sporophyte ($2n$).
7. Ovum (egg cell) of a liverwort Haploid ($n$) Female gamete produced by mitosis inside the haploid archegonium.
8. Zygote of a fern Diploid ($2n$) Formed by syngamy (fusion) of haploid antherozoid ($n$) and haploid ovum ($n$).

Question 5
NCERT Exercise
Write a note on economic importance of algae and gymnosperms.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
A. Economic Importance of Algae:
  • Carbon Fixation & Oxygen Production: Algae carry out at least 50% of the total carbon dioxide fixation on Earth via photosynthesis, serving as fundamental primary producers in aquatic food chains.
  • Direct Food Source: Over 70 species of marine algae are edible, notably Porphyra (Nori), Laminaria (Kombu), and Sargassum.
  • Hydrocolloids (Phycocolloids):
    • Agar-agar: Derived from red algae (Gelidium and Gracilaria); used worldwide to solidify bacteriological culture media and prepare jellies, ice creams, and desserts.
    • Algin: Hydrocolloid extracted from brown algae (e.g., Laminaria, Ascophyllum); used as a stabilizer, thickener in foods, paints, and dental impressions.
    • Carrageenan: Extracted from red alga Chondrus crispus; used as a clearing and emulsifying agent.
  • Single Cell Protein (SCP) & Space Food: Unicellular green alga Chlorella and cyanobacterium Spirulina are exceptionally rich in proteins and vitamins, used as nutritional supplements and food for space travelers.
B. Economic Importance of Gymnosperms:
  • Timber and Softwood: Conifers such as Pinus, Cedrus deodara, Picea (Spruce), and Abies (Fir) yield premium softwood lumber for heavy construction, railway sleepers, furniture, and packing cases.
  • Paper Pulp: Conifer wood fibers are extensively digested to manufacture high-grade paper, newsprint, and rayon.
  • Resins & Turpentine: Pinus roxburghii yields oleoresin, which is distilled to produce turpentine oil (used in paints and varnishes) and rosin (used in sealing wax, waterproofing, and violin bows).
  • Medicinal Products:
    • Taxol: Highly potent anticancer drug extracted from the bark of Taxus brevifolia (Yew).
    • Ephedrine: Alkaloid extracted from Ephedra, used in treating asthma and respiratory bronchial spasms.
  • Edible Seeds (Chilgoza): Seeds of Pinus gerardiana (Chilgoza) are rich in fats and eaten as roasted dry fruits.

Question 6
NCERT Exercise
Both gymnosperms and angiosperms bear seeds, then why are they classified separately?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Scientific Rationale for Classifying Gymnosperms and Angiosperms Separately:
Although both Gymnosperms and Angiosperms belong to the overarching division Spermatophyta (seed-bearing plants), they diverge fundamentally in crucial morphological and reproductive features:

Distinguishing Feature Gymnosperms (gymnos = naked; sperma = seed) Angiosperms (angeion = vessel; sperma = seed)
1. Ovule & Seed Enclosure Ovules are naked, borne directly exposed on megasporophyll surfaces before and after fertilization. Ovules are completely enclosed inside the protective cavity of an ovary (carpel).
2. Fruit Formation No ovary exists; hence, no true fruits are ever formed. Seeds remain naked on cone scales. Post-fertilization, the ovary matures into a true fruit containing enclosed seeds.
3. Reproductive Organs Reproduce via distinct unisexual strobili or cones (microsporangiate and megasporangiate cones). Reproduce via specialized bisexual or unisexual flowers with perianth/calyx/corolla.
4. Double Fertilization Absent. Endosperm is pre-fertilization tissue and haploid ($n$). Universal hallmark: Double fertilization occurs; endosperm is post-fertilization triploid ($3n$).
5. Vascular Elements Xylem lacks true vessels (only tracheids; except Gnetales); phloem lacks companion cells (possesses albuminous cells). Xylem contains true vessels (tracheae); phloem possesses sieve tubes with companion cells.

Question 7
NCERT Exercise
What is heterospory? Briefly comment on its significance. Give two examples.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition of Heterospory:
Heterospory is the phenomenon of producing two distinctly different morphological and functional types of spores by the same plant sporophyte:
  1. Microspores: Smaller in size, produced in microsporangia, and germinate to produce the reduced male gametophyte.
  2. Megaspores: Much larger in size, produced in megasporangia, and germinate to produce the nutrient-rich female gametophyte.
Biological & Evolutionary Significance (Precursor to the Seed Habit):
Heterospory is considered one of the greatest milestones in plant evolution and directly paved the way for the origin of the seed habit in higher plants:
  • Endosporic Development & Retention: In heterosporous pteridophytes, the megaspore is not immediately shed; it is retained and germinates inside the parent megasporangium, shielded from environmental stresses.
  • Abundant Maternal Nutrition: The large megaspore accumulates rich food reserves, nourishing the developing embryo within the female gametophyte.
  • Development of Zygote inside Female Gametophyte: The fertilized zygote develops into a young embryo while still residing on the parent sporophyte. This retention of the developing embryo inside the megasporangium on the maternal plant is the critical evolutionary precursor to the seed habit seen in gymnosperms and angiosperms.
Two Examples of Heterosporous Pteridophytes:
1. Selaginella (Spike moss)
2. Salvinia (Aquatic water fern) • (Also Marsilea, Isoetes).

Question 8
NCERT Exercise
Explain briefly the following terms with suitable examples: (i) protonema (ii) antheridium (iii) archegonium (iv) diplontic (v) sporophyll (vi) isogamy.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Concise Explanation of Botanical Terms:

(i) Protonema:
The juvenile, creeping, green, branched, frequently filamentous haploid gametophytic stage in the life cycle of mosses, formed directly by the mitotic germination of a spore. It reproduces vegetatively by fragmentation and develops lateral buds that grow into leafy gametophores. Example: Funaria, Sphagnum.

(ii) Antheridium:
The multicellular, club-shaped or spherical male sex organ in cryptogams (algae, bryophytes, pteridophytes) surrounded by a sterile jacket of cells that produces flagellated, motile male gametes called antherozoids. Example: Marchantia, Dryopteris.

(iii) Archegonium:
The multicellular, flask-shaped female sex organ found in bryophytes, pteridophytes, and gymnosperms. It consists of a slender upper neck (containing neck canal cells) and a swollen lower venter containing a single non-motile egg (ovum). Example: Riccia, Pinus.

(iv) Diplontic Life Cycle:
A life cycle pattern wherein the dominant, free-living, photosynthetic phase of the plant is the diploid sporophyte ($2n$). The gametophytic phase is represented only by single to few-celled haploid gametes produced directly by meiosis. Example: Fucus (brown alga), and all Gymnosperms and Angiosperms.

(v) Sporophyll:
A specialized leaf or leaf-like laminar organ that bears sporangia on its surface or in its axils. In many pteridophytes and gymnosperms, sporophylls aggregate compactly to form strobili or cones. Example: Fertile fronds of Dryopteris, Microsporophylls of Cycas.

(vi) Isogamy:
A primitive mode of sexual reproduction characterized by the fusion of two morphologically similar and physiologically compatible gametes of equal size and shape (either both flagellated/motile or both non-motile). Example: Chlamydomonas debaryanum, Ulothrix (flagellated motile), and Spirogyra (non-motile).

Question 11
NCERT Exercise
Describe the important characteristics of gymnosperms.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Comprehensive Anatomical, Morphological & Reproductive Features of Gymnosperms:
Gymnosperms (from Greek gymnos = naked; sperma = seed) are seed-bearing vascular plants characterized by the following diagnostic features:

1. Naked Ovules and Absence of Fruit:
  • The ovules are not enclosed by an ovary wall; they remain freely exposed on the surface of megasporophylls before and after fertilization. Consequently, seeds are naked, and no true fruits are produced.
2. Habit and Morphological Adaptations (Xerophytic Nature):
  • Include medium-sized trees to colossal tall trees (e.g., giant red-wood tree Sequoia sempervirens, one of the tallest living trees) and shrubs.
  • Possess a well-developed tap root system. In some genera, roots exhibit specialized symbiotic associations:
    • Pinus: Roots form obligate fungal associations called mycorrhizae.
    • Cycas: Specialized apogeotropic coralloid roots harbor symbiotic nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria (Anabaena cycadae, Nostoc).
  • Leaves are xerophytically adapted to withstand extreme temperature, wind, and water stress: needle-like leaves (reducing surface area), thick cuticle, and deeply sunken stomata.
3. Vascular Tissue Architecture:
  • Xylem consists primarily of tracheids with bordered pits (true xylem vessels are absent, except in advanced order Gnetales: Gnetum, Ephedra, Welwitschia).
  • Phloem contains sieve cells and albuminous cells (lacks true sieve tubes and companion cells).
4. Heterospory and Cone (Strobilus) Formation:
  • All gymnosperms are strictly heterosporous, producing haploid microspores and megaspores.
  • Sporophylls are spirally arranged along an axis to form compact strobili or cones:
    • Male strobili (pollen cones): Bear microsporophylls with microsporangia where pollen grains develop. The pollen grain represents the highly reduced male gametophyte.
    • Female strobili (seed cones): Bear megasporophylls with megasporangia (ovules).
5. Fertilization and Embryogeny:
  • Pollination is strictly anemophilous (wind-pollinated). Pollen grains reach the micropyle of ovules directly.
  • Fertilization occurs via a pollen tube carrying non-motile male gametes directly to the archegonia in the ovule (siphonogamy).
  • Endosperm is formed prior to fertilization from the female gametophyte and is therefore permanently haploid ($n$).
