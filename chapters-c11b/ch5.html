Morphology of Flowering Plants

NCERT Rationalised 2026–27 Syllabus • All 14 Verbatim Questions with Official Step-by-Step Marking Rubrics

Floral Architecture & Placentation Patterns in Angiosperms
NCERT Textbook Exercise 1 to 14: Roots, Stems, Leaves, Inflorescence, Flowers, Placentation & Floral Formulas
Question 1
NCERT Exercise
What is meant by modification of root? What type of modification of root is found in the: (a) Banyan tree (b) Turnip (c) Mangrove trees?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition of Root Modification:
Root modification refers to permanent morphological, anatomical, and structural changes undergone by roots to perform specialized functions other than their normal physiological roles of water/mineral absorption and anchorage (such as mechanical support, food storage, and respiration).

Modifications in the Specified Plants:
  • (a) Banyan tree (Ficus benghalensis) — Prop Roots (Pillar Roots):
    Massive, adventitious aerial roots arise from heavy horizontal branches and grow vertically downward into the soil. Upon entering the soil, they become thick, woody, and pillar-like, providing mechanical support to the colossal canopy.
  • (b) Turnip (Brassica rapa) — Napiform Tap Root:
    The primary tap root becomes spherical and swollen with stored carbohydrates (starch), narrowing abruptly at the lower tip into a slender tail.
  • (c) Mangrove trees (e.g., Rhizophora) — Pneumatophores (Respiratory Roots):
    In waterlogged, saline, oxygen-deficient coastal swamp soils, subterranean roots produce negatively geotropic, erect peg-like vertical aerial roots that emerge above water. They bear numerous aerating pores called lenticels (pneumathodes) to intake oxygen for underground tissue respiration.

Question 2
NCERT Exercise
Justify the following statements on the basis of external features: (a) Underground parts of a plant are not always roots (b) Flower is a modified shoot.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
(a) Justification: Underground parts of a plant are not always roots:
Roots lack nodes, internodes, leaves, and buds. In contrast, several underground plant structures are morphologically modified stems because they exhibit diagnostic stem characteristics:
  • Potato Tuber: Bears "eyes", which are axillary buds situated in the axils of scale-leaf scars at definite nodes.
  • Ginger and Turmeric Rhizome: Horizontally growing underground stems with distinct nodes, internodes, scaly leaves, and terminal buds.
  • Colocasia and Corm of Amorphophallus: Vertically condensed underground stems with circular nodes and axillary buds.
  • Onion Bulb: Highly reduced disc-like stem bearing fleshy concentric scale leaves storing food.
(b) Justification: Flower is a modified shoot:
Goethe first proposed that a flower is an indeterminate vegetative shoot condensed and modified for sexual reproduction:
  • Condensation of Axis: The apical meristem transforms into a floral meristem; internodes do not elongate, condensing the floral axis into a swollen thalamus (receptacle).
  • Homology with Leaves: Floral whorls (sepals, petals, stamens, and carpels) are borne successively at swollen nodes like modified leaves (phylls): sepals ≈ microphylls, petals ≈ leaves, stamens ≈ microsporophylls, carpels ≈ megasporophylls.
  • Axillary Position & Reversion: Flowers arise in the axils of bracts (modified leaves), precisely where vegetative axillary branches normally emerge. In abnormal conditions (e.g., in Cleome or Rosa), floral axes occasionally elongate between whorls (anthophore, androphore, gynophore).

(a) Clear evidence of stem features (nodes, internodes, buds, scale leaves) in tubers/rhizomes2 Marks
(b) Explanation of condensed axis (thalamus) and floral organs as homologous modified leaves2 Marks
Question 3
NCERT Exercise
How is a pinnately compound leaf different from a palmately compound leaf?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Differences Between Pinnately and Palmately Compound Leaves:

Feature Pinnately Compound Leaf Palmately Compound Leaf
1. Arrangement of Leaflets Leaflets (pinnae) are attached laterally along an elongated common central axis called the rachis (which represents the extended midrib). Leaflets are articulated and radiate out from a single common point at the tip of the petiole (like fingers from a palm).
2. Presence of Rachis Rachis is present, prominent, and bears leaflets at intervals. Rachis is completely absent or extremely reduced.
3. Subdivisions Can be unipinnate (paripinnate/imparipinnate), bipinnate, tripinnate, or decompound. Classified based on leaflet number: unifoliolate, bifoliolate, trifoliolate, quadrifoliolate, or multifoliolate.
4. Representative Examples Neem (Azadirachta indica), Rose, Cassia, Tamarind, Gulmohar. Silk cotton (Bombax ceiba), Citrus, Oxalis, Marsilea.

Question 4
NCERT Exercise
Explain with suitable examples the different types of phyllotaxy.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition of Phyllotaxy:
Phyllotaxy is the pattern of arrangement of leaves on the stem or branch. Its primary adaptive objective is to position leaf blades in a non-overlapping configuration to maximize sunlight interception.

Three Major Types of Phyllotaxy:

1. Alternate (Spiral) Phyllotaxy:
  • A single leaf arises at each node in an alternate and spiral fashion along the stem axis.
  • Examples: China rose (Hibiscus rosa-sinensis), Mustard (Brassica campestris), and Sunflower (Helianthus annuus).
2. Opposite Phyllotaxy:
  • A pair of leaves arises at each node on opposite sides facing each other. Subtypes:
    • Opposite decussate: Successive pairs lie at right angles to each other (e.g., Calotropis, Ocimum / Holy Basil).
    • Opposite superposed: Successive pairs lie in the exact same plane (e.g., Guava / Psidium guajava, Syzygium).
3. Whorled Phyllotaxy:
  • More than two leaves arise at each node to form a circular ring or whorl around the stem.
  • Examples: Alstonia (Devil's tree), Nerium (Oleander).
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Definition of phyllotaxy and adaptive purpose0.5 Mark

Question 5
NCERT Exercise
Define the following terms: (a) aestivation (b) placentation (c) actinomorphic (d) zygomorphic (e) superior ovary (f) perigynous flower (g) epipetalous stamen.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Precise Botanical Definitions of Floral Terms:

(a) Aestivation:
The mode of arrangement of accessory floral leaves (sepals or petals) in a floral bud with respect to other members of the same whorl. Major types: Valvate, Twisted, Imbricate, and Vexillary.

(b) Placentation:
The pattern of distribution and attachment of ovules upon the fertile tissue called placenta within the ovary of a flower. Types: Marginal, Axile, Parietal, Free-central, and Basal.

(c) Actinomorphic (Radial Symmetry):
A flower that can be divided into two equal radial halves in any vertical plane passing through the central axis (represented by $\oplus$). Examples: Mustard, Datura, Chilli.

(d) Zygomorphic (Bilateral Symmetry):
A flower that can be divided into two similar halves in only one vertical plane (represented by $%$). Examples: Pea, Gulmohar, Bean, Cassia.

(e) Superior Ovary (Hypogynous Flower):
An ovary that occupies the highest apical position on the convex thalamus, while sepals, petals, and stamens are inserted below it. Symbolized as $\underline{\text{G}}$. Examples: Mustard, China rose, Brinjal.

(f) Perigynous Flower:
A flower in which the gynoecium is situated at the center, and other floral parts are located on the rim or margin of a saucer- or cup-shaped thalamus at approximately the same level. The ovary is termed half-inferior. Examples: Plum, Rose, Peach.

(g) Epipetalous Stamen:
A condition in which the filaments of stamens are fused or attached to the inner face of the corolla petals (represented by $\overgroup{\text{C}\quad\text{A}}$). Example: Brinjal (Solanaceae), Datura.

Question 7
NCERT Exercise
Draw the labelled diagram of the following: (i) gram seed (ii) V.S. of maize grain.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Morphological and Anatomical Architecture of Seeds:

(i) Non-Endospermic Dicot Seed — Gram Seed (Cicer arietinum):
  • Seed Coat: Double-layered; outer thick brownish testa and inner thin membranous tegmen.
  • Hilum & Micropyle: Hilum is the scar of stalk attachment; just above it lies a minute pore, the micropyle (for water/oxygen entry).
  • Embryo: Consists of two massive, fleshy, starch-rich cotyledons enclosing an embryonal axis (tigellum).
    • Upper conical tip: Plumule (future shoot).
    • Lower pointed tip: Radicle (future root).
    • Endosperm: Completely absorbed during embryogeny (exalbuminous / non-endospermic).
(ii) Endospermic Monocot Seed — V.S. of Maize Grain (Zea mays):
  • Fused Pericarp & Testa: Single-seeded fruit (caryopsis) where fruit wall is fused with seed coat.
  • Aleurone Layer: A specialized protein-rich outer layer separating the endosperm from the embryo.
  • Endosperm: Large, bulky tissue storing starch, occupying two-thirds of the grain volume.
  • Embryo: Shield-shaped single cotyledon called the scutellum positioned laterally against the endosperm.
    • Coleoptile: Protective hollow sheath enclosing the plumule.
    • Coleorhiza: Protective parenchymatous sheath enclosing the radicle and root cap.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
(i) Gram seed components: testa, tegmen, hilum, micropyle, 2 cotyledons, radicle, plumule1.5 Marks
(ii) Maize grain: fused pericarp, aleurone layer, endosperm, scutellum, coleoptile, coleorhiza1.5 Marks
Question 8
NCERT Exercise
Describe modifications of stem with suitable examples.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Diverse Morphological Modifications of Stem:

1. Underground Modifications (Storage & Perennation):
  • Tuber: Swollen terminal ends of underground branches storing starch, bearing buds/eyes. Example: Potato (Solanum tuberosum).
  • Rhizome: Fleshy, prostrate dorsiventral horizontal stem with distinct nodes, internodes, and scaly leaves. Example: Ginger (Zingiber officinale), Turmeric.
  • Corm: Unbranched, stout, vertically growing condensed underground stem. Example: Colocasia, Amorphophallus (Zamikand).
  • Bulb: Highly reduced discoid stem bearing fleshy scale leaves. Example: Onion (Allium cepa), Garlic.
2. Sub-Aerial Modifications (Vegetative Propagation):
  • Runner: Creeping prostrate branch rooting at nodes. Example: Grass (Cynodon dactylon), Oxalis.
  • Stolon: Slender lateral branch arching downward to touch the ground. Example: Mint (Mentha), Jasmine.
  • Offset: Short, thick lateral branch of one internode length with a rosette of leaves and a tuft of roots. Example: Pistia, Eichhornia (Water hyacinth).
  • Sucker: Arises from underground stem, grows horizontally, then emerges obliquely upward. Example: Chrysanthemum, Banana, Pineapple.
3. Aerial Modifications (Specialised Adaptive Functions):
  • Stem Tendrils: Axillary buds modified into slender spirally coiled climbing structures. Example: Gourds (cucumber, pumpkin, watermelon) and Grapevine.
  • Thorns: Axillary buds modified into woody, straight, pointed protective organs preventing grazing. Example: Citrus, Bougainvillea.
  • Phylloclades: Green, flattened or cylindrical succulent stems taking over photosynthesis and storing water in xerophytes. Example: Opuntia (flattened), Euphorbia (cylindrical).

Question 9
NCERT Exercise
Take one flower each of the families Fabaceae and Solanaceae and write its semi-technical description. Also give their floral formula.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Family Fabaceae (e.g., Pisum sativum — Pea Flower):
  • Inflorescence: Racemose.
  • Flower: Bisexual, zygomorphic, hypogynous, bracteate, pentamerous.
  • Calyx: Sepals 5, gamosepalous (fused), valvate or imbricate aestivation.
  • Corolla: Petals 5, polypetalous, papilionaceous consisting of a large posterior standard (vexillum), two lateral wings (alae), and two anterior fused petals forming a keel (carina); vexillary aestivation.
  • Androecium: Stamens 10, diadelphous [9 + 1] (9 filaments fused into a tube, 1 free posterior stamen), dithecous anthers.
  • Gynoecium: Monocarpellary, superior unilocular ovary with marginal placentation, single style.
Floral Formula of Fabaceae:
$$\% \; \text{ ⚥ } \; \text{K}_{(5)} \; \text{C}_{1+2+(2)} \; \text{A}_{(9)+1} \; \underline{\text{G}}_{1}$$
2. Family Solanaceae (e.g., Solanum nigrum — Makoi / Black Nightshade):
  • Inflorescence: Solitary, axillary or cymose (as in Solanum).
  • Flower: Bisexual, actinomorphic, hypogynous, ebracteate, pentamerous.
  • Calyx: Sepals 5, gamosepalous, persistent, valvate aestivation.
  • Corolla: Petals 5, gamopetalous, rotate or infundibuliform, valvate aestivation.
  • Androecium: Stamens 5, epipetalous (attached to corolla tube), alternating with petals, free (polyandrous), anthers dithecous.
  • Gynoecium: Bicarpellary, syncarpous, superior ovary, bilocular, obliquely placed ovary with swollen placenta bearing numerous ovules with axile placentation.
Floral Formula of Solanaceae:
$$\oplus \; \text{ ⚥ } \; \text{K}_{(5)} \; \overgroup{\text{C}_{(5)} \quad \text{A}_{5}} \; \underline{\text{G}}_{(2)}$$

Question 10
NCERT Exercise
Describe the various types of placentations found in flowering plants.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Detailed Description of the Five Primary Types of Placentation:

1. Marginal Placentation:
The placenta forms a continuous ridge along the ventral suture of a monocarpellary unilocular ovary, and ovules are borne in two alternating rows along the ridge. Example: Pea (Pisum), Gram, Bean.

2. Axile Placentation:
The ovary is multicarpellary, syncarpous, and partitioned into two or more chambers (multilocular) by radial septa. The walls of carpels meet at the central axis where the placenta forms, bearing ovules in each locule. Examples: Tomato, China rose, Lemon, Orange.

3. Parietal Placentation:
The ovules develop on the inner peripheral wall of a syncarpous, unilocular ovary. In some cases (such as Brassicaceae), the ovary becomes falsely two-chambered due to the development of a false septum called the replum. Examples: Mustard, Argemone.

4. Free Central Placentation:
The ovules are borne on a distinct central axis column in a unilocular ovary, and septa are completely absent. Examples: Dianthus, Primrose (Primula).

5. Basal Placentation:
The placenta develops directly at the base of the ovary and bears a single solitary ovule. Examples: Sunflower (Helianthus), Marigold (Tagetes).

Question 11
NCERT Exercise
What is a flower? Describe the parts of a typical angiosperm flower.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition of a Flower:
A flower is a specialized, condensed reproductive shoot of angiosperms, bearing modified leaves adapted for sexual reproduction and the subsequent production of seeds and fruits.

Four Concentric Whorls of a Typical Flower (from Outermost to Innermost):

1. Calyx (Outermost Non-Essential Whorl):
  • Composed of individual units called sepals, which are typically green and leaf-like.
  • Protects the delicate internal floral organs in the bud condition.
2. Corolla (Second Non-Essential Whorl):
  • Composed of individual units called petals, brightly colored and often fragrant.
  • Attracts pollinating agents (insects, birds) and shields sexual organs.
3. Androecium (Male Reproductive Whorl):
  • Composed of stamens (microsporophylls). Each stamen consists of a slender stalk (filament) and a terminal bilobed anther.
  • Each anther lobe contains two pollen sacs (microsporangia) producing haploid pollen grains.
4. Gynoecium / Pistil (Female Reproductive Whorl):
  • Composed of one or more carpels (megasporophylls). Each carpel consists of three parts:
    • Stigma: Sticky receptive terminal landing platform for pollen grains.
    • Style: Slender elongated neck through which the pollen tube travels.
    • Ovary: Swollen basal portion containing one or more ovules attached to the placenta.

Question 12
NCERT Exercise
How do the various leaf modifications help plants?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Adaptive Value of Diverse Leaf Modifications:
Leaves frequently undergo structural modifications beyond photosynthesis to aid survival:

1. Leaf Tendrils (Climbing Support):
In weak-stemmed plants, entire leaves or leaflets modify into slender, sensitive, coiling filaments that twine around supports to elevate the photosynthetic foliage towards light. Example: Wild pea (Lathyrus), Garden pea (Pisum sativum).

2. Leaf Spines (Defense & Xerophytic Water Conservation):
In arid xerophytes, leaves are reduced to sharp, stiff spines. This dramatically minimizes surface area for transpiration and protects succulents from herbivorous predators. Example: Opuntia, Cactus, Aloe.

3. Fleshy Storage Leaves (Food & Water Reserves):
Fleshy scale leaves store reserves of water, mucilage, and carbohydrates. Example: Onion and Garlic bulbs, Bryophyllum.

4. Phyllodes (Photosynthesis with Reduced Transpiration):
In Acacia auriculiformis (Australian acacia), the bipinnate lamina sheds early. The petiole flattens into a sickle-shaped green blade called a phyllode, which synthesizes food while losing minimal water through transpiration.

5. Insect Traps (Carnivorous Nitrogen Supplementation):
Leaves modify into pitchers (Nepenthes), bladders (Utricularia), or snapping traps (Dionaea) to capture insects and digest them to obtain nitrogenous compounds in nutrient-poor bogs.

Question 13
NCERT Exercise
Define the term inflorescence. Explain the basis for the different types of inflorescence in flowering plants.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition of Inflorescence:
An inflorescence is the natural arrangement and mode of distribution of a cluster of flowers on the floral axis (called the peduncle or mother axis) of a plant.

Basis of Classification into Major Types:
Inflorescences are classified primarily on whether the main growing apex continues to grow indefinitely or terminates in the formation of a flower:

1. Racemose Inflorescence (Indeterminate / Indefinite Growth):
  • The main axis (peduncle) continues to grow continuously by its apical bud and does not terminate in a flower.
  • Flowers are produced laterally in acropetal succession (oldest flowers at the base, youngest buds towards the growing apex).
  • In flat-topped inflorescences, the opening of flowers is centripetal (from periphery towards center).
2. Cymose Inflorescence (Determinate / Definite Growth):
  • The apical bud of the main axis terminates in a flower, arresting further vertical growth of the peduncle.
  • Further growth continues via lateral branches arising below the terminal flower, each also terminating in a flower.
  • Flowers are arranged in basipetal succession (terminal apical flower is the oldest, youngest flowers are towards the base).
  • In flat-topped cymes, flower opening is centrifugal (from center towards periphery).

Question 14
NCERT Exercise
Write the floral formula of an actinomorphic, bisexual, hypogynous flower with five united sepals, five free petals, five free stamens and two united carpels with superior ovary and axile placentation.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Step-by-Step Derivation of the Floral Formula:

  • 1. Actinomorphic (Radial Symmetry): Represented by the symbol — $\oplus$
  • 2. Bisexual (Hermaphrodite / Perfect Flower): Represented by — $\text{⚥}$
  • 3. Calyx: 5 united sepals (gamosepalous) — $\text{K}_{(5)}$
  • 4. Corolla: 5 free petals (polypetalous) — $\text{C}_{5}$
  • 5. Androecium: 5 free stamens (polyandrous, not epipetalous) — $\text{A}_{5}$
  • 6. Gynoecium: Bicarpellary (2), united carpels (syncarpous — enclosed in brackets), superior ovary (hypogynous — line under G) — $\underline{\text{G}}_{(2)}$

Final Accurate Floral Formula:
$$\oplus \; \text{ ⚥ } \; \text{K}_{(5)} \; \text{C}_{5} \; \text{A}_{5} \; \underline{\text{G}}_{(2)}$$
