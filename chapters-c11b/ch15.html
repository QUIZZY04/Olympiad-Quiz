Question 1
NCERT Exercise
Name the components of the formed elements in the erythrocyte, leukocyte and thrombocytes.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Detailed Components of Formed Elements of Blood (45% of Total Blood Volume):

1. Erythrocytes (Red Blood Cells — RBCs):
  • Count: 5.0 to 5.5 million per $\text{mm}^3$ of blood.
  • Components: Biconcave circular enucleate discs packed with the iron-bearing chromoprotein Hemoglobin ($12–16\text{ g/100 mL}$), carbonic anhydrase enzyme, plasma membrane with surface blood group antigens (A, B, Rh).
2. Leukocytes (White Blood Cells — WBCs):
  • Count: 6,000 to 8,000 per $\text{mm}^3$ of blood. Nucleated defensive cells categorized into:
    • Granulocytes:
      • Neutrophils (60–65%): Multilobed nucleus; phagocytic engulfment of bacteria.
      • Eosinophils (2–3%): Bilobed nucleus; defense against parasites, allergic reactions.
      • Basophils (0.5–1%): S-shaped nucleus; secretes histamine, serotonin, and heparin.
    • Agranulocytes:
      • Lymphocytes (20–25%): B-lymphocytes (produce antibodies) and T-lymphocytes (cell-mediated immunity).
      • Monocytes (6–8%): Kidney-shaped nucleus; transform into phagocytic tissue macrophages.
3. Thrombocytes (Blood Platelets):
  • Count: 150,000 to 350,000 per $\text{mm}^3$. Non-nucleated cellular fragments derived from bone marrow megakaryocytes; contain thromboplastin, serotonin, and platelet factor 3 (PF3) essential for blood coagulation.

Question 2
NCERT Exercise
What is the importance of plasma proteins? (Albumin for osmotic balance, Globulins for antibodies/defense, Fibrinogen for blood clotting).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Physiological Importance of the Three Major Plasma Proteins (6–8% of Plasma):

1. Fibrinogen (~0.3 g/100 mL):
  • Synthesized exclusively by the liver; soluble high-molecular weight glycoprotein precursor.
  • Role: Essential for blood coagulation (hemostasis). In the clotting cascade, thrombin cleaves fibrinogen into insoluble, sticky fibrin threads that form a mesh trapping RBCs to seal injured blood vessels and stop hemorrhage.
2. Globulins (~2.0 to 2.5 g/100 mL):
  • Subdivided into $alpha$, $eta$, and $gamma$ (gamma) globulins:
    • $gamma$-Globulins (Immunoglobulins / Antibodies): Synthesized by plasma B-lymphocytes; act as the primary defense mechanism against bacterial and viral pathogens.
    • $alpha$ and $eta$-Globulins: Transport lipid hormones, iron (transferrin), and copper (ceruloplasmin).
3. Albumins (~4.0 to 4.5 g/100 mL — Most Abundant):
  • Smallest plasma proteins synthesized by liver.
  • Role: Responsible for maintaining blood colloidal osmotic (oncotic) pressure (~25 to 28 mm Hg). This pressure pulls water from interstitial spaces back into capillary lumen, preventing tissue edema. Also transports bilirubin and free fatty acids.

Question 4
NCERT Exercise
Why do we consider blood as a connective tissue?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Scientific Justification for Classifying Blood as a Specialized Fluid Connective Tissue:

1. Common Embryological Origin:
Like all other connective tissues (bone, cartilage, areolar tissue, adipose), blood is derived embryologically from the embryonic mesoderm.

2. Structural Matrix Composition:
A classic histological definition of connective tissue requires widely spaced living cells suspended within an abundant non-living extracellular ground substance (matrix). In blood, formed elements (RBCs, WBCs, platelets) are suspended within a fluid matrix called plasma.

3. Physiological Connecting Function:
Connective tissues connect and integrate different organs and anatomical systems of the body. Blood continuously circulates through closed vessels, physically and physiologically linking every tissue: it transports oxygen from lungs to deep tissues, nutrients from intestines to liver and muscles, metabolic wastes from cells to kidneys, and endocrine hormones from ductless glands to distant target receptors.

Distinguishing Exception: Unlike proper connective tissues where fibers (collagen/elastin) are secreted by resident cells, blood plasma fibers are soluble precursors (fibrinogen) that polymerize into insoluble fibrin fibers only during clotting.

Question 5
NCERT Exercise
What is the difference between lymph and blood?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Differences Between Blood and Lymph:

Parameter Blood Lymph (Tissue Fluid / Interstitial Fluid)
1. Colour Bright red (oxygenated) or dark purplish-red (deoxygenated) due to hemoglobin. Colorless to pale straw-colored fluid (lacks hemoglobin).
2. Formed Elements Contains abundant RBCs, all classes of WBCs, and platelets. RBCs and platelets are completely absent. Contains predominantly lymphocytes.
3. Protein & Nutrient Content High protein concentration (6–8% plasma proteins including abundant albumin, fibrinogen). Significantly lower protein content and low calcium/phosphorus; higher in metabolic wastes.
4. Fat Content Normal soluble lipoprotein transport. Rich in emulsified dietary fats absorbed from intestinal villi via specialized lymph vessels (lacteals) as chylomicrons.
5. Circulation Direction Circular closed pathway: Heart → Arteries → Capillaries → Veins → Heart. Unidirectional flow: Tissues → Lymphatic vessels → Thoracic duct → Subclavian veins → Blood.

Question 6
NCERT Exercise
What is meant by double circulation? What is its significance?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Definition of Double Circulation:
Double circulation is a specialized circulatory system found in birds and mammals where blood passes through the four-chambered heart twice during one complete systemic cardiac cycle. The deoxygenated and oxygenated blood streams are completely separated by interatrial and interventricular septa, preventing any mixing.

The Two Distinct Circuits of Double Circulation:
  • A. Pulmonary Circulation:
    Deoxygenated blood from the Right Ventricle is pumped into the Pulmonary Artery → Pulmonary Capillaries in Lungs (releases $CO_2$, absorbs $O_2$) → Pulmonary Veins → delivers oxygenated blood to the Left Atrium.
  • B. Systemic Circulation:
    Oxygenated blood from the Left Ventricle is pumped under high pressure into the massive Aorta → Systemic Arteries → Capillaries of body tissues (delivers $O_2$, takes up $CO_2$) → Systemic Veins → Superior and Inferior Vena Cava → returns deoxygenated blood to the Right Atrium.
2. Biological Significance of Double Circulation:
  • Zero Mixing of Oxygenated and Deoxygenated Blood: Guarantees delivery of blood with maximum oxygen saturation to tissues.
  • Supports High Metabolic Demands of Homeothermy: Birds and mammals are warm-blooded (endothermic); maintaining constant elevated core body temperatures requires colossal cellular ATP production and intense oxygen delivery.
  • Differential Pressure Regulation: Allows pulmonary circuit to operate under safe low hydrostatic pressure (~25/10 mm Hg) to protect delicate alveolar membranes from pulmonary edema, while systemic circuit operates under high pressure (~120/80 mm Hg) to propel blood through extensive systemic vascular beds against gravity.

Question 7
NCERT Exercise
Write the differences between: (a) Blood and Lymph (b) Open and Closed system of circulation (c) Systole and Diastole (d) P-wave and T-wave.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
(a) Blood vs. Lymph:
Blood is red, contains RBCs, high protein content, and circulates in a closed circuit. Lymph is colorless, lacks RBCs, contains predominantly lymphocytes, carries dietary fats from lacteals, and flows unidirectionally from tissues to venous blood.

(b) Open vs. Closed System of Circulation:
TraitOpen Circulatory SystemClosed Circulatory System
VesselsBlood pumped by heart into large open spaces/sinuses (hemocoel). Capillaries absent.Blood flows strictly confined within continuous closed network of blood vessels (arteries, veins, capillaries).
Pressure & VelocityVery low pressure, sluggish flow; poor regulation of flow to specific organs.High hydrostatic pressure, rapid velocity; tightly regulated by vasomotor constriction.
ExamplesArthropods (cockroach, crab) and Non-cephalopod Molluscs (pila).Annelids (earthworm), Cephalopods (squid), and all Vertebrates.

(c) Systole vs. Diastole:
  • Systole: The phase of active muscular contraction of the heart chambers (atrial systole = 0.1 s; ventricular systole = 0.3 s), during which chamber volume decreases and blood is pumped out under high pressure.
  • Diastole: The phase of muscular relaxation and dilation of heart chambers (joint diastole = 0.4 s; ventricular diastole = 0.5 s), during which chambers refill with returning venous blood under low pressure.
(d) P-Wave vs. T-Wave of Electrocardiogram (ECG):
  • P-Wave: Small positive upward deflection representing atrial depolarisation, which leads directly to the mechanical contraction (systole) of both atria.
  • T-Wave: Broader, smooth positive upward deflection representing ventricular repolarisation (return of ventricles from excited to resting electrical state), marking the end of ventricular systole.

Question 8
NCERT Exercise
Describe the evolutionary change in the pattern of heart among the vertebrates.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Evolutionary Trajectory of the Vertebrate Heart (from Single to Double Circulation):

1. Two-Chambered Heart — Fishes (Class Pisces):
  • Consists of one atrium and one ventricle (plus accessory sinus venosus and conus arteriosus).
  • Single Circulation (Venous Heart): The heart receives and pumps only deoxygenated blood. Blood pumped by ventricle goes to gills for oxygenation and flows directly to systemic tissues before returning to atrium.
2. Three-Chambered Heart — Amphibians (Frogs, Salamanders):
  • Consists of two atria and a single undivided ventricle.
  • Left atrium receives oxygenated blood from lungs/skin; right atrium receives deoxygenated blood from body. Both atria empty into the single ventricle, where mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood occurs (incomplete double circulation).
3. Incomplete Four-Chambered Heart — Reptiles (Except Crocodiles):
  • Consists of two atria and a partially partitioned ventricle with an incomplete interventricular septum. Mixing of blood is reduced but still occurs (incomplete double circulation).
  • Crocodiles possess a fully partitioned four-chambered heart.
4. Complete Four-Chambered Heart — Birds (Aves) and Mammals:
  • Consists of two atria and two completely separated ventricles divided by interatrial and interventricular septa.
  • Complete Double Circulation: Complete anatomical separation of pulmonary and systemic circuits; zero mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood, fulfilling the high metabolic requirements of homeothermy.

Question 9
NCERT Exercise
Why do we call our heart myogenic?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Why the Human Heart is Designated as Myogenic:

1. Intrinsic Origin of Heartbeat in Muscle Cells (Myocytes):
In the human heart, the rhythmic electrical impulse for contraction (action potential) is generated intrinsically by specialized auto-rhythmic cardiac muscular tissue (nodal tissue), entirely independent of external nervous stimulation.
The primary site of origin is the Sino-Atrial (SA) node, which consists of modified, self-excitable neuromuscular cells situated in the upper wall of the right atrium.

2. Contrast with Neurogenic Hearts:
In contrast, neurogenic hearts (found in invertebrates like annelids and arthropods) require external rhythmic nerve impulses from motor nerve ganglia to initiate contraction. If the motor nerve is severed, a neurogenic heart stops beating immediately.

3. Experimental Proof of Myogenic Nature:
If a mammalian or human heart is completely excised from the body, severed from all cranial and spinal nerves, and placed in a warm, oxygenated Ringer-Locke nutrient perfusion solution, it continues to beat rhythmically and autonomously for hours.

Regulatory Note: Extrinsic autonomic nerves (sympathetic accelerator and parasympathetic vagus nerves) merely modulate the heart rate, but do NOT initiate the heartbeat.

Question 10
NCERT Exercise
Sino-atrial node is called the pacemaker of our heart. Why?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Why the Sino-Atrial (SA) Node is Designated as the Natural Pacemaker:

1. Anatomical Location:
The Sino-Atrial (SA) node is a crescent-shaped patch of specialized auto-rhythmic neuromuscular tissue located in the right upper corner of the wall of the right atrium, close to the opening of the superior vena cava.

2. Highest Intrinsic Rate of Action Potential Generation:
While all components of the nodal conduction system (SA node, AV node, Bundle of His, Purkinje fibers) possess inherent rhythmicity, the SA node exhibits the highest intrinsic frequency of spontaneous electrical depolarization:
  • SA Node: Generates 70 to 75 action potentials per minute (average ~72/min).
  • AV Node: Generates only ~40 to 60 action potentials per minute.
  • Purkinje Fibers: Generate only ~20 to 35 action potentials per minute.
3. Dominant Pacemaking Function:
Because the SA node fires fastest, its electrical wave of excitation spreads rapidly across atrial syncytial muscle fibers, depolarizing and capturing control of all subsidiary nodal tissues before they can reach their own spontaneous threshold. It thus sets and dictates the rhythmic pace of contraction for the entire heart, rightfully earning the title of the pacemaker.

Question 11
NCERT Exercise
What is the significance of atrio-ventricular node and atrio-ventricular bundle in the functioning of heart?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Functional Significance of the AV Node and AV Bundle (Bundle of His):

1. Significance of the Atrio-Ventricular (AV) Node:
Situated in the lower-left corner of the right atrium close to the atrio-ventricular septum.
  • AV Nodal Delay (~0.1 second): The electrical impulse conducted from the SA node across atrial walls is delayed by approximately 0.1 second (100 milliseconds) at the AV node due to thin, slow-conducting fibers.
  • Physiological Cruciality: This brief delay ensures that atria have completely finished their mechanical contraction (atrial systole) and emptied their full volume of blood into the relaxed ventricles before the massive ventricles are stimulated to contract. Without this delay, atria and ventricles would contract simultaneously, preventing proper ventricular filling.
  • Reserve Pacemaker: If the SA node fails, the AV node can take over as secondary pacemaker, generating 40–60 beats/min to sustain life.
2. Significance of the Atrio-Ventricular (AV) Bundle (Bundle of His & Purkinje Fibers):
The dense non-conducting fibrous atrio-ventricular ring acts as an electrical insulator between atria and ventricles.
  • The AV Bundle (Bundle of His) is the sole electrical bridge through which action potentials can cross from atria into the ventricles.
  • It bifurcates into Right and Left Bundle Branches, branching into colossal Purkinje fibers that conduct action potentials at an extraordinary velocity (~4 meters/second).
  • This ensures that both ventricles contract synchronously from the apex upward toward the base, efficiently squeezing blood upward into the aorta and pulmonary artery.

Question 12
NCERT Exercise
Define a cardiac cycle and the cardiac output.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Definition of Cardiac Cycle:
The cardiac cycle is the complete, orderly sequence of coordinated electrical, mechanical, pressure, and volume events that occur in the heart from the beginning of one heartbeat to the beginning of the next.
  • At a resting heart rate of 72 beats per minute, the total duration of a single cardiac cycle is: $$\text{Duration} = \frac{60\text{ seconds}}{72} = \mathbf{0.8\text{ seconds}}$$
  • Comprises: (i) Atrial systole (0.1 s), (ii) Ventricular systole (0.3 s), and (iii) Joint diastole (0.4 s).
2. Definition of Cardiac Output (CO):
Cardiac output is the total volume of blood pumped out by each ventricle per minute.
It is mathematically calculated as the product of the Stroke Volume (SV) and the Heart Rate (HR): $$\mathbf{\text{Cardiac Output (CO)} = \text{Stroke Volume (SV)} \times \text{Heart Rate (HR)}}$$
  • Stroke Volume: Volume of blood ejected by each ventricle during one beat (~70 mL).
  • Heart Rate: Number of heartbeats per minute (~72 beats/min).
$$\text{CO} = 70\text{ mL} \times 72\text{ beats/min} = 5040\text{ mL/min} \approx \mathbf{5.0 \text{ Litres/minute}}$$ Physiological Variability: In trained endurance athletes, cardiac output can increase four- to five-fold during intense exertion (up to 25–30 L/min) due to higher stroke volume.

Question 13
NCERT Exercise
Explain heart sounds. (Lubb: closure of AV tricuspid/bicuspid valves; Dubb: closure of semilunar valves).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Acoustic and Clinical Analysis of Heart Sounds:
During each cardiac cycle, two prominent clinical heart sounds are audible through a stethoscope placed on the precordium, produced by the vibration of heart walls and blood eddies associated with the sudden snapping closure of cardiac valves:

1. First Heart Sound — "Lubb" ($S_1$):
  • Cause: Produced by the simultaneous, sharp closure of the atrio-ventricular (AV) valves (the tricuspid valve on the right and bicuspid / mitral valve on the left) at the very onset of ventricular systole.
  • Acoustic Character: Relatively lower pitched, dull, booming, and longer in duration (~0.10 to 0.14 seconds).
  • Clinical Significance: Marks the beginning of ventricular contraction and prevents backflow of blood into atria.
2. Second Heart Sound — "Dubb" ($S_2$):
  • Cause: Produced by the abrupt, synchronized closure of the semilunar valves (aortic and pulmonary semilunar valves) at the onset of ventricular diastole.
  • Acoustic Character: Sharper, higher pitched, snapping, and shorter in duration (~0.08 to 0.10 seconds).
  • Clinical Significance: Marks the beginning of ventricular relaxation and prevents backflow of blood from aorta and pulmonary trunk into ventricles.
Clinical Pathology: Defective, leaking, or stenosed valves generate abnormal murmurs / hissing sounds.

Question 14
NCERT Exercise
Draw a standard ECG and explain the different segments. (P wave: atrial depolarisation; QRS complex: ventricular depolarisation; T wave: ventricular repolarisation).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Clinical Interpretation of Standard Electrocardiogram (ECG) Deflections:
An Electrocardiogram (ECG) is a graphic recording of the electrical potentials generated by the heart muscle during each cardiac cycle, recorded using standard limb electrodes:

ECG Waveform Electrical Event Represented Mechanical Cardiac Correlation
1. P-Wave Atrial Depolarisation Spread of excitation from SA node over both atria, leading to mechanical atrial contraction (systole).
2. QRS Complex Ventricular Depolarisation Rapid spread of impulse through Bundle of His and Purkinje fibers; initiates ventricular contraction (systole) just after Q-wave. The number of QRS complexes per minute determines heart rate.
3. T-Wave Ventricular Repolarisation Return of ventricles from excited to resting state; marks the end of ventricular systole and entry into ventricular diastole.

Question 15
NCERT Exercise
Mention common circulatory disorders: Hypertension, Coronary Artery Disease (Atherosclerosis), Angina Pectoris, Heart Failure.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Clinical Pathology of Common Human Cardiovascular Disorders:

1. Hypertension (High Blood Pressure):
  • Condition where arterial blood pressure is persistently higher than normal (normal = $120/80\text{ mm Hg}$).
  • Diagnosed when repeated resting readings exceed $140/90\text{ mm Hg}$ ($140 = \text{systolic}, 90 = \text{diastolic}$).
  • Leads to cardiac hypertrophy and damages vital organs: brain (cerebral stroke), kidneys (renal failure), and retina.
2. Coronary Artery Disease (CAD / Atherosclerosis):
  • Progressive narrowing and loss of elasticity of coronary arteries supplying oxygenated blood to heart musculature.
  • Caused by pathological deposition of calcium, fat, cholesterol, and fibrous tissue (atheromatous plaques) inside coronary lumen, obstructing coronary circulation.
3. Angina Pectoris ("Angina"):
  • Severe, acute, suffocating retrosternal chest pain that radiates down the left shoulder and arm.
  • Occurs due to transient myocardial ischemia (insufficient blood and oxygen flow reaching the heart muscle). Triggered by physical exertion or emotional stress.
4. Heart Failure:
  • The state of heart when it cannot pump blood effectively enough to meet the metabolic demands of the body.
  • Often called congestive heart failure because lungs become congested with fluid. (Distinct from Cardiac Arrest — when heart stops beating; and Heart Attack / Myocardial Infarction — when heart muscle dies due to total coronary artery occlusion).
