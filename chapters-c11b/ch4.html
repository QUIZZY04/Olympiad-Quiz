Fundamental Basis of Animal Classification & Coelom Types
NCERT Textbook Exercise 1 to 14: Non-Chordates, Chordates, Coelom, Symmetry, Flight Adaptations & Taxonomic Identification
Question 1
NCERT Exercise
What are the difficulties that you would face in classification of animals, if common fundamental features are not taken into account?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Difficulties in Animal Classification Without Fundamental Features:
The animal kingdom encompasses over a million described species displaying astounding morphological diversity. Without standard, universally accepted fundamental criteria (such as levels of organization, symmetry, germ layers, coelom, and body segmentation), biological classification would encounter severe obstacles:

1. Overwhelming Chaos and Confusion:
Animals sharing superficial or convergent adaptations (e.g., bats, birds, and flying insects) would be erroneously grouped together due to the presence of wings, masking their true genetic lineages.

2. Misleading Grouping Based on Habitat:
Classifying solely by habitat would group jellyfish, whales, sharks, and squids together as "aquatic animals," despite jellyfish lacking organs and whales being air-breathing placental mammals.

3. Inability to Track Evolutionary Phylogeny:
Fundamental homologies—such as the emergence of bilateral symmetry, cephalization, true triploblastic coelom, and the notochord—allow scientists to map out evolutionary descent. Ignoring these would erase phylogenetic lineages.

4. Impossibility of Placing Newly Discovered Species:
Every year thousands of new marine and terrestrial invertebrates are cataloged. Without fundamental architectural hallmarks, there would be no objective taxonomic rubric to assign new specimens to their correct phylum or class.

Risk of grouping unrelated species based on superficial analogies (e.g. wings/habitat)1 Mark
Loss of phylogenetic and evolutionary relationships1 Mark
Impossibility of systematic placement of newly discovered species1 Mark
Question 2
NCERT Exercise
If you are given a specimen, what are the steps that you would follow to classify it?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Hierarchical Scientific Steps to Classify an Unknown Animal Specimen:

Step 1: Determine the Level of Structural Organisation:
  • Cellular level: Cells aggregate loosely without forming true tissues (Phylum Porifera).
  • Tissue level: Cells performing similar functions form specialized tissues (Cnidaria, Ctenophora).
  • Organ / Organ-system level: Tissues group into functional organs and systems (Platyhelminthes to Chordata).
Step 2: Assess Body Symmetry:
  • Asymmetrical: Cannot be divided into equal halves through any central plane (most Sponges).
  • Radial symmetry: Any plane passing through central longitudinal axis divides body into identical halves (Cnidaria, Ctenophores, Adult Echinoderms).
  • Bilateral symmetry: Body can be divided into identical right and left halves in only one plane (Platyhelminthes to Chordates).
Step 3: Identify Germ Layer Organisation:
  • Diploblastic: Developed from two embryonic layers—outer ectoderm and inner endoderm, separated by undifferentiated mesoglea (Cnidaria, Ctenophora).
  • Triploblastic: Possesses three distinct germ layers—ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm (Platyhelminthes to Chordata).
Step 4: Examine Nature of the Body Cavity (Coelom):
  • Acoelomate: No cavity between gut and body wall (Platyhelminthes).
  • Pseudocoelomate: Mesoderm exists as scattered pouches, not a true continuous peritoneal lining (Aschelminthes / Roundworms).
  • Eucoelomate: True cavity completely lined by mesodermal peritoneum (Annelids, Arthropods, Molluscs, Echinoderms, Chordates).
Step 5: Inspect Metameric Segmentation & Notochord Presence:
  • Check for true internal and external segmentation (metamerism) as seen in Annelids and Arthropods.
  • Check for a dorsal rod-like notochord derived from mesoderm: if present, classify under Chordata; if absent, classify under Non-chordata.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Level of organisation (cellular, tissue, organ system)1 Mark
Body symmetry (asymmetrical, radial, bilateral)1 Mark
Germ layers (diploblastic vs triploblastic) and coelom (acoelomate, pseudocoelomate, eucoelomate)1 Mark
Segmentation and presence/absence of notochord (Chordate vs Non-chordate)1 Mark
Question 3
NCERT Exercise
How useful is the study of the nature of body cavity and coelom in the classification of animals?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Diagnostic Significance of the Body Cavity (Coelom) in Animal Classification:
The presence or absence of a fluid-filled cavity between the body wall and the gut wall (the coelom) is one of the most decisive diagnostic benchmarks in zoological taxonomy:

1. Fundamental Primary Categorisation:
  • Acoelomates: Animals in which the blastocoel is completely obliterated by solid mesodermal mesenchymal parenchyma cells. There is no cavity around internal organs, limiting their size and mechanical independence (e.g., Platyhelminthes / Flatworms).
  • Pseudocoelomates: Animals in which the body cavity is derived directly from the persistent embryonic blastocoel rather than splitting of mesoderm. The mesoderm appears only as scattered pouches beneath the body wall, lacking an inner visceral mesodermal lining (e.g., Aschelminthes / Roundworms).
  • Eucoelomates (True Coelomates): The coelom is a true fluid-filled body space arising entirely within embryonic mesoderm and lined on both parietal (body wall) and visceral (gut wall) sides by mesodermal epithelium (peritoneum). Subdivided into:
    • Schizocoelomates: Formed by the splitting of mesodermal bands (Annelida, Arthropoda, Mollusca).
    • Enterocoelomates: Formed by outpocketings of the embryonic archenteron (Echinodermata, Hemichordata, Chordata).
2. Physiological and Functional Utility:
  • The coelom provides room for the suspension, enlargement, muscular pulsation, and independent peristaltic movement of vital visceral organs (heart, gut, lungs).
  • Coelomic fluid acts as a hydrostatic skeleton (e.g., in annelids) providing locomotion and turgor, acts as a shock absorber against mechanical concussions, and facilitates metabolic exchange and excretion.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Clear definition of coelom (space lined by mesoderm between body wall and alimentary canal)1 Mark
Three major categories: Acoelomate (Platyhelminthes), Pseudocoelomate (Aschelminthes), Eucoelomate1 Mark
Physiological advantages of coelom (space for organ growth, hydrostatic skeleton, shock protection)1 Mark
Question 4
NCERT Exercise
Distinguish between intracellular and extracellular digestion.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Differences Between Intracellular and Extracellular Digestion:

Parameter Intracellular Digestion Extracellular Digestion
1. Site of Digestion Occurs inside the cell cytoplasm within specialized food vacuoles or phagosomes. Occurs outside cells within the specialized lumen of a gastrovascular cavity or tubular alimentary canal.
2. Enzyme Action Lysosomal enzymes are secreted directly into the intracellular food vacuole. Digestive glands and mucosal cells secrete enzymes into an open digestive cavity.
3. Size of Ingested Food Can digest only microscopic food particles or single-celled organisms ingested by phagocytosis. Capable of breaking down large, complex macroscopic food chunks or prey.
4. Efficiency & Regional Specialisation Relatively less efficient; no regional differentiation for digestion and absorption. Highly efficient; permits extensive regional organ specialization (mouth, stomach, intestine, glands).
5. Occurrence / Examples Protozoans and Porifera (sponges via choanocytes). Also initial phase in Cnidaria and Ctenophora. Annelida, Arthropoda, Mollusca, Echinodermata, and all Chordates (also partly in Cnidaria/Platyhelminthes).
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Site comparison: inside food vacuole vs outside cells in cavity/gut lumen1 Mark
Mechanism & prey size: phagocytosis of microscopic particles vs enzyme secretion on large prey1 Mark
Accurate animal examples for both types1 Mark
Question 5
NCERT Exercise
What are the difference between direct and indirect development?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Comparative Differences Between Direct and Indirect Development:

Parameter Direct Development Indirect Development
1. Larval Stage Completely absent. Involves one or more distinct, free-living intermediate larval stages.
2. Resemblance of Young to Adult The hatchling or newborn closely resembles the adult in body plan and morphology, differing only in size and sexual maturity. The larva looks morphologically very different from the adult in structure, feeding apparatus, and locomotion.
3. Metamorphosis No metamorphosis occurs; growth is continuous and isometric. Undergoes dramatic transformation called metamorphosis (retrogressive or progressive) to reach adulthood.
4. Representative Examples Hydra, Earthworms, Birds, Reptiles, and Mammals (including humans). Porifera (amphiblastula), Cnidaria (planula), Platyhelminthes (miracidium), Insects (caterpillar, maggot), Frogs (tadpole), Echinoderms (bipinnaria).
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Absence of larva in direct vs presence of morphologically distinct larva in indirect1 Mark
Resemblance of newborn to adult vs requirement of metamorphosis1 Mark
Accurate animal examples for both categories1 Mark
Question 6
NCERT Exercise
What are the peculiar features that you find in parasitic platyhelminthes?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Morphological, Physiological & Anatomical Adaptations in Parasitic Platyhelminthes:
Endoparasitic flatworms (e.g., Taenia solium / tapeworm and Fasciola hepatica / liver fluke) inhabit hostile microenvironments such as the human bile ducts or intestinal lumen. They exhibit remarkable evolutionary modifications:

1. Organs of Adhesion (Hooks and Suckers):
  • Possess specialized muscular suckers (acetabula) and chitinous hooks (rostellum) to firmly anchor themselves to the intestinal mucosa, preventing expulsion by peristalsis.
2. Protective Thick Tegument:
  • Body is covered by a living syncytial, metabolically active outer coating called the tegument, which is resistant to digestive enzymes, bile salts, and host immune surveillance.
3. Extreme Degeneration or Total Loss of Digestive System:
  • Since they bathe in predigested chyme in the host small intestine, Taenia completely lacks a mouth and alimentary canal; it absorbs dissolved nutrients directly across its microtriches-covered body surface.
4. Highly Developed Reproductive System and Fecundity:
  • Reproductive organs are enormously enlarged. They are hermaphroditic, with each mature proglottid containing both male and female systems, producing millions of fertilized eggs to ensure high transmission probability despite hazards of finding new hosts.
5. Anaerobic Respiration:
  • Adapted to thrive in virtually oxygen-free intestinal lumen via facultative or obligate anaerobic glycolysis.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Presence of hooks and suckers for anchorage to host mucosa1 Mark
Thick protective syncytial tegument and absence/reduction of digestive system1 Mark
Huge reproductive potential (hermaphroditism, millions of eggs) and anaerobic metabolism1 Mark
Question 7
NCERT Exercise
What are the reasons that you can think of for the arthropods to constitute the largest group of the animal kingdom?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Evolutionary Success Factors Accounting for Arthropod Dominance (>80% of All Animal Species):
Phylum Arthropoda constitutes over two-thirds of all named living organisms on Earth. Their unprecedented evolutionary triumph is attributed to several key biological adaptations:

1. Tough Chitinous Cuticular Exoskeleton:
  • The outer body is enveloped by an impermeable, hardened chitin-protein exoskeleton. It provides structural support, protection against mechanical injury and predators, and, crucially, prevents desiccation (water loss), allowing colonization of arid terrestrial habitats.
2. Jointed Appendages (Extensive Diversification):
  • Paired, segmented, jointed limbs operated by internal striated muscles have been modified into diverse specialized tools: walking legs, jumping legs, swimming paddles, grasping claws (chelae), biting mandibles, and piercing-sucking proboscises.
3. Highly Efficient Tracheal Respiratory System:
  • Insects possess a network of branching air tubes (tracheae and spiracles) that deliver oxygen directly to tissues without relying on blood circulatory speed, supporting exceptionally high metabolic rates.
4. Flight (Wings in Insects):
  • Insects were the first animals to evolve powered flight, enabling rapid dispersal, escaping predators, foraging over vast geographic ranges, and finding mates.
5. Compound Eyes and Advanced Sensory Systems:
  • Possess mosaic-forming compound eyes (composed of thousands of ommatidia), sensitive antennae detecting pheromones, statocysts for balance, and auditory organs.
6. High Reproductive Output and Metamorphosis:
  • Short generation times, vast egg counts, and complete metamorphosis (holometabolous development: egg → larva → pupa → adult) eliminate competitive resource overlap between larvae (leaf-feeders) and adults (nectar-feeders).
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Chitinous exoskeleton preventing water loss and offering protection1 Mark
Jointed appendages adapted for diverse functions (walking, feeding, defense)1 Mark
Tracheal respiratory system and evolution of powered flight in insects1 Mark
Compound eyes, pheromone detection, and high fecundity with niche-differentiating metamorphosis1 Mark
Question 8
NCERT Exercise
Water vascular system is the characteristic of which group of the following: (a) Porifera (b) Ctenophora (c) Echinodermata (d) Chordata?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Correct Option: (c) Echinodermata

Detailed Biological Explanation & Working Mechanism:
  • The water vascular system (also known as the ambulacral system) is an exclusive, diagnostic hallmark of Phylum Echinodermata (e.g., Asterias / Starfish, Echinus / Sea urchin, Antedon / Sea lily, Cucumaria / Sea cucumber).
  • It is a specialized hydrostatic coelomic network consisting of:
    • A sieve-like perforated plate on the aboral surface called the madreporite.
    • A stone canal connecting to a central circular ring canal.
    • Five radial canals extending into the arms.
    • Hundreds of hydraulic, muscular, sucker-bearing tube feet (podia) operated by contractile ampullae.
  • Functions of Water Vascular System:
    1. Locomotion and adhesion to rocky seabeds.
    2. Capture, handling, and transport of prey (such as prying open bivalve molluscs).
    3. Gas exchange (respiration) through the thin walls of tube feet and dermal branchiae.
    4. Excretion of nitrogenous wastes via diffusion.
Clarification Note: In contrast, Porifera (sponges) possess a water canal system (Ostia → Spongocoel → Osculum) driven by choanocyte flagella, which is completely distinct from the hydraulic water vascular system of echinoderms.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Identification of (c) Echinodermata1 Mark
Explanation of ambulacral mechanism (madreporite, tube feet) and functions (locomotion, food capture, respiration)1 Mark
Question 9
NCERT Exercise
"All vertebrates are chordates but all chordates are not vertebrates". Justify the statement.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Rigorous Justification of the Statement:

1. Fundamental Hallmarks of Phylum Chordata:
All animals placed under Phylum Chordata must possess the following three diagnostic features at some stage in their life cycle:
  • A solid, dorsal, longitudinal rod called the notochord.
  • A dorsal, hollow, single nerve cord.
  • Paired pharyngeal gill slits (and a post-anal tail).
2. Classification of Phylum Chordata into Subphyla:
Phylum Chordata is subdivided into three distinct subphyla:
  • (i) Urochordata (Tunicata): The notochord is present only in the larval tail and disappears during retrogressive metamorphosis in the sessile adult (e.g., Ascidia, Salpa, Doliolum).
  • (ii) Cephalochordata: The notochord extends from head to tail tip (rostrum to tail) and persists throughout life, without ever being transformed into a backbone (e.g., Branchiostoma / Amphioxus).
  • (iii) Vertebrata (Craniata): The embryonic notochord is completely replaced by a cartilaginous or bony vertebral column (backbone) in the adult.
Logical Conclusion:
  • Since all members of Vertebrata possess a notochord during their embryonic phase, all vertebrates are fundamentally chordates.
  • However, the protochordates (Urochordata and Cephalochordata) possess a notochord but never develop a vertebral column. Hence, all chordates are NOT vertebrates.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Definition of chordate hallmarks (notochord, dorsal hollow nerve cord, pharyngeal slits)1 Mark
Account of Protochordates (Urochordata & Cephalochordata) lacking a vertebral column1 Mark
Explanation that Vertebrata replaces embryonic notochord with bony/cartilaginous backbone1 Mark
Question 10
NCERT Exercise
How important is the presence of air bladder in Pisces?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Functional Importance of the Air Bladder (Swim Bladder) in Pisces:
The air bladder (or swim bladder) is a hydrostatic, gas-filled sac situated dorsally in the body cavity between the alimentary canal and the vertebral column in bony fishes (Class Osteichthyes):

1. Hydrostatic Organ for Neutral Buoyancy:
  • By regulating the volume and pressure of oxygen, nitrogen, and carbon dioxide within the bladder (via the gas gland and ovale or pneumatic duct), the fish adjusts its specific gravity to match the density of water at various depths.
  • This achieves neutral buoyancy, allowing the fish to hover effortlessly at any chosen water depth without constantly exerting muscular swimming effort.
2. Contrast with Chondrichthyes (Cartilaginous Fishes):
  • Cartilaginous fishes (e.g., sharks, dogfish Scoliodon, stingrays) lack an air bladder. Consequently, they are heavier than water and must swim continuously to generate hydrodynamic lift, otherwise they sink to the bottom.
3. Energy Conservation:
  • The buoyancy conferred by the air bladder allows bony fishes to rest motionlessly in the water column, conserving substantial metabolic energy.
4. Accessory Respiratory & Auditory Roles:
  • In lungfishes (Dipnoi, e.g., Protopterus), the air bladder is heavily vascularized and functions as an accessory lung during seasonal droughts.
  • In many teleosts, the air bladder connects to the inner ear via Weberian ossicles, acting as a sound resonator to enhance auditory acuity.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Primary function: hydrostatic buoyancy regulation allowing effortless hovering1 Mark
Contrast with cartilaginous fishes (sharks) which must swim continuously to avoid sinking1 Mark
Accessory functions: energy conservation, auditory perception (Weberian ossicles), and respiration in lungfishes1 Mark
Question 11
NCERT Exercise
What are the modifications that are observed in birds that help them fly?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Anatomical, Physiological & Morphological Flight Adaptations in Birds (Class Aves):

1. Forelimbs Modified into Wings:
  • Forelimbs are completely modified into aerofoil wings covered with flight feathers (remiges and rectrices) that generate lift and thrust.
2. Pneumatic (Hollow) Skeleton:
  • Long bones are hollow and filled with air cavities (pneumatic bones) rather than heavy bone marrow, drastically reducing specific body weight while retaining high tensile strength.
3. Streamlined Spindle-Shaped Body:
  • Streamlined contour offers minimum aerodynamic resistance during flight through air.
4. Powerful Flight Muscles & Keeled Sternum:
  • Breastbone (sternum) is modified into a massive ventral ridge called a keel (carina), which anchors colossal flight muscles: Pectoralis major (depresses the wing) and Pectoralis minor (elevates the wing).
5. Air Sacs and Continuous Biphasic Respiration:
  • Lungs are supplemented by 9 interconnected membranous air sacs that extend into bones. They ensure continuous unidirectional airflow across parabronchi, allowing gas exchange during both inhalation and exhalation to fulfill intense flight metabolic demands.
6. Weight-Reduction Adaptations:
  • Absence of heavy jaws and teeth; replaced by a lightweight keratinous beak.
  • Absence of a urinary bladder; nitrogenous wastes are excreted as semisolid paste of insoluble uric acid (uricotelism).
  • Females possess only a single functional left ovary and oviduct, shedding the weight of the right reproductive tract.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Forelimbs modified into wings and pneumatic (hollow air-filled) bones1 Mark
Streamlined body, keeled sternum, and powerful flight pectoralis muscles1 Mark
Air sacs connected to lungs ensuring continuous double respiration1 Mark
Weight reduction: toothless beak, absence of urinary bladder, single functional left ovary1 Mark
Question 12
NCERT Exercise
Could the number of eggs or young ones produced by an oviparous and viviparous mother be equal? Why?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Answer: No, the number of eggs produced by an oviparous mother is vastly greater than the number of young ones produced by a viviparous mother.

Biological and Evolutionary Reasons:

1. High Vulnerability and Predation Risk in Oviparous Species:
  • Oviparous animals (e.g., frogs, bony fishes, reptiles, birds) lay eggs outside the maternal body in aquatic or terrestrial environments.
  • These eggs are exposed to severe environmental fluctuations (temperature, desiccation, floods) and intense predation by aquatic and terrestrial carnivores.
  • A huge percentage of eggs and hatched larvae perish before reaching reproductive maturity. Therefore, oviparous mothers must produce thousands to millions of eggs (e.g., millions of eggs in teleosts) to ensure that at least a small fraction survives to perpetuate the species.
2. Internal Protection and High Survival Rate in Viviparous Species:
  • Viviparous animals (e.g., eutherian mammals) retain the fertilized zygote internally within the female uterus.
  • The developing embryo receives continuous physiological protection, optimal temperature, oxygen, and nourishment directly from maternal blood via the placenta.
  • After birth, young ones receive intensive parental care and lactation, ensuring an extraordinarily high survival rate. Consequently, producing 1 to few offspring per reproductive cycle is evolutionarily optimal and conserves maternal resources.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Direct answer: No, oviparous mothers produce significantly more eggs than viviparous young1 Mark
Oviparous hazards: external development, environmental threats, high predation loss requiring large numbers1 Mark
Viviparous advantages: intrauterine embryonic protection, placenta, parental care giving high survival1 Mark
Question 13
NCERT Exercise
Segmentation in the body is first observed in which of the following: (a) Platyhelminthes (b) Aschelminthes (c) Annelida (d) Arthropoda?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Correct Option: (c) Annelida

Scientific Justification:
  • True metameric segmentation (metamerism) is an evolutionary hallmark first exhibited by animals belonging to Phylum Annelida (e.g., Pheretima / Earthworm, Hirudinaria / Leech, Nereis).
  • Definition of Metamerism: The body is divided both externally (by grooves/annuli) and internally (by intersegmental septa) into a linear series of repeating segments called metameres or somites, with serial repetition of at least some organs (such as nephridia, ganglia, and blood vessels).
  • Platyhelminthes exhibit pseudo-segmentation (proglottids in tapeworms formed by strobilization, not true metamerism).
  • Aschelminthes are unsegmented roundworms.
  • Arthropoda display metamerism, but evolved chronologically after Annelida.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Correct choice: (c) Annelida1 Mark
Definition of true metameric segmentation with serial repetition of internal organs1 Mark
