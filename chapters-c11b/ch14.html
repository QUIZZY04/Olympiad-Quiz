NCERT Textbook Exercise 1 to 10: Respiratory Volumes, Diffusion Membrane, CO2 Transport, Bohr Effect & Regulation
Question 1
NCERT Exercise
Define vital capacity. What is its significance?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Definition of Vital Capacity (VC):
Vital Capacity (VC) is the maximum volume of air a person can breathe out after a forceful, maximal inhalation (or the maximum volume of air a person can breathe in after a forceful, maximal exhalation).
Mathematically, Vital Capacity is the sum of three pulmonary volumes: $$\mathbf{VC = ERV + TV + IRV}$$
  • TV (Tidal Volume): Volume of air inspired or expired during normal quiet breathing (~500 mL).
  • IRV (Inspiratory Reserve Volume): Extra volume that can be forcibly inspired (~2500 to 3000 mL).
  • ERV (Expiratory Reserve Volume): Extra volume that can be forcibly expired (~1000 to 1100 mL).
  • Normal Value: Approximately 3500 mL to 4500 mL in healthy young adults (higher in athletes, divers, and males).
2. Clinical & Physiological Significance of Vital Capacity:
  • Index of Lung Functional Capacity: Serves as a vital diagnostic spirometric indicator of pulmonary fitness and respiratory reserve. High VC ensures superior oxygen supply during strenuous exercise.
  • Diagnosis of Restrictive Lung Diseases: Vital capacity is significantly reduced in restrictive pulmonary disorders (pulmonary fibrosis, silicosis, scoliosis) due to loss of alveolar elasticity and chest wall compliance.

Question 2
NCERT Exercise
State the volume of air remaining in the lungs after a normal breathing.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Functional Residual Capacity (FRC):
The volume of air that remains in the lungs after a normal, quiet expiration is termed the Functional Residual Capacity (FRC).

Formula and Numerical Calculation:
$$\mathbf{FRC = ERV + RV}$$
  • ERV (Expiratory Reserve Volume): The additional volume of air a person can expire by forceful expiration (~1000 to 1100 mL).
  • RV (Residual Volume): The volume of air that always remains in the lungs even after maximal forceful expiration, preventing alveolar collapse (~1100 to 1200 mL).
$$\text{FRC} = 1000\text{ mL (ERV)} + 1200\text{ mL (RV)} = \mathbf{2100 \text{ to } 2300\text{ mL}}$$
Physiological Importance:
The presence of FRC ensures continuous, uninterrupted diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide across alveolar capillaries even during the expiratory pause between breaths.

Question 3
NCERT Exercise
Diffusion of gases occurs only in alveolar region and not in other parts of respiratory system. Why?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Why Gaseous Exchange is Confined Exclusively to the Alveolar Region:

The human respiratory tract is anatomically divided into two distinct functional zones:
1. Conducting Zone: External nostrils → Pharynx → Larynx → Trachea → Bronchi → Terminal Bronchioles.
2. Respiratory (Exchange) Zone: Respiratory bronchioles → Alveolar ducts → Alveoli.

Scientific Reasons for Alveolar Exclusivity:
  • 1. Microscopic Thinness of Diffusion Membrane (< 0.5 $\mu m$):
    The conducting zone is lined by thick pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium with goblet cells, thick basement membranes, and rigid cartilaginous rings, creating an impenetrable physical barrier to diffusion. In contrast, the alveolar wall consists of an ultra-thin single layer of simple squamous epithelium closely apposed to capillary endothelium, with a total diffusion thickness of less than half a micrometer (0.2–0.5 $\mu m$), minimizing diffusion resistance.
  • 2. Immense Surface Area:
    The human lungs contain approximately 300 million alveoli, providing a colossal combined exchange surface area of 70 to 80 $\text{m}^2$ (equivalent to a tennis court). The conducting zone has negligible surface area.
  • 3. Extensive Capillary Vascularization:
    Alveoli are completely enveloped by a dense, anastomosing sheet of pulmonary capillary networks where RBCs flow in single file, maximizing gas contact time.
  • 4. Surfactant Lining:
    Alveoli are lined with dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (surfactant) which reduces surface tension and maintains alveolar patency.

Question 4
NCERT Exercise
What are the major transport mechanisms for $CO_2$? Explain.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
The Three Major Physiological Transport Mechanisms for Carbon Dioxide ($CO_2$) in Blood:
Tissue cells produce $CO_2$ through catabolism, which is transported by blood to the lungs via three pathways:

1. As Bicarbonate Ions ($HCO_3^-$) — 70% of Total $CO_2$:
  • The primary and predominant mode. $CO_2$ diffuses into RBCs where the zinc-containing enzyme carbonic anhydrase accelerates the reversible hydration of $CO_2$ by 10 million times: $$\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightleftharpoons{\text{Carbonic Anhydrase}} \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3 \xrightleftharpoons{\text{Carbonic Anhydrase}} \text{H}^+ + \text{HCO}_3^-$$
  • $HCO_3^-$ diffuses out of RBCs into blood plasma. To maintain electrical neutrality, chloride ions ($Cl^-$) diffuse from plasma into RBCs — a phenomenon called the Chloride Shift (Hamburger's Phenomenon). At the alveoli, the reaction reverses, expelling $CO_2$.
2. As Carbamino-Hemoglobin ($Hb-CO_2$) — 20% to 25% of Total $CO_2$:
  • $CO_2$ binds reversibly to the free amino ($-NH_2$) groups of globin polypeptide chains (not to heme): $$\text{Hb-NH}_2 + \text{CO}_2 \xrightleftharpoons{} \text{Hb-NH-COO}^- + \text{H}^+$$
  • At tissue level ($pCO_2$ high, $pO_2$ low), binding is favored. At alveolar level ($pO_2$ high, $pCO_2$ low), $O_2$ binding to heme displaces $CO_2$ from globin (Haldane Effect).
3. In Dissolved State in Plasma — ~7% of Total $CO_2$:
  • Because $CO_2$ has a solubility roughly 20 to 25 times higher than $O_2$ in water, approximately 7% dissolves directly into the aqueous phase of blood plasma as carbonic acid.

Question 5
NCERT Exercise
What will be the $pO_2$ and $pCO_2$ in the atmospheric air compared to those in the alveolar air? Being:
(i) $pO_2$ lesser, $pCO_2$ higher
(ii) $pO_2$ higher, $pCO_2$ lesser
(iii) $pO_2$ higher, $pCO_2$ higher
(iv) $pO_2$ lesser, $pCO_2$ lesser.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Correct Option: (ii) $pO_2$ higher, $pCO_2$ lesser

Quantitative Comparison of Partial Pressures (in mm Hg):

Respiratory Gas Atmospheric Air (at Sea Level) Alveolar Air (Lungs) Net Diffusion Gradient
$pO_2$ (Oxygen) 159 mm Hg (Higher) 104 mm Hg (Lesser) $O_2$ flows from atmosphere into alveoli.
$pCO_2$ (Carbon Dioxide) 0.3 mm Hg (Lesser) 40 mm Hg (Higher) $CO_2$ flows from alveoli into atmosphere.

Reason: Inhaled atmospheric air mixes with residual alveolar air (FRC) and becomes humidified with water vapor ($pH_2O = 47\text{ mm Hg}$), which dilutes $pO_2$ down to 104 mm Hg, while continuous capillary excretion of $CO_2$ raises alveolar $pCO_2$ to 40 mm Hg.

Identification of (ii) pO2 higher, pCO2 lesser in atmospheric air compared to alveolar air1 Mark
Numerical values: Atmospheric (pO2 = 159, pCO2 = 0.3) vs Alveolar (pO2 = 104, pCO2 = 40 mm Hg)1 Mark
Question 6
NCERT Exercise
Explain the process of inspiration under normal conditions.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Biomechanical Process of Normal Inspiration (Inhalation):
Inspiration is an active process driven by the contraction of primary inspiratory muscles, which generates a negative intra-thoracic pressure gradient according to Boyle's Law ($P \propto 1/V$):

  1. Contraction and Flattening of the Diaphragm:
    • The dome-shaped, muscular diaphragm contracts under motor stimulation from phrenic nerves, flattening downwards toward the abdominal cavity.
    • This expands the volume of the thoracic cavity along the antero-posterior (vertical) axis (responsible for ~75% of air entry in quiet breathing).
  2. Contraction of External Intercostal Muscles:
    • Stimulated by intercostal nerves, the external intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs and the sternum upwards and outwards.
    • This increases the volume of the thoracic cavity along the dorso-ventral axis.
  3. Expansion of Pulmonary Volume:
    • Because the parietal pleura adheres to the thoracic wall and visceral pleura adheres to lung tissue with pleural fluid between them, thoracic expansion forces the elastic lungs to expand proportionally.
  4. Negative Pressure Gradient:
    • Lung expansion drops intra-pulmonary pressure below atmospheric pressure (to about $-1 \text{ to } -2\text{ mm Hg}$ relative to atmosphere).
    • Atmospheric air rushes down its pressure gradient through the respiratory tract into the alveoli until pressures equalize (Tidal Volume ~500 mL).

Question 7
NCERT Exercise
How is respiration regulated? (Medullary rhythm center, pneumotaxic center in pons, chemosensitive area sensitive to $CO_2$ and $H^+$).
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Neural and Chemical Regulation of Respiration:
Respiration is automatically adjusted to meet the body's metabolic oxygen demands and eliminate excess $CO_2$ through coordinated neural centers in the brainstem and peripheral chemoreceptors:

A. Neural Regulatory Centers:
  • 1. Respiratory Rhythm Center (Medulla Oblongata): The primary regulatory center responsible for generating the basic, involuntary rhythmic cycle of inspiration (dorsal respiratory group) and expiration (ventral respiratory group).
  • 2. Pneumotaxic Center (Pons Varolii): Located dorsally in the upper pons. It acts as an off-switch: it transmits inhibitory signals to the inspiratory center, limiting the duration of inspiration and thereby altering respiratory rate. Strong signals produce rapid, shallow breathing.
  • 3. Apneustic Center (Lower Pons): Sends stimulatory signals to prolong inspiration (acts antagonistically to the pneumotaxic center).
B. Chemical Regulation (Chemoreceptive Reflexes):
  • 1. Central Chemosensitive Area: Situated adjacent to the medullary rhythm center. Highly sensitive to increases in $CO_2$ partial pressure ($pCO_2$) and hydrogen ion concentration ($[H^+]$ / decreased pH) in cerebrospinal fluid. Accumulation of $CO_2$ and $H^+$ triggers urgent nerve impulses to the rhythm center, accelerating depth and rate of breathing (hyperventilation) to blow off $CO_2$.
  • 2. Peripheral Chemoreceptors: Receptors located in the carotid bodies (at bifurcation of carotid arteries, innervated by glossopharyngeal nerve IX) and aortic bodies (on aortic arch, innervated by vagus nerve X). They detect rising arterial $pCO_2$, acidosis, and severe hypoxemia ($pO_2 < 60\text{ mm Hg}$), sending immediate reflex signals to the respiratory center.
Critical Note: The role of oxygen ($pO_2$) in normal respiratory regulation is quite insignificant; $CO_2$ and $H^+$ are the true primary chemical drivers of human breathing.

Question 8
NCERT Exercise
What is the effect of $pCO_2$ on oxygen transport? (Bohr effect, shift of oxygen dissociation curve).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Effect of $pCO_2$ on Oxygen Transport — The Bohr Effect (Christian Bohr, 1904):

1. The Bohr Effect Phenomenon:
The affinity of hemoglobin for oxygen is inversely related to both blood acidity ($H^+$ concentration) and the partial pressure of carbon dioxide ($pCO_2$). An increase in $pCO_2$ causes the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve to shift to the right, decreasing hemoglobin's oxygen-binding affinity and promoting the dissociation (unloading) of $O_2$ to tissues.

2. Molecular Mechanism at the Tissue Level:
  • Actively metabolizing tissues (e.g., exercising skeletal muscles) consume $O_2$ and generate large quantities of $CO_2$, raising local $pCO_2$ and $[H^+]$ (lactic acid, carbonic acid).
  • $CO_2$ diffuses into erythrocytes and hydrates to form carbonic acid, releasing $H^+$ ions.
  • $H^+$ ions bind specifically to histidine residues in deoxyhemoglobin, stabilizing the low-affinity T-state (tense conformation) of the hemoglobin tetramer.
  • This conformational change forces hemoglobin to release its bound oxygen molecules precisely where active tissues require it most.
3. Reverse Effect at the Alveolar Level:
  • In the lungs, $CO_2$ is rapidly expired; hence, $pCO_2$ is low and pH is alkaline.
  • This shifts the oxygen dissociation curve back to the left, stabilizing the high-affinity R-state (relaxed conformation) of hemoglobin, promoting maximal oxygen loading to form oxyhemoglobin.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Definition of Bohr effect: elevated pCO2 lowers hemoglobin-O2 affinity, shifting curve to the right1 Mark
Tissue-level mechanism: high pCO2 and H+ induce O2 unloading to exercising cells1 Mark
Alveolar reversal: low pCO2 and high pH shift curve left for maximal O2 loading1 Mark
Question 9
NCERT Exercise
What happens to the respiratory process in a man going up a hill? (High altitude sickness, hypoxia, hyperventilation, polycythemia).
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Physiological Responses & Acclimatization at High Altitude:

1. Environmental Challenge — Hypoxia:
As an individual ascends a high mountain (>2500–3500 meters), the barometric atmospheric pressure drops significantly (from 760 mm Hg at sea level to <500 mm Hg). Consequently, although the percentage of oxygen remains 21%, the partial pressure of oxygen ($pO_2$) declines sharply, leading to arterial oxygen desaturation, known as hypoxia.

2. Acute Effects — High Altitude Sickness:
An un-acclimatized person rapidly develops Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS) within 8–24 hours, characterized by:
  • Severe breathlessness (dyspnea) and rapid fatigue.
  • Nausea, vomiting, throbbing headache, dizziness, and insomnia.
3. Immediate Compensatory Physiological Mechanisms:
  • Hyperventilation: Hypoxia stimulates peripheral carotid body chemoreceptors, driving the respiratory rhythm center to accelerate the rate and depth of breathing.
  • Tachycardia: Heart rate and cardiac output increase to accelerate oxygen delivery.
4. Long-Term Acclimatization Mechanisms:
  • Polycythemia: Chronic renal hypoxia triggers the juxtaglomerular cells of kidneys to secrete the peptide hormone erythropoietin (EPO). EPO stimulates bone marrow hematopoiesis, drastically increasing total RBC count and hemoglobin concentration (polycythemia).
  • Elevated 2,3-BPG: RBCs synthesize more 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate, shifting the oxygen dissociation curve to the right to enhance tissue $O_2$ release.
  • Breathing Rate Normalizes: Alveolar ventilation remains chronically elevated with increased vascular capillarization.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Low atmospheric pressure causing hypoxia (reduced arterial pO2)1 Mark
Acute mountain sickness symptoms: breathlessness, headache, fatigue, dizziness1 Mark
Immediate compensatory hyperventilation via carotid chemoreceptors1 Mark

Question 10
NCERT Exercise
What is the site of gaseous exchange in an insect?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Site and Working Mechanism of Gaseous Exchange in Insects:

In insects (e.g., Cockroach, Grasshopper), the circulatory system lacks hemoglobin and plays zero role in gas transport. Instead, gas exchange occurs directly at the cellular level through an intricate network of internal air tubes called the Tracheal System:

  • 1. Spiracles: Atmospheric air enters the body through 10 pairs of lateral valved openings called spiracles (2 pairs thoracic, 8 pairs abdominal).
  • 2. Tracheal Trunks: Spiracles lead into wide internal longitudinal tracheae, reinforced by spiral cuticular chitinous rings (taenidia) that prevent tube collapse.
  • 3. Tracheoles (The Actual Exchange Site):
    The tracheae branch repeatedly into microscopic, non-taenidial, blind-ended terminal tubes called tracheoles (diameter < 1 $\mu m$).
    • The tips of tracheoles are filled with watery tracheolar fluid and terminate in direct, intimate contact with the plasma membranes of individual body tissue cells.
    • Gaseous Exchange Mechanism: Oxygen diffuses directly from the air in tracheoles into the tracheolar fluid and across cell membranes into mitochondria. Simultaneously, metabolic carbon dioxide diffuses from tissue cells into tracheoles and is expelled through spiracles.
Conclusion: The true microscopic sites of gaseous exchange are the thin, fluid-filled endings of tracheoles directly bathing tissues.
