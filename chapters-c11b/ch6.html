NCERT Textbook Exercise 1 to 12: Meristems, Tissues, Stomata, Monocot vs Dicot Anatomy, Secondary Growth & Periderm
Question 1
NCERT Exercise
State the location and function of different types of meristems.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Classification of Meristematic Tissues Based on Location in the Plant Body:

1. Apical Meristem:
  • Location: Situated at the growing tips of roots (Root Apical Meristem — RAM) and shoots (Shoot Apical Meristem — SAM).
  • Function: Responsible for primary growth of the plant, causing elongation of the main axis and its branches, and producing primary permanent tissues (dermal, ground, and primary vascular tissues).
2. Intercalary Meristem:
  • Location: Located between mature permanent tissues, commonly at the base of internodes or leaf bases (e.g., in grasses, mint, and bamboo).
  • Function: Facilitates rapid internodal elongation and regenerates parts removed by grazing herbivores (in grasses). Being derived from apical meristem, it is also a primary meristem.
3. Lateral Meristem:
  • Location: Arranged parallel to the longitudinal lateral axes of roots and stems (e.g., fascicular vascular cambium, interfascicular cambium, and cork cambium / phellogen).
  • Function: Responsible for secondary growth, causing an increase in the girth (thickness/diameter) of the plant body by adding secondary xylem, secondary phloem, and cork.

Question 2
NCERT Exercise
Cork cambium forms tissues that form the cork. Do you agree with this statement? Explain.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Answer: Yes, I fully agree with this statement.

Detailed Biological Explanation of Phellogen Activity:
As woody dicot stems and roots increase in girth due to secondary vascular growth, the peripheral epidermis and outer cortical layers experience severe tangential stress, stretching, and eventually rupture. To replace the broken protective epidermal layer, a secondary lateral meristem develops called the cork cambium (or phellogen):

  • Origin of Phellogen: Typically arises from dedifferentiation of collenchymatous or parenchymatous cells in the outer cortex (hypodermis).
  • Centrifugal & Centripetal Activity: Phellogen cells are rectangular, thin-walled, and divide periclinally on both outer and inner faces:
    • Outer Cuts (Centrifugal): Differentiates into cork or phellem. Cells of phellem become dead at maturity, lose protoplasts, and their radial and tangential cell walls are heavily impregnated with an impervious waxy, fatty substance called suberin. This renders the cork completely impermeable to water and gases, protecting underlying living tissues from desiccation, mechanical abrasion, and pathogen entry.
    • Inner Cuts (Centripetal): Differentiates into secondary cortex or phelloderm, composed of living parenchymatous cells containing chloroplasts or starch.
  • Periderm: Phellogen, phellem (cork), and phelloderm together constitute the protective secondary covering called the periderm.

Question 3
NCERT Exercise
Explain the process of secondary growth in the stems of woody angiosperms with the help of schematic diagrams. What is its significance?
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Comprehensive Process of Secondary Growth in Dicot Stems:
Secondary growth is the increase in girth (thickness) caused by the activity of two lateral meristems: vascular cambium and cork cambium.

A. Activity of Vascular Cambium (Stele Region):
  1. Formation of Cambial Ring: In dicot stems, intrafascicular cambium strip is present within open vascular bundles. The medullary ray cells adjacent to it dedifferentiate to form strips of interfascicular cambium. Together, they unite to form a continuous, circular cambial ring.
  2. Formation of Secondary Xylem & Phloem: The cambial ring cuts off cells on both sides:
    • Towards the interior (pith): Differentiates into secondary xylem (wood). Cambium is much more active internally; hence, secondary xylem accumulates rapidly.
    • Towards the exterior (cortex): Differentiates into secondary phloem.
  3. Crushing of Primary Tissues: The relentless inward accumulation of secondary xylem crushes the primary and secondary phloem, cortex, and epidermis.
  4. Formation of Annual Rings: In temperate zones, cambium activity varies with seasons:
    • Spring wood (early wood): Wide vessels with broad cavities formed when sap flow is high.
    • Autumn wood (late wood): Narrow vessels with thick walls formed during winter dormancy.
    • One spring wood + one autumn wood ring forms one annual growth ring, used to determine the age of a tree (dendrochronology).
  5. Heartwood vs. Sapwood: With age, central secondary xylem becomes plugged with tyloses, impregnated with tannins, resins, and oils, turning dark, hard, durable, and non-conducting (heartwood / duramen). Outer lighter, water-conducting wood is sapwood / alburnum.
B. Activity of Cork Cambium (Cortex Region):
  • Phellogen cuts off suberized cork (phellem) externally and phelloderm internally, forming the periderm. Phellogen produces lens-shaped openings called lenticels for gaseous exchange across the impermeable cork.
Biological Significance of Secondary Growth:
1. Increases mechanical strength and rigidity to support large woody tree trunks.
2. Replaces damaged, worn-out primary vascular elements with massive secondary conduits.
3. Provides commercial timber, cork, resins, and tannins.

Question 5
NCERT Exercise
Cut a transverse section of young stem of a plant from your school garden and observe it under the microscope. How would you ascertain whether it is a monocot stem or a dicot stem? Give reasons.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Microscopic Diagnostic Criteria to Distinguish Monocot Stem from Dicot Stem:

Upon examining the stained transverse section (T.S.) under high-power magnification, observe the following diagnostic structural features:

A. Signs Confirming a DICOT Stem:
  • Ring Arrangement of Vascular Bundles: Vascular bundles are uniform, wedge-shaped, and arranged in a clean, orderly circular ring around the central axis.
  • Open Vascular Bundles: A distinct strip of meristematic intrafascicular cambium is sandwiched between the outer phloem and inner xylem.
  • Differentiated Ground Tissue: Ground tissue is clearly demarcated into an outer collenchymatous hypodermis, a multi-layered parenchymatous cortex, a wavy starch sheath (endodermis), a pericycle with sclerenchymatous semilunar patches, and a prominent central parenchymatous pith.
B. Signs Confirming a MONOCOT Stem:
  • Scattered Vascular Bundles: Numerous oval vascular bundles are randomly scattered throughout an undifferentiated parenchymatous ground tissue (smaller bundles crowded towards periphery, larger ones towards the center).
  • Closed Vascular Bundles: Cambium is completely absent (bundles are closed).
  • Sclerenchymatous Bundle Sheath: Each bundle is encased by a dense sheath of sclerenchyma fibers.
  • Y- or V-shaped Xylem: Xylem vessels are arranged in the shape of the letter 'Y' with a prominent lysigenous water cavity (protoxylem lacuna).
  • Absence of Phloem Parenchyma: Phloem consists solely of sieve tubes and companion cells.

Question 6
NCERT Exercise
The transverse section of a plant material shows the following anatomical features: (a) the vascular bundles are conjoint, scattered and surrounded by a sclerenchymatous bundle sheaths (b) phloem parenchyma is absent. What will you identify it as?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Identification:
The plant material is definitively identified as a Monocotyledonous Stem (e.g., Zea mays / Maize stem).

Detailed Scientific Justification:
  • 1. "Vascular bundles are conjoint and scattered":
    • Conjoint: Xylem and phloem lie along the same radial plane, a hallmark of stems (roots have radial bundles where xylem and phloem lie on alternating radii).
    • Scattered: Scattered distribution (atactostele) throughout the undifferentiated parenchymatous ground tissue is an exclusive anatomical signature of monocot stems. Dicot stems have bundles arranged in a neat single ring.
  • 2. "Surrounded by a sclerenchymatous bundle sheath":
    • In monocot stems, every vascular bundle is completely encased by a prominent fibrous sheath of dead, lignified sclerenchyma cells providing rigidity, which is absent in dicot stems.
  • 3. "Phloem parenchyma is absent":
    • A classic textbook diagnostic feature of monocot stems is the total absence of phloem parenchyma (phloem consists only of sieve tube elements and companion cells). In contrast, phloem parenchyma is abundantly present in dicot stems.

Question 7
NCERT Exercise
Why are xylem and phloem called complex tissues?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Scientific Explanation for Classifying Xylem and Phloem as Complex Permanent Tissues:

1. Definition of Complex Tissue:
In plant anatomy, a simple permanent tissue (such as parenchyma, collenchyma, and sclerenchyma) is composed of only one type of structurally and functionally identical cell. In contrast, a complex tissue is composed of more than one type of cell that originate from the same meristem and coordinate harmoniously as a unified physiological unit to perform a common transport function.

2. Cellular Heterogeneity of Xylem (Wood):
Xylem is composed of four distinct cellular elements coordinating to conduct water, dissolved mineral ions, and provide mechanical strength:
  • Tracheids: Elongated dead tube-like cells with tapering ends and bordered pits.
  • Vessels (Tracheae): Long cylindrical syncytial tubes with perforated end walls.
  • Xylem Fibres: Highly thickened, lignified dead sclerenchymatous fibers offering mechanical tensile strength.
  • Xylem Parenchyma: The only living cells of xylem, storing starch, fats, and assisting radial water transport.
3. Cellular Heterogeneity of Phloem (Bast):
Phloem is composed of four distinct cellular elements coordinating to translocate organic photosynthates (sucrose):
  • Sieve Tube Elements: Long tubular conducting cells with perforated end walls (sieve plates); enucleated at maturity.
  • Companion Cells: Specialized nucleated parenchymatous sister cells maintaining the metabolic pressure gradient of sieve tubes.
  • Phloem Parenchyma: Living elongated cells storing resins, latex, mucilage, and food.
  • Phloem Fibres (Bast Fibres): Dead lignified sclerenchymatous fibers providing tensile strength (e.g., jute, flax, hemp).

Question 8
NCERT Exercise
What is stomatal apparatus? Explain the structure of stomata with a labelled diagram.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition of Stomatal Apparatus:
The stomatal apparatus is the specialized epidermal functional complex consisting of the microscopic stomatal aperture (pore), flanked by a pair of specialized guard cells, and surrounded by specialized epidermal cells called subsidiary (accessory) cells.

Detailed Structural Components:

1. Stomatal Pore:
A minute elliptical opening in the leaf epidermis through which gaseous exchange ($CO_2$ uptake, $O_2$ release) and evaporative water loss (transpiration) occur.

2. Guard Cells:
  • Shape: Typically kidney-shaped (reniform) in dicot leaves, and dumbbell-shaped in grasses and monocots.
  • Wall Thickening: The inner wall facing the stomatal pore is thick, rigid, and inelastic, whereas the outer wall away from the pore is thin, elastic, and flexible.
  • Cellular Contents: Unlike ordinary epidermal cells, guard cells possess a conspicuous nucleus, abundant cytoplasm, and functional chloroplasts.
  • Stomatal Mechanism: When potassium ions ($K^+$) and water enter guard cells, turgor pressure increases, causing the thin outer walls to bulge outward; this pulls the thick inner concave walls apart, opening the stomatal pore. When turgor drops, the walls relax, closing the pore.
3. Subsidiary Cells:
Specialized epidermal cells surrounding the guard cells that serve as chemical and water reservoirs, facilitating rapid ionic and turgor adjustments in guard cells.

Question 10
NCERT Exercise
How is the study of plant anatomy useful to us?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Practical Applications and Utility of Plant Anatomy:
The anatomical study of internal plant structure has immense industrial, agricultural, and scientific value:

1. Quality Control & Pharmacognosy:
Enables identification of adulterants and counterfeits in commercial herbal medicines, spices (cinnamon, saffron, cloves), and crude botanical drugs by examining microscopic cell structures (trichomes, stomata, calcium oxalate raphides/druses).

2. Timber & Wood Technology:
Helps timber industries distinguish high-strength hardwood from softwood, evaluate grain patterns, determine fiber length for paper pulp quality, and calculate tree age via dendrochronology (annual growth rings).

3. Crop Breeding and Disease Resistance:
Breeding scientists evaluate anatomical barriers against pests and pathogens: thick cuticles, sclerenchymatous hypodermis, and dense silicified epidermis confer resistance to sucking insect pests and fungal hyphal penetration.

4. Evolutionary Phylogeny & Taxonomy:
Internal structural features (vascular bundle arrangement, vessel perforation plates, nodal anatomy) help resolve complex taxonomic disputes and decipher evolutionary relationships among plant families.

5. Forensic Botany:
Fragmented leaf, root, or wood evidence found at crime scenes can be matched to precise botanical species through micro-anatomical sectioning.

Question 11
NCERT Exercise
What is periderm? How does periderm formation take place in the dicot stems?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition of Periderm:
The periderm is a protective secondary tissue of secondary origin that replaces the ruptured epidermis of stems and roots during secondary growth. It is composed of three distinct concentric layers:
  1. Phellogen: Cork cambium (the secondary lateral meristem).
  2. Phellem: Cork (outer dead, suberized protective layer).
  3. Phelloderm: Secondary cortex (inner living parenchymatous layer).
Step-by-Step Formation of Periderm in Dicot Stems:
  • 1. Trigger: Due to continuous formation of secondary xylem and phloem by vascular cambium, internal pressure stretches and breaks the primary epidermis and outer cortex.
  • 2. Dedifferentiation to form Phellogen: Mature living parenchymatous or collenchymatous cells in the sub-epidermal layer (outer cortex) dedifferentiate into a secondary meristem called phellogen (cork cambium).
  • 3. Periclinal Cell Divisions: Phellogen cells divide periclinally, giving off cells towards the exterior and interior:
    • Centrifugally (Outer): Cut-off cells lose protoplasm and become dead, compactly arranged hexagonal cells impregnated with suberin, forming the impervious phellem (cork).
    • Centripetally (Inner): Cut-off cells mature into living parenchymatous cells with cellulosic walls, forming the phelloderm (secondary cortex).
  • 4. Formation of Lenticels: At certain intervals, phellogen produces loosely arranged, thin-walled parenchymatous cells called complementary cells instead of suberized cork. These rupture the epidermis to form lens-shaped aerating pores called lenticels, permitting vital gas exchange across the impermeable cork layer.

Question 12
NCERT Exercise
Describe the internal structure of a dorsiventral leaf with the help of labelled diagram.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Anatomical Architecture of a Dorsiventral (Dicotyledonous) Leaf:
A transverse section (T.S.) of a typical dorsiventral leaf (e.g., Sunflower / Mango) shows three distinct tissue regions:

1. Epidermis (Upper & Lower):
  • Adaxial Epidermis (Upper): Covered by a thick, waxy, water-impermeable cuticle; generally bears fewer or no stomata (hypostomatic condition).
  • Abaxial Epidermis (Lower): Bears numerous stomata with kidney-shaped guard cells to facilitate transpiration and gas exchange, covered by a thinner cuticle.
2. Mesophyll (Ground Tissue):
The green photosynthetic tissue sandwiched between upper and lower epidermis, richly packed with chloroplasts and differentiated into two distinct zones:
  • Palisade Parenchyma (Adaxial): Located beneath the upper epidermis; composed of vertically elongated, columnar, tightly packed cylindrical cells with narrow intercellular spaces, maximizing sunlight absorption.
  • Spongy Parenchyma (Abaxial): Located below palisade cells and extending to the lower epidermis; composed of loosely arranged, oval or spherical cells with large intercellular spaces and conspicuous air cavities (sub-stomatal chambers) facilitating rapid gaseous diffusion.
3. Vascular System (Vascular Bundles):
  • Seen in the veins and midrib. Reticulate venation results in bundles of varying sizes.
  • Each bundle is conjoint, collateral, and closed (no cambium).
  • Xylem lies towards the upper (adaxial) surface, while phloem lies towards the lower (abaxial) surface.
  • Each vascular bundle is completely encased by a prominent layer of tightly packed parenchymatous cells called the bundle sheath.
