Plant Growth and Development

NCERT Rationalised 2026–27 Syllabus • All 11 Verbatim Questions with Official Step-by-Step Marking Rubrics

Plant Growth Kinetics (Sigmoid Curve) & Phytohormone Regulatory Actions
NCERT Textbook Exercise 1 to 11: Growth Kinetics, Phytohormones, Photoperiodism, Vernalisation & Stress Physiology
Question 1
NCERT Exercise
Define growth, differentiation, development, dedifferentiation, redifferentiation, determinate growth, meristem and growth rate.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Precise Botanical Definitions of Developmental Terms:

1. Growth: An irreversible, permanent increase in the size, volume, surface area, or dry biomass of an organ, cell, or organism, accompanied by metabolic energy consumption (anabolism).

2. Differentiation: The process by which structurally unspecialized meristematic cells undergo permanent biochemical and structural changes in their cell walls and protoplasm to perform specific physiological duties (e.g., loss of protoplasm and lignification of xylem tracheary elements).

3. Development: The complete progression of events and changes that an organism undergoes throughout its entire life cycle from the germination of a seed, through growth, differentiation, flowering, seed setting, senescence, to death ($Development = Growth + Differentiation$).

4. Dedifferentiation: The biological capacity of living, mature, fully differentiated permanent cells to regain the power of mitotic cell division under specific hormonal stimuli (e.g., formation of interfascicular cambium and cork cambium / phellogen from mature parenchymatous cortical cells).

5. Redifferentiation: The process whereby secondary meristematic cells (produced by dedifferentiation) once again lose their capacity to divide and mature into specialized permanent tissues (e.g., secondary xylem, secondary phloem, and cork).

6. Determinate Growth: A growth pattern wherein an organ or organism ceases growth after reaching a characteristic, genetically fixed size and shape (e.g., leaves, flowers, and fruits).

7. Meristem: A specialized region of continuously dividing, undifferentiated, isodiametric embryonic plant cells possessing dense cytoplasm, thin cellulosic walls, and prominent nuclei (e.g., apical, lateral, and intercalary meristems).

8. Growth Rate: The mathematically measured increase in plant growth per unit of time (arithmetic: $L_t = L_0 + rt$; or geometric: $W_1 = W_0 e^{rt}$).

Question 2
NCERT Exercise
Why is not any one parameter good enough to demonstrate growth in plants?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Why Multiple Quantitative Parameters Are Required to Measure Plant Growth:

At the fundamental cellular level, growth is a direct consequence of an increase in the amount of protoplasm. Because directly measuring cellular protoplasmic mass inside living, intact tissues is technically impossible without destroying the plant, biologists measure parameters that are proportionally correlated with protoplasmic expansion.

However, no solitary parameter is universally applicable to all organs or tissues because plants exhibit diverse growth habits:
  • 1. Fresh Weight: Highly unreliable because it fluctuates wildly depending on the water status, humidity, and transpiration rate of the plant; water absorption increases fresh weight without true synthesis of dry protoplasmic matter.
  • 2. Dry Weight: The most accurate index of growth (true biomass), but requires destroying and desiccating the plant tissue in an oven at 80°C, terminating further measurement of that individual.
  • 3. Length / Height: Ideal for linear organs like stems, roots, and pollen tubes (a pollen tube increases strictly in length), but completely useless for measuring planar organs like leaves or spherical organs like fruits.
  • 4. Surface Area: Appropriate for measuring the expansion of a dorsiventral leaf blade, but cannot quantify the growth of roots or tree trunks.
  • 5. Volume: Useful for measuring fruits (e.g., watermelon, apple), but inappropriate for slender filaments.
  • 6. Cell Number: A single maize root apical meristem generates >17,500 new cells per hour, whereas a watermelon cell can increase in volume by 350,000 times without dividing.
Conclusion: Different growth parameters must be tailored to the specific organ, developmental stage, and experimental objective.

Question 3
NCERT Exercise
Describe briefly: (a) Arithmetic growth (b) Geometric growth (c) Sigmoid growth curve (d) Absolute and relative growth rates.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Detailed Description of Growth Kinetics:

(a) Arithmetic Growth:
Following mitotic cell division, only one daughter cell continues to divide, while the other daughter cell differentiates and matures into a permanent cell.
  • Produces a constant, linear increase in growth over time.
  • Mathematical Formula: $$\mathbf{L_t = L_0 + rt}$$ (where $L_t$ = length at time $t$, $L_0$ = initial length, $r$ = growth rate / elongation per unit time). When plotted against time, it yields a straight line (e.g., root or shoot elongation at constant velocity).
(b) Geometric Growth:
Both daughter cells resulting from mitotic division retain the capacity to divide and continue dividing repeatedly ($1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 8 \rightarrow 16 \dots$).
  • Growth proceeds exponentially.
  • Mathematical Formula: $$\mathbf{W_1 = W_0 e^{rt}}$$ (where $W_1$ = final size, $W_0$ = initial size, $r$ = relative growth rate, $t$ = time, $e$ = base of natural logarithms).
(c) Sigmoid (S-shaped) Growth Curve:
A characteristic S-shaped curve obtained when the cumulative biomass or organ size is plotted against time under natural, limited nutrient conditions. Characterized by three successive phases:
  1. Lag Phase: Initial slow, preparatory growth period.
  2. Log (Exponential) Phase: Rapid, maximal, exponential growth.
  3. Stationary (Senescent) Phase: Growth slows down and reaches a plateau due to nutrient limitation and internal genetic constraints.
(d) Absolute vs. Relative Growth Rates:
  • Absolute Growth Rate (AGR): Measurement and comparison of total net growth per unit of time ($AGR = \frac{\text{Total Growth}}{\text{Time}}$).
  • Relative Growth Rate (RGR): Growth per unit of time expressed on a per-unit initial parameter basis ($RGR = \frac{\text{Growth in given time}}{\text{Initial parameter} \times \text{Time}}$). It reflects the comparative biological efficiency (efficiency index) of the system.

Question 4
NCERT Exercise
List five main groups of natural plant growth regulators. Write a note on discovery, physiological functions and agricultural applications of any one of them.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Five Main Groups of Natural Plant Growth Regulators (Phytohormones):
1. Auxins  |  2. Gibberellins  |  3. Cytokinins  |  4. Ethylene  |  5. Abscisic Acid (ABA).

Comprehensive Account of AUXINS:

1. Discovery of Auxins:
  • First observed by Charles Darwin and his son Francis Darwin (1880) while studying the phototropic curvature of the coleoptiles of canary grass (Phalaris canariensis); they concluded that an influence was transmitted from the tip to the sub-apical zone causing bending.
  • Frits Went (1928) successfully isolated the diffusible chemical substance from oat (Avena sativa) coleoptile tips in agar blocks, demonstrating curvature (Avena curvature test) and naming it Auxin (Greek auxein = to grow). First chemically isolated from human urine as Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA).
2. Physiological Functions of Auxin:
  • Apical Dominance: High concentrations of auxin produced by the shoot apical bud suppress the outgrowth of lateral (axillary) buds. Decapitation allows bushy lateral branching (utilized in tea plucking and hedge-making).
  • Cell Elongation: Stimulates proton pumping, loosening cell walls (acid growth hypothesis).
  • Root Initiation: Induces adventitious root formation in stem cuttings.
  • Prevents Early Abscission: Prevents premature drop of young leaves and fruits, but promotes abscission of older, senescent leaves.
3. Agricultural and Horticultural Applications:
  • Rooting of Cuttings: Synthetic auxins (NAA — Naphthalene acetic acid, IBA — Indole butyric acid) are widely used as rooting powders for rapid vegetative propagation.
  • Parthenocarpy: Applied to unpollinated flowers to induce seedless fruits (e.g., in tomatoes).
  • Selective Weedicide / Herbicide: Synthetic auxin 2,4-D (2,4-Dichlorophenoxyacetic acid) is extensively sprayed to selectively eradicate broad-leaved dicot weeds in cereal (monocot) crop fields and lawns.
  • Flowering Promotion: Promotes uniform synchronous flowering and fruit set in pineapples and litchi.

Question 5
NCERT Exercise
What do you understand by photoperiodism and vernalisation? Describe their significance.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Photoperiodism:
Photoperiodism is the physiological response of plants to the relative lengths of day (light period) and night (dark period) in regulating flowering (first discovered by Garner and Allard, 1920, in Maryland Mammoth tobacco).
  • Short Day Plants (SDP): Require a photoperiod shorter than a critical day length and an uninterrupted long dark period to flower (e.g., Tobacco, Soybean, Chrysanthemum, Rice).
  • Long Day Plants (LDP): Require a photoperiod longer than a critical day length to flower (e.g., Wheat, Radish, Spinach, Henbane).
  • Day-Neutral Plants (DNP): Flowering is independent of photoperiod length (e.g., Tomato, Sunflower, Maize, Pea).
  • Significance: The site of light perception is the leaves, which synthesize a hypothetical mobile floral stimulus called florigen that migrates to shoot apices. Enables crop scheduling, commercial floriculture, and growing off-season flowers.
2. Vernalisation:
Vernalisation is the promotion of flowering by a mandatory period of low-temperature (chilling / cold treatment, typically 1°C to 5°C) during the seed or vegetative seedling stage (first reported by T.D. Lysenko).
  • Examples: Winter varieties of wheat, barley, and rye. If planted in spring, winter wheat fails to flower; when exposed to cold winter, it flowers in summer. Also in biennials (sugarbeet, cabbage, carrot).
  • Significance: Prevents premature flowering late in the growing season before the plant has attained full vegetative maturity; allows temperate winter crops to be grown in spring. Gibberellins ($GA_3$) can substitute for cold vernalization.

Question 6
NCERT Exercise
Why is abscisic acid also known as stress hormone?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Why Abscisic Acid (ABA) is Termed the "Plant Stress Hormone":

Abscisic Acid (ABA) is synthesized in large quantities whenever a plant experiences adverse, harsh, or stressful environmental conditions (such as severe water deficit / drought, extreme cold, salinity, or high temperature), triggering emergency survival responses:

  • 1. Rapid Stomatal Closure Under Water Stress:
    During drought or soil water deficit, roots detect moisture stress and export ABA via xylem to leaves. ABA binds to plasma membrane receptors on guard cells, stimulating a massive efflux of potassium ($K^+$) and chloride ($Cl^-$) ions. Loss of turgor causes immediate stomatal closure, drastically reducing transpiration and conserving water.
  • 2. Induction of Seed and Bud Dormancy:
    ABA inhibits seed germination and precocious embryo sprouting (vivipary). It induces seed dormancy, enabling seeds to withstand desiccation, extreme winter freezing, and heat until favorable conditions return.
  • 3. Stress-Resistant Protein Synthesis:
    Upregulates gene expression of protective molecular chaperones called Late Embryogenesis Abundant (LEA) proteins and heat-shock proteins that stabilize cellular membranes and enzymes during cellular dehydration.
  • 4. Antagonist to Growth Promoters:
    Acts as an internal metabolic brake, counteracting the effects of growth stimulators (Gibberellins and Auxins).

Question 7
NCERT Exercise
"Both growth and differentiation in higher plants are open." Comment.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Scientific Commentary: Open Form of Growth and Differentiation in Higher Plants:

1. Open Form of Growth:
Unlike animals that show closed, determinate, unitary growth (where the adult body plan and organ number are fixed early in embryogenesis), higher plants retain the remarkable capacity for unlimited, continuous growth throughout their lifespan.
  • This perpetual growth is due to the presence of localized groups of perpetually dividing embryonic cells called meristems at specific locations (Root Apical Meristems, Shoot Apical Meristems, and Lateral Meristems).
  • New cells, tissues, leaves, branches, flowers, and roots are continuously and iteratively added to the plant body. Hence, plant growth is termed open.
2. Open Form of Differentiation:
Differentiation in plants is also open because the final developmental fate of a cell derived from the same meristem is not rigidly predetermined; rather, it is dictated by its spatial location and chemical microenvironment:
  • Cells positioned peripherally differentiate into epidermal cells; cells positioned deeper differentiate into cortical or vascular parenchyma.
  • Furthermore, fully differentiated, mature permanent cells retain developmental totipotency: they can dedifferentiate into secondary meristems (phellogen, interfascicular cambium) and redifferentiate into entirely new tissue types depending on hormonal concentrations (Auxin/Cytokinin ratio).
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Explanation of open growth: perpetual addition of new organs via apical/lateral meristems1.5 Marks
Explanation of open differentiation: cell fate governed by location, dedifferentiation, and plasticity1.5 Marks
Question 8
NCERT Exercise
"Both a short day plant and a long day plant can flower simultaneously in a given place." Explain.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Scientific Explanation: Simultaneous Flowering of SDP and LDP:

Yes, a Short Day Plant (SDP) and a Long Day Plant (LDP) can flower simultaneously in the exact same geographic location under specific photoperiodic conditions.

Photoperiodic Mechanism:
  • Flowering is not determined by an absolute universal number of daylight hours, but by whether the ambient day length is shorter or longer than the plant's own specific Critical Day Length (CDL).
  • Each species possesses its own unique genetically determined CDL:
    • A Short Day Plant flowers when the day length is less than its CDL.
    • A Long Day Plant flowers when the day length is greater than its CDL.
Concrete Numerical Example:
Suppose in a given geographical location during spring or autumn, the actual natural day length is 12 hours:
  • Consider an SDP (e.g., Xanthium) whose critical day length is 14 hours. Since the actual day length (12 hours) is less than its critical day length (14 hours), this SDP will flower.
  • Consider an LDP (e.g., Hyoscyamus) whose critical day length is 10 hours. Since the actual day length (12 hours) is greater than its critical day length (10 hours), this LDP will also flower.
Conclusion: Whenever the natural day length falls simultaneously below the CDL of the short-day plant and above the CDL of the long-day plant, both plants will flower concurrently side-by-side.

Question 10
NCERT Exercise
Would a defoliated plant respond to photoperiodic cycle? Why?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Answer: No, a defoliated plant will NOT respond to the photoperiodic cycle.

Scientific Explanation:
  • Leaves are the Sole Sites of Photoperiodic Perception: Experimental evidence demonstrates that the light or dark duration (photoperiod) is perceived exclusively by the leaves of a plant, not by the stem, apex, or roots.
  • Synthesis of the Florigen Stimulus: When mature leaves receive the critical inductive photoperiodic cycle, their photoreceptors (phytochromes) trigger the expression of the CONSTANS and FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) genes, synthesizing the mobile floral hormone called florigen.
  • Translocation to Shoot Apex: Florigen is transported through the phloem conduits to the vegetative shoot apical meristem, where it reprograms the apical meristem to transform into a floral meristem.
  • Defoliation Consequence: If all leaves are completely removed (defoliated), the plant lacks the organ to perceive light signals or synthesize florigen. Even if a defoliated plant is given ideal critical photoperiods, it will remain vegetative and fail to flower. Even a single intact leaf is sufficient to perceive the stimulus and induce flowering.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Clear answer: No, a defoliated plant cannot perceive photoperiodic cycles1 Mark
Explanation: Leaves are the exclusive site of light perception and florigen synthesis1 Mark
Question 11
NCERT Exercise
What would be expected to happen if: (a) $GA_3$ is applied to rice seedlings (b) dividing cells stop differentiating (c) a rotten fruit gets mixed with unripe fruits (d) you forget to add cytokinin to the culture medium?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Expected Biological Outcomes and Mechanistic Explanations:

(a) If $GA_3$ is applied to rice seedlings:
The seedlings will exhibit extreme, abnormal internodal stem elongation, becoming tall, slender, pale, and unable to support their own weight — mimicking the classic "bakanae" (foolish seedling) disease of rice discovered by Kurosawa (1926) caused by the fungus Gibberella fujikuroi.

(b) If dividing cells stop differentiating:
The dividing cells will fail to specialize into dermal, ground, or vascular tissues (xylem, phloem). The tissue will form an unorganized, undifferentiated, irregular proliferating mass of parenchymatous cells called a callus or tumorous mass, halting organogenesis and normal plant development.

(c) If a rotten (over-ripe) fruit gets mixed with unripe fruits:
Over-ripe or damaged fruits synthesize and release large quantities of volatile gaseous Ethylene ($C_2H_4$) into the surrounding air. Ethylene diffuses into adjacent unripe fruits, triggering a climacteric respiratory surge that causes premature and rapid ripening of all stored fruits, potentially leading to spoilage.

(d) If you forget to add cytokinin to the culture medium:
In plant tissue culture, cytokinins are strictly required for mitotic cell division (cytokinesis) and shoot organogenesis. In the complete absence of cytokinin, explant cells will fail to divide; cell division and shoot bud differentiation will not occur, leading to culture failure. (High auxin with no cytokinin promotes only root induction, but zero shoot formation).

(b) Cessation of differentiation: formation of undifferentiated callus/tumor1 Mark
(c) Rotten fruit: ethylene release triggering rapid ripening of unripe fruits1 Mark
(d) Omission of cytokinin: failure of cell division and shoot bud differentiation1 Mark
