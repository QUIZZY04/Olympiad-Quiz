Poem 10: For Anne Gregory

William Butler Yeats — Outer Glamour vs Inner Soul & Unconditional Divine Love — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

“Never shall a young man, thrown Into despair By those great honey-coloured Ramparts at your ear, Love you for yourself alone And not your yellow hair.” “But I can get a hair-dye And set such colour there, Brown, or black, or carrot, That young men in despair May love me for myself alone And not my yellow hair.” “I heard an old religious man But yesternight declare That he had found a text to prove That only God, my dear, Could love you for yourself alone And not your yellow hair.”
— William Butler Yeats
I. Textbook Exercise Solutions & Important Questions — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27
Q1
What does the speaker mean by 'those great honey-coloured ramparts at your ear'? What effect do they have on young men?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

'Ramparts' are high defensive walls surrounding a fort or castle. Here, Yeats uses 'ramparts' as a striking metaphor for Anne Gregory's voluminous, glowing golden-yellow hair falling over her ears. Just as a fortress wall keeps intruders out, her stunning hair acts as a barrier that dazzles young men, throwing them into romantic despair and blinding them to her true internal personality.

Anne Gregory responds that she can easily alter her outward appearance by dyeing her hair brown, black, or carrot-colored. By shedding her prized golden locks, she hopes to repel superficial suitors and attract someone who will love her for her inner character, intellect, and soul rather than her external beauty.

Q4
Critically evaluate W.B. Yeats' dialogue on the tension between superficial physical attraction and spiritual love in 'For Anne Gregory'.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Superficiality of Human Love:
Yeats addresses the universal human longing to be appreciated for one's intrinsic personality, intellect, and moral worth rather than physical skin and hair. However, he candidly points out that human attraction is inescapably visual and sensory; young men are captivated by external physical beauty ('honey-coloured ramparts').

2. Anne's Naive Idealism:
Anne Gregory naively believes that by simply dyeing her hair a less glamorous color, she can filter out shallow suitors. She fails to see that humans will merely switch to admiring other physical traits.

3. The Sublimity of Divine Love:
The poem concludes on a profound spiritual note: unconditional love that transcends physical form is a divine virtue possessed solely by the Creator. To expect flawed mortals to love purely without physical aesthetic attraction is to demand divine perfection from human nature.

Philosophical & Cultural Reflection
Analysis: In the era of social media filters and cosmetic surgery, how does 'For Anne Gregory' critique modern obsession with physical appearances?

Yeats' century-old poem anticipated modern society's intense anxiety over physical packaging. Digital media and cosmetic trends commodify looks just like Anne's 'yellow hair', confusing external photogenic beauty with personal worth. The poem reminds us that while physical beauty is transient, superficial, and easily manufactured (through hair-dye or filters), true selfhood resides in the eternal spirit within.
