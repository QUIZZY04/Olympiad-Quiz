What kind of a person was Horace Danby? Why was he considered good and respectable, yet not completely honest?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Horace Danby was a fifty-year-old bachelor who lived with a housekeeper and ran a successful lock-making business with two assistants. He was universally regarded as a good, peaceful, and respectable citizen. However, he was not completely honest because fifteen years ago he had served a prison sentence, and he meticulously burgled one safe every year to finance his expensive passion for collecting rare and valuable books.

Q5
Why does Horace Danby get furious whenever anyone speaks of 'honor among thieves'? Analyze the irony in 'A Question of Trust'.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Arrest and Betrayal:
Two days later, Horace was arrested by the police based on his fingerprints found on the looted safe. The actual owner of Shotover Grange—a sharp-tongued, sixty-year-old grey-haired woman—declared that Horace's story of the charming young lady was sheer nonsense. Horace realized that the young woman in red was herself a professional burglar who had effortlessly manipulated him into doing the dirty work for her.

2. The Irony of 'Honor Among Thieves':
The traditional criminal adage 'honor among thieves' dictates that criminals should respect and protect fellow thieves. Horace, an old-school gentleman thief, had naively trusted the woman. However, the modern, ruthless female thief possessed zero loyalty; she used him as a scapegoat, leaving him to rot in prison while she enjoyed the jewels. Now working as the prison librarian, Horace boils with rage at the phrase because he was comprehensively outwitted and ruined by one of his own kind.

🎯 Competency-Based & Value-Based Questions — Unit 23CBSE 2026-27
Critical Analysis
Analysis: Horace Danby was a meticulous planner who never stole more than what he needed for books. Why did his intelligence fail so miserably against the lady in red?

Horace's intelligence failed because he suffered from class-based gullibility and panic. When confronted by a woman who exuded the poise, voice, and commanding aura of high aristocracy, he immediately bowed to her perceived authority. His acute fear of returning to prison clouded his analytical faculties, making him overlook obvious red flags—such as why the mistress would need a thief to break her own safe rather than calling a locksmith. His desire to appease a pretty, high-born woman blinded him to forensic basics.
