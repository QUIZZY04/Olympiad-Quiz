FIRST FLIGHT POETRY

Poem 4: How to Tell Wild Animals

Carolyn Wells — Humor, Parody & Linguistic Wordplay — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

If ever you should go by chance To jungles in the east; And if there should to you advance A large and tawny beast, If he roars at you as you’re dyin’ You’ll know it is the Asian Lion... Or if some time when roaming round, A noble wild beast greets you, With black stripes on a yellow ground, Just notice if he eats you. This simple rule may help you learn The Bengal Tiger to discern.
— Carolyn Wells
I. Textbook Exercise Solutions & Important Questions — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27
Q1
How does the poet distinguish between the Asian Lion and the Bengal Tiger?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Asian Lion: The Asian Lion is found in the eastern jungles; it is a large and tawny-colored beast that terrifies you with a mighty roar so deafening that you feel you are dying of fear.

2. Bengal Tiger: The Bengal Tiger is a 'noble wild beast' with distinct black stripes on a yellow ground. The humorous identification rule is that you will know it is a tiger only when it pounces upon and eats you!

CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Asian Lion: Large, tawny coat, roars ferociously while you're dying1½ Marks
Bengal Tiger: Noble beast, black stripes on yellow coat; 'notice if he eats you'1½ Marks
Q2
What humorous characteristics does the poet provide for the Leopard, the Bear, and the Hyena/Crocodile?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Leopard: Has a peppered, spotted hide; it will leap and leap upon you ('lep and lep again'), and roaring with pain will do no good.

2. Bear: Found in your yard, it will greet you with a ferocious 'bear hug' that crushes the life out of you.

3. Hyena vs Crocodile: Hyenas always appear with merry, deceptive smiles, while weeping tears belong to the deadly crocodile swallows its prey.

CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Leopard: Peppered spots, leaping repeatedly despite your screams1 Mark
Bear: Crushing lethal 'hug'1 Mark
Hyena smiles vs weeping Crocodile tears1 Mark
Q3
How does Carolyn Wells describe the chameleon, and how is it different from a lizard?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

A true chameleon is a small reptile shaped like a lizard, but unlike a lizard, it has no ears and not a single wing. If you look at a tree and see nothing at all, that is the chameleon camouflaging its skin to match the tree bark.

CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Traits: Small lizard-like reptile with no ears and no wings1 Mark
Camouflage: Invisible on tree bark because it changes color1 Mark
Q4
Analyze the comic techniques, poetic license, and irony in 'How to Tell Wild Animals'.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Parody of Educational Wildlife Guides:
Carolyn Wells parodies conventional zoological handbooks by providing absurd identification tests where the confirmation of the animal's identity occurs only after the observer has been devoured or mauled to death.

2. Poetic License & Linguistic Puns:
The poet deliberately invents and bends English spelling and syntax to maintain comic rhyme: writing 'dyin'' to rhyme with 'lion', and creating 'lep and lep again' instead of 'leap' to achieve dynamic, snappy rhyming.

3. Incongruity and Irony:
Calling a man-eating tiger 'noble' and a bone-crushing bear's attack an affectionate 'hug' subverts fear into comic delight through dramatic understatement.

CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Parody: Fatal confirmation rules (learning identity only after being eaten)2 Marks
Poetic license: Invented spellings ('dyin'', 'lep') to fit rhyme scheme1½ Marks
Understatement and irony: 'Noble' predator, deadly bear 'hug'1½ Marks
Q5
How does the poet distinguish between a hyena and a crocodile in 'How to Tell Wild Animals'? What humorous characteristic is attributed to each?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Carolyn Wells humorously distinguishes the two predators by their apparent human-like facial expressions while attacking their prey. Hyenas always approach with deceitful, cheerful smiles and laughing sounds, whereas crocodiles shed deceptive tears (popularly known as 'crocodile tears') while devouring their victims. The poet warns that if you encounter a smiling beast it is a hyena, and if it is weeping, it is a crocodile.

CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Hyenas: Always approach prey with merry, deceptive smiles1 Mark
Crocodiles: Shed false tears while swallowing their victims1 Mark

Poetic Devices & Language
Analysis: Explain the rhyme scheme and meter used in the poem. How does it enhance the humorous tone?

The poem uses a regular six-line stanza structure with the rhyme scheme **ababcc**. The rhythmic, jaunty iambic meter gives the verses a nursery-rhyme sing-song lightness. By pairing lighthearted rhyming couplets with lethal jungle predators, the meter creates comic incongruity, rendering terrifying beasts thoroughly amusing.
