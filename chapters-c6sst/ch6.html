📌 Key Archaeological Concepts: The Harappan Civilisation
  • Terminology: Also known as the Harappan Civilisation (after Harappa, the first excavated site in 1921), the Indus Valley Civilisation, or the Indus-Sarasvatī Civilisation (over 60% of known settlements lie along the dried-up bed of the ancient Sarasvatī/Ghaggar-Hakra river).
  • Chronology: Mature urban phase flourished between c. 2600 BCE and 1900 BCE.
  • Outstanding Urban Features:
    • Town Planning: Divided into two sections: an elevated Citadel in the west (public buildings) and a larger Lower Town in the east (residential houses). Grid pattern streets intersecting at right angles.
    • Standardised Baked Bricks: Interlocking burnt bricks made in a precise mathematical ratio of 1:2:4 (thickness:width:length) used uniformly across thousands of kilometres.
    • Covered Drainage & Sanitation: Household wastewater flowed through terracotta pipes into covered street sewers equipped with inspection traps and silt pits.
    • Water Architecture: The Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro made watertight with natural bitumen; massive rock-cut water reservoirs at Dholavira; world's earliest tidal dockyard at Lothal.
  • Crafts, Trade & Decline: Steatite seals with unicorn/bull motifs and undeciphered script, bronze casting (Dancing Girl), cubical chert weights, and overseas trade with Mesopotamia (Meluhha). The civilisation declined around 1900 BCE due to the drying up of the Sarasvatī River and climatic shifts.

The Big Questions (Chapter Inquiry & Openers)
Big Q1. Why Did Early Cities Emerge Along Indus and Sarasvatī? Concept ▼

Question: Why did India’s first urban civilisation develop along the valleys of the Indus and Sarasvatī rivers? What natural advantages supported urban growth?

1. Perennial Water Supply:
The Himalayan snow-fed Indus and Sarasvatī rivers provided reliable year-round freshwater for large city populations, agriculture, and animal husbandry.

2. Rich Alluvial Soil:
Annual river flooding deposited fertile silt across floodplains, allowing farmers to produce large agricultural surpluses of wheat, barley, pulses, and cotton to feed non-farming city populations.

3. Riverine Transport Highways:
Boats could easily transport heavy building timber, stones, and agricultural goods between riverine settlements.

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for water supply + 1 Mark for alluvial fertility & food surplus + 1 Mark for riverine transport routes.

Big Q2. What Innovations Made Harappan Cities Unique in Antiquity? Explain ▼

Question: What technological, civic, and architectural innovations distinguished the Harappan cities from other ancient Bronze Age civilisations?

1. Scientific Town Planning:
Harappan towns were laid out on a meticulous grid plan with wide streets crossing at right angles, unlike the cramped, winding alleys of contemporary Mesopotamia or Egypt.

2. Covered Drainage & Domestic Sanitation:
Almost every house had a private bathing area and toilet with drains emptying into covered street sewers equipped with inspection traps and soak pits.

3. Standardization of Bricks and Weights:
Baked bricks followed an exact uniform ratio (1:2:4) across hundreds of towns spanning 1.5 million sq km, supported by binary chert weights for fair trade.

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for grid town planning + 1 Mark for covered underground drainage + 1 Mark for standard bricks (1:2:4) and weights.

Questions, activities and projects (All 8 Official NCERT Textbook Exercises)
Q1. Why Is the Civilisation Known by Multiple Names? Short ▼

Question: Why is this civilisation known by multiple names—Harappan, Indus Valley, and Indus-Sarasvatī Civilisation? Why is "Harappan Civilisation" the standard archaeological term? [3 Marks]

1. Indus Valley Civilisation:
Early excavations in the 1920s took place around the Indus River valley (Harappa on the Ravi and Mohenjo-daro on the Indus), leading historians to coin this name.

2. Indus-Sarasvatī Civilisation:
Subsequent discoveries revealed that over 60% of all Harappan settlements were situated along the basin of the sacred Sarasvatī River (today’s Ghaggar-Hakra dry river bed), justifying this title.

3. Why "Harappan Civilisation" Is Preferred:
In archaeology, international convention mandates that when a previously unknown ancient culture is discovered, it is officially named after the type-site—the very first site where its remains were excavated. Since Harappa was excavated first in 1921 by Daya Ram Sahni, archaeologists call it the Harappan Civilisation.

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for Indus Valley origin + 1 Mark for Indus-Sarasvatī concentration + 1 Mark for archaeological type-site naming convention (Harappa in 1921).

Q2. 150–200 Word Report: Achievements of the Civilisation Long Answer ▼

Question: Write a concise report (150–200 words) on the remarkable achievements of the Harappan Civilisation in town planning, engineering, and crafts. [5 Marks]

Report: The Architectural and Technological Marvels of the Harappan Civilisation

The Harappan Civilisation (c. 2600–1900 BCE) represents South Asia’s earliest urban revolution. Its achievements include:

1. Advanced Urban Planning: Cities were divided into an elevated western Citadel (for public and administrative buildings) and a Lower Town for residential housing. Streets were aligned north-south and east-west in a precise grid.

2. Unsurpassed Sanitation: Every household had private paved bathrooms. Wastewater discharged through clay conduits into covered street sewers made of baked bricks with removable inspection slabs for desilting—a public hygiene standard unmatched in the ancient world.

3. Standardization & Engineering: Standardized burnt bricks (1:2:4 ratio) were used everywhere. At Dholavira, rock-cut water reservoirs harvested scarce monsoon rainwater, while Lothal featured the world’s earliest tidal dockyard.

4. Art, Crafts & Commerce: Craftspersons produced intricate steatite seals, bronze sculptures (like the Dancing Girl), carnelian beads, and cubical chert weights, maintaining flourishing maritime trade with ancient Mesopotamia.

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for grid town planning + 1.5 Marks for covered drainage & sanitation + 1 Mark for brick & water engineering (Dholavira/Lothal) + 1.5 Marks for crafts, metallurgy, and trade.

Q3. Journey from Harappa to Kalibangan in 2500 BCE Long Answer ▼

Question: Imagine you are traveling from Harappa to Kalibangan in the year 2500 BCE. Describe the modes of transport, the landscape, the goods you might carry, and what you would see upon arrival. [4 Marks]

1. Modes of Transport:
We would travel across land using solid wooden-wheeled bullock carts drawn by humped oxen (as evidenced by terracotta toy carts excavated at Harappa), and journey along the river network using flat-bottomed wooden boats with oars.

2. The Landscape:
The terrain transitioned from the fertile river plains of the Ravi and Indus to the green floodplains of the flowing Sarasvatī River. Along the way, we would pass agricultural villages harvesting wheat, barley, and cotton.

3. Goods Carried for Trade:
We would carry finished steatite seals, carnelian beads from Gujarat, and bronze tools to exchange for copper ingots from Rajasthan (Khetri mines) and agricultural grain.

4. Arrival at Kalibangan:
Upon reaching Kalibangan (in Rajasthan), we would see fortified mud-brick walls, ploughed agricultural fields showing furrow marks, and rows of ceremonial fire altars built on mud-brick platforms.

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for transport (bullock carts & boats) + 1 Mark for landscape description + 1 Mark for trade goods (beads, bronze, copper) + 1 Mark for Kalibangan features (ploughed field, fire altars).

Q4. A Harappan Visitor in a Modern Indian Kitchen Think & Answer ▼

Question: If an ancient Harappan citizen were magically transported into a modern Indian kitchen today, what things would surprise them, and what things would feel familiar? [4 Marks]

1. Things That Would Feel Familiar:
• Grains and Ingredients: Wheat flour (atta), barley, mustard seeds, sesame (til), chickpeas, lentils, and peas.
• Cooking Utensils & Tools: Clay pots (matkas), round stone rolling boards (chakla), pestle and mortar for grinding spices, and open flame cooking.
• Use of Cotton Aprons: The fabric of cotton clothes and simple clay lamps.

2. Things That Would Completely Surprise Them:
• Modern Materials: Shiny stainless steel thalis, plastic containers, and glass jars.
• Electric Appliances: Electric refrigerators, microwave ovens, blender-mixers, and chimney exhausts.
• LPG Gas Stoves & Running Tap Water: Cooking without firewood on metal gas burners, and turning on shiny metal taps to get running water instead of drawing water from a brick well.

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 2 Marks for identifying familiar items (wheat, barley, clay pots, mortar/pestle) + 2 Marks for identifying surprising modern items (steel, electricity, gas stove, tap water).

Q5. Water Management: Great Bath & Dholavira Reservoirs Explain ▼

Question: Describe the remarkable water management and sanitation engineering of the Harappan Civilisation with special reference to Mohenjo-daro’s Great Bath and Dholavira’s rock-cut reservoirs. [4 Marks]

1. The Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro:
• A magnificent rectangular pool built of finely fitted baked bricks laid on edge.
• Made completely watertight with a thick backing layer of natural bitumen (asphalt) between brick layers.
• Featured flights of steps on north and south ends, surrounding rooms for changing clothes, and a huge corbelled drain to empty and clean dirty bath water.

2. Rainwater Harvesting at Dholavira (Rann of Kutch):
• Because Kutch experienced low rainfall, engineers excavated 16 massive rock-cut reservoirs.
• Stone dams were built across seasonal streams (Manhar and Mansar) to divert rainwater through stone channels into gigantic storage tanks, providing fresh water year-round.

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 2 Marks for Great Bath construction (bitumen waterproofing, steps, drainage) + 2 Marks for Dholavira rock-cut reservoirs and rainwater harvesting dams.

Q6. Archaeological Evidence of Harappan Civic Sense Short ▼

Question: What archaeological evidence proves that Harappan citizens and administrators had a highly developed civic sense? Give three examples. [3 Marks]

1. Public Covered Sewerage:
Rather than dumping household waste into the streets, every family discharged waste through individual wall chutes into underground brick-covered sewers.

2. Regular Street Dustbins and Soak Pits:
Excavations revealed terracotta soak jars and public waste disposal bins placed along streets for solid garbage collection.

3. Strict Enforcement of Building Regulations:
House doors never opened into busy main avenues (preventing traffic blocks), and private houses were strictly forbidden from encroaching onto public streets.

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark each for covered underground drainage, street dustbins/soak pits, and strict anti-encroachment building rules.

Q7. Harappan Seals, Script and International Trade Short ▼

Question: Describe the Harappan seals. What materials were they made of, what symbols were carved on them, and how were they used in long-distance maritime trade? [3 Marks]

1. Material & Crafting:
Most seals were square or rectangular tablets carved out of soft stone called steatite (soapstone), then baked to harden.

2. Motifs & Inscriptions:
Carved with lifelike animal figures (the humped bull, elephant, tiger, and the mythological unicorn) alongside short inscriptions written in the undeciphered Harappan pictographic script.

3. Role in International Trade:
Merchants tied wet clay tags around bundles of trade goods and stamped their seal into the clay. If the seal impression arrived intact at ports in Mesopotamia (Oman, Bahrain, Ur), the buyer knew the shipment had not been tampered with.

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for steatite material + 1 Mark for animal motifs and undeciphered script + 1 Mark for sealing goods for trade security.

Q8. Causes of the Decline of the Harappan Civilisation Short ▼

Question: Why did the great Harappan civilisation decline around 1900 BCE? Explain the major environmental and geographical factors identified by archaeologists. [3 Marks]

1. Drying Up of the Sarasvatī River:
Geological shifts and earthquakes diverted tributaries (like the Yamuna and Sutlej) away from the Sarasvatī River basin, causing this lifeline river to gradually dry up and forcing mass migrations eastward towards the Ganga plains.

2. Climate Change and Deforestation:
Felling massive forests to bake billions of bricks and smelt copper, combined with a weakening monsoon, led to desertification and severe crop failures.

3. Devastating Floods and Trade Collapse:
Repeated silt-laden flooding of the Indus submerged cities like Mohenjo-daro, while the collapse of trade with Mesopotamia impoverished the urban merchant class.

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for drying of Sarasvatī River + 1 Mark for deforestation & weakened monsoon + 1 Mark for flooding and trade collapse.
