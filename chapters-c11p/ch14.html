Question 14.1
NCERT Exercise
A string of mass $2.50\text{ kg}$ is under a tension of $200\text{ N}$. The length of the stretched string is $20.0\text{ m}$. If the transverse jerk is struck at one end of the string, how long does the disturbance take to reach the other end?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given Data:
• Mass of string $M = 2.50\text{ kg}$
• Length of string $L = 20.0\text{ m}$
• Tension in string $T = 200\text{ N}$

1. Linear Mass Density ($\mu$):
$$\mu = \frac{M}{L} = \frac{2.50\text{ kg}}{20.0\text{ m}} = \mathbf{0.125\text{ kg m}^{-1}}$$

2. Speed of Transverse Wave ($v$):
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{200\text{ N}}{0.125\text{ kg m}^{-1}}} = \sqrt{1600} = \mathbf{40\text{ m s}^{-1}}$$

3. Time taken by the disturbance to reach the other end ($t$):
$$t = \frac{L}{v} = \frac{20.0\text{ m}}{40\text{ m s}^{-1}} = \mathbf{0.50\text{ s}}$$

$$\mathbf{\text{The disturbance takes } 0.50\text{ seconds to reach the other end.}}$$

Question 14.2
NCERT Exercise
A stone dropped from the top of a tower of height $300\text{ m}$ splashes into the water of a pond near the base of the tower. When is the splash heard at the top? Given that the speed of sound in air is $340\text{ m s}^{-1}$ ($g = 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given Data:
• Height of tower $h = 300\text{ m}$
• Speed of sound in air $v = 340\text{ m s}^{-1}$
• Acceleration due to gravity $g = 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$
• Initial velocity of stone $u = 0$

1. Time taken by the stone to fall into the pond ($t_1$):
From kinematics: $h = ut_1 + \frac{1}{2}gt_1^2 = 0 + \frac{1}{2}(9.8)t_1^2 = 4.9 t_1^2$
$$t_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 300}{9.8}} = \sqrt{\frac{600}{9.8}} = \sqrt{61.224} = \mathbf{7.825\text{ s}}$$

2. Time taken by the sound of the splash to travel back up to the top ($t_2$):
$$t_2 = \frac{h}{v} = \frac{300\text{ m}}{340\text{ m s}^{-1}} = \mathbf{0.882\text{ s}}$$

3. Total time after which the splash is heard ($T_{\text{total}}$):
$$T_{\text{total}} = t_1 + t_2 = 7.825\text{ s} + 0.882\text{ s} = \mathbf{8.707\text{ s} \approx 8.71\text{ s} \approx 8.7\text{ s}}$$

$$\mathbf{\text{The splash is heard at the top after approximately } 8.71\text{ seconds (or } 8.7\text{ s}\text{).}}$$

Calculation of sound ascent time $t_2 = h/v = 0.882\text{ s}$1 Mark

Question 14.3
NCERT Exercise
A steel wire has a length of $12.0\text{ m}$ and a mass of $2.10\text{ kg}$. What should be the tension in the wire so that speed of a transverse wave on the wire equals the speed of sound in dry air at $20^\circ\text{C} = 343\text{ m s}^{-1}$?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given Data:
• Length of steel wire $L = 12.0\text{ m}$
• Mass of steel wire $M = 2.10\text{ kg}$
• Required transverse wave speed $v = 343\text{ m s}^{-1}$

1. Linear Mass Density of the wire ($\mu$):
$$\mu = \frac{M}{L} = \frac{2.10\text{ kg}}{12.0\text{ m}} = \mathbf{0.175\text{ kg m}^{-1}}$$

2. Required Tension ($T$):
From the wave speed formula: $v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} \implies v^2 = \frac{T}{\mu} \implies T = \mu v^2$

$$T = (0.175\text{ kg m}^{-1}) \times (343\text{ m s}^{-1})^2$$
$$T = 0.175 \times 117649 = \mathbf{20588.58\text{ N} \approx 2.06 \times 10^4\text{ N}}$$

$$\mathbf{\text{The required tension in the wire is approximately } 2.06 \times 10^4\text{ N.}}$$

Question 14.4
NCERT Exercise
Use the formula $v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}}$ to explain why the speed of sound in air:
(a) is independent of pressure,
(b) increases with temperature,
(c) increases with humidity.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Laplace's formula for the speed of sound in an ideal gas is:
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}}$$
where $\gamma = C_p/C_v$ is the adiabatic index, $P$ is pressure, and $\rho$ is density.

(a) Independence of Pressure:
From the ideal gas equation: $PV = nRT = \frac{m}{M}RT \implies P = \frac{\rho}{M}RT \implies \frac{P}{\rho} = \frac{RT}{M}$.
Substitute into Laplace's formula:
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma R T}{M}}$$
At a constant temperature $T$, if pressure $P$ increases, the gas is compressed proportionally so that density $\rho$ increases by the exact same factor (Boyle’s Law: $P/\rho = \text{constant}$). Therefore, the ratio $P/\rho$ remains unchanged, making the **speed of sound completely independent of pressure** at constant temperature.

(b) Dependence on Temperature:
From $v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma R T}{M}}$, we observe:
$$v \propto \sqrt{T}$$
The speed of sound is directly proportional to the square root of the absolute temperature ($T$ in Kelvin). As temperature rises, molecular thermal velocity increases, causing acoustic pressure disturbances to propagate faster.

(c) Dependence on Humidity:
Molar mass of dry air ($M_{\text{dry}} \approx 29\text{ g mol}^{-1}$) is greater than the molar mass of water vapour ($M_{\text{vapour}} = 18\text{ g mol}^{-1}$).
When air becomes humid, low-density water vapour molecules displace heavier nitrogen ($N_2 = 28$) and oxygen ($O_2 = 32$) molecules.
Consequently, the density of moist air ($\rho_{\text{moist}}$) is **less than** the density of dry air ($\rho_{\text{dry}}$) at the same temperature and pressure:
$$\rho_{\text{moist}} < \rho_{\text{dry}}$$
Since $v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}} \implies v \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}$, **the speed of sound in moist air is greater than in dry air** ($v_{\text{moist}} > v_{\text{dry}}$). Hence, sound travels faster in humid air.

(b) Proof of temperature dependence $v \propto \sqrt{T}$1 Mark
(c) Humidity explanation: $\rho_{\text{moist}} < \rho_{\text{dry}} \implies v_{\text{moist}} > v_{\text{dry}}$1 Mark
Question 14.5
NCERT Exercise
You have learnt that a travelling wave in one dimension is represented by a function $y = f(x, t)$ where $x$ and $t$ must appear in the combination $x - vt$ or $x + vt$, i.e. $y = f(x \pm vt)$. Is the converse true? Examine if the following functions for $y$ can possibly represent a travelling wave:
(a) $(x - vt)^2$
(b) $\log\left[\frac{x + vt}{x_0}\right]$
(c) $\frac{1}{x + vt}$
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Analysis of the Converse Statement:
No, the converse is NOT true. Every travelling wave must have the form $y = f(x \pm vt)$, but NOT every function of the form $f(x \pm vt)$ represents a physical travelling wave.

Physical Criterion for a Wave Function:
A mathematical function can represent a real, physical wave **if and only if it remains finite, single-valued, and bounded for all values of position $x$ and time $t$**. If a function diverges to $\pm \infty$ at any point or at infinity, it cannot represent a physically realizable wave because displacement and energy cannot become infinite.

(a) $y = (x - vt)^2$:
• As $x \to \infty$ or $t \to \infty$, $y \to \infty$.
• The function is unbounded.
• **Cannot represent a travelling wave.**

(b) $y = \log\left[\frac{x + vt}{x_0}\right]$:
• At $x + vt = 0$, $y \to -\infty$; and as $x + vt \to \infty$, $y \to +\infty$.
• The function is unbounded.
• **Cannot represent a travelling wave.**

(c) $y = \frac{1}{x + vt}$:
• At $x + vt = 0$ (i.e. $x = -vt$), the denominator vanishes and $y \to \infty$.
• The function blows up to infinity at the wavefront.
• **Cannot represent a travelling wave.**

$$\mathbf{\text{Conclusion: Converse is false. None of the three functions represent a travelling wave because all are unbounded.}}$$

Evaluation of (a) and (b) showing divergence as $x, t \to \infty$1 Mark
Evaluation of (c) showing singular divergence at $x = -vt$1 Mark
Question 14.6
NCERT Exercise
A bat emits ultrasonic sound of frequency $1000\text{ kHz}$ in air. If the sound meets a water surface, what is the wavelength of (a) the reflected sound, (b) the transmitted sound? Speed of sound in air is $340\text{ m s}^{-1}$ and in water $1486\text{ m s}^{-1}$.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given Data:
• Frequency of ultrasonic sound $\nu = 1000\text{ kHz} = 10^6\text{ Hz}$ (constant across reflection and refraction)
• Speed of sound in air $v_{\text{air}} = 340\text{ m s}^{-1}$
• Speed of sound in water $v_{\text{water}} = 1486\text{ m s}^{-1}$

Fundamental Principle:
Frequency is a characteristic of the source and remains **strictly invariant** when a wave enters a new medium or reflects. However, wave speed and wavelength change in proportion: $\lambda = v/\nu$.

(a) Wavelength of the reflected sound (in air):
The reflected sound propagates back into air with speed $v_{\text{air}} = 340\text{ m s}^{-1}$:
$$\lambda_r = \frac{v_{\text{air}}}{\nu} = \frac{340\text{ m s}^{-1}}{10^6\text{ Hz}} = \mathbf{3.4 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m} = 0.34\text{ mm}}$$

(b) Wavelength of the transmitted sound (in water):
The transmitted sound propagates into water with speed $v_{\text{water}} = 1486\text{ m s}^{-1}$:
$$\lambda_t = \frac{v_{\text{water}}}{\nu} = \frac{1486\text{ m s}^{-1}}{10^6\text{ Hz}} = \mathbf{1.486 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m} = 1.49\text{ mm}}$$

$$\mathbf{\text{Summary: } (a)\; \lambda_{\text{reflected}} = 0.34\text{ mm},\; (b)\; \lambda_{\text{transmitted}} = 1.49\text{ mm.}}$$

Question 14.8
NCERT Exercise
A transverse harmonic wave on a string is described by $y(x, t) = 3.0 \sin(36 t + 0.018 x + \pi/4)$ where $x$ and $y$ are in $\text{cm}$ and $t$ in $\text{s}$. The positive direction of $x$ is from left to right.
(a) Is this a travelling wave or a stationary wave? If it is travelling, what are the speed and direction of its propagation?
(b) What are its amplitude and frequency?
(c) What is the initial phase at the origin?
(d) What is the least distance between two successive crests in the wave?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
The given wave equation is:
$$y(x, t) = 3.0 \sin\left(36 t + 0.018 x + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)$$
Compare with the standard harmonic travelling wave equation: $y(x, t) = a \sin(\omega t + k x + \phi)$.

(a) Nature, speed, and direction of propagation:
• It is a travelling (progressive) wave.
• Since the terms $\omega t$ and $kx$ have the same sign ($+$), the wave travels along the negative $x$-direction (from right to left).
• Speed of wave: $v = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{36\text{ rad s}^{-1}}{0.018\text{ rad cm}^{-1}} = \mathbf{2000\text{ cm s}^{-1} = 20\text{ m s}^{-1}}$.

(b) Amplitude and Frequency:
• Amplitude: $\mathbf{a = 3.0\text{ cm} = 0.03\text{ m}}$.
• Angular frequency: $\omega = 36\text{ rad s}^{-1} \implies \nu = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{36}{2 \times 3.1416} = \mathbf{5.73\text{ Hz}}$.

(c) Initial Phase at the origin ($x = 0, t = 0$):
$$\mathbf{\phi = \frac{\pi}{4}\text{ rad} = 45^\circ}$$

(d) Least distance between two successive crests (Wavelength $\lambda$):
The distance between two successive crests is the wavelength $\lambda$:
$$k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \implies \lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} = \frac{2 \times 3.1416}{0.018\text{ cm}^{-1}} = \frac{6.2832}{0.018} = \mathbf{349.07\text{ cm} \approx 3.49\text{ m}}$$

$$\mathbf{\text{Summary: } (a)\; v = 20\text{ m/s (right to left)},\; (b)\; a = 3\text{ cm}, \nu = 5.73\text{ Hz},\; (c)\; \phi = \pi/4,\; (d)\; \lambda = 3.49\text{ m.}}$$

(b) Amplitude $a = 3.0\text{ cm}$ and frequency $\nu = 5.73\text{ Hz}$1 Mark
(c) Initial phase $\phi = \pi/4\text{ rad}$1 Mark
(d) Wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/k = 349\text{ cm} = 3.49\text{ m}$1 Mark

For the wave described in Exercise 14.8, plot the displacement ($y$) versus ($t$) graphs for $x = 0, 2$ and $4\text{ cm}$. What are the shapes of these graphs? In which aspects do the oscillatory motion in travelling wave differ from one point to another: amplitude, frequency or phase?

✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
From Exercise 14.8: $y(x, t) = 3.0 \sin(36 t + 0.018 x + \pi/4)$ (cm, s).

• For $x = 4\text{ cm}$: $y(4, t) = 3.0 \sin(36 t + 0.072 + \pi/4) = 3.0 \sin(36 t + 0.857)$

All three plots are identical sinusoidal (harmonic) wave curves having:
• Same Amplitude: $a = 3.0\text{ cm}$
• Same Time Period: $T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac{2\pi}{36} = \frac{\pi}{18}\text{ s} \approx 0.175\text{ s}$ (same frequency $\nu = 5.73\text{ Hz}$)

For the travelling harmonic wave $y(x, t) = 2.0 \cos 2\pi (10t - 0.0080 x + 0.35)$ where $x$ and $y$ are in $\text{cm}$ and $t$ in $\text{s}$. Calculate the phase difference between oscillatory motion of two points separated by a distance of:
(a) $4\text{ m}$,
(b) $0.5\text{ m}$,
(c) $\lambda/2$,
(d) $3\lambda/4$.

$$y(x, t) = 2.0 \cos[2\pi(10t - 0.0080x + 0.35)] = 2.0 \cos(20\pi t - 0.016\pi x + 0.70\pi)$$

Question 14.11
NCERT Exercise
The transverse displacement of a string (clamped at its both ends) is given by $y(x, t) = 0.06 \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{3} x\right) \cos(120\pi t)$ where $x$ and $y$ are in $\text{m}$ and $t$ in $\text{s}$. The length of the string is $1.5\text{ m}$ and its mass is $3.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ kg}$.
(a) Does the function represent a travelling wave or a stationary wave?
(b) Interpret the wave as a superposition of two waves travelling in opposite directions. What are the wavelength, frequency, and speed of each wave?
(c) Determine the tension in the string.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given: $y(x, t) = 0.06 \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{3} x\right) \cos(120\pi t)$ (m, s), $L = 1.5\text{ m}$, $M = 3.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ kg}$.

(a) Nature of the wave:
• The spatial dependence ($x$) and temporal dependence ($t$) appear in separate trigonometric factors $\sin(kx)$ and $\cos(\omega t)$.
• Therefore, this function represents a stationary (standing) wave.

(b) Superposition Interpretation:
Using the identity $2\sin A \cos B = \sin(A + B) + \sin(A - B)$:
$$y(x, t) = 0.03 \left[\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{3} x + 120\pi t\right) + \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{3} x - 120\pi t\right)\right]$$
This is the superposition of two identical travelling waves moving in opposite directions ($+x$ and $-x$):
• Propagation constant $k = \frac{2\pi}{3}\text{ rad m}^{-1} \implies \lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{2\pi/3} = \mathbf{3.0\text{ m}}$
• Angular frequency $\omega = 120\pi\text{ rad s}^{-1} \implies \nu = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{120\pi}{2\pi} = \mathbf{60\text{ Hz}}$
• Speed of each wave: $v = \nu \lambda = 60\text{ s}^{-1} \times 3.0\text{ m} = \mathbf{180\text{ m s}^{-1}}$ (or $v = \omega/k = 120\pi / (2\pi/3) = 180\text{ m/s}$)

(c) Tension in the string ($T$):
• Linear mass density: $\mu = \frac{M}{L} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ kg}}{1.5\text{ m}} = 2.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ kg m}^{-1}$.
• From wave speed $v = \sqrt{T/\mu} \implies T = \mu v^2$:
$$T = (2.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ kg m}^{-1}) \times (180\text{ m s}^{-1})^2 = 0.02 \times 32400 = \mathbf{648\text{ N}}$$

$$\mathbf{\text{Summary: } (a)\; \text{Stationary},\; (b)\; \lambda = 3\text{ m},\; \nu = 60\text{ Hz},\; v = 180\text{ m/s},\; (c)\; T = 648\text{ N.}}$$

Question 14.13
NCERT Exercise
Given below are some functions of $x$ and $t$ to represent the displacement (transverse or longitudinal) of an elastic wave. State which of these represent (i) a travelling wave, (ii) a stationary wave or (iii) none at all:
(a) $y = 2\cos(3x) \sin(10t)$
(b) $y = 2\sqrt{x - vt}$
(c) $y = 3\sin(5x - 0.5t) + 4\cos(5x - 0.5t)$
(d) $y = \cos x \sin t + \cos 2x \sin 2t$
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Classification Criteria:
• A travelling wave has the form $f(kx \pm \omega t)$ and must remain finite and bounded for all $x, t$.
• A stationary wave consists of factored space and time harmonic terms: $\sin(kx) \cos(\omega t)$ or $\cos(kx) \sin(\omega t)$.
• If it is unbounded or cannot be expressed as a wave mode, it represents none at all.

(a) $y = 2\cos(3x) \sin(10t)$:
• The spatial term $\cos(3x)$ and temporal term $\sin(10t)$ appear as separate multiplicative factors.
• **Represents a Stationary Wave.**

(b) $y = 2\sqrt{x - vt}$:
• As $x \to \infty$, $y \to \infty$. Furthermore, for $x < vt$, the expression under the square root becomes negative, yielding imaginary displacements.
• **Represents None at all (Not a physical wave).**

(c) $y = 3\sin(5x - 0.5t) + 4\cos(5x - 0.5t)$:
• Both terms have the identical phase argument $(5x - 0.5t)$.
• Can be written as a single harmonic progressive wave: $y = 5 \sin(5x - 0.5t + \theta)$ where $\tan\theta = 4/3$.
• **Represents a Travelling Wave** moving in the positive $x$-direction with speed $v = \frac{0.5}{5} = 0.1\text{ m/s}$.

(d) $y = \cos x \sin t + \cos 2x \sin 2t$:
• This is a superposition of two separate stationary waves with different spatial wavelengths ($k_1 = 1, k_2 = 2$) and different frequencies ($\omega_1 = 1, \omega_2 = 2$).
• While it represents a stationary vibration pattern of a bounded medium, it is a combination rather than a single simple stationary wave mode.
• **Represents a Stationary Wave (Superposition of two standing modes).**

(a) Correctly classified as Stationary Wave0.5 Mark
(b) Correctly classified as None at all (unbounded/imaginary)1 Mark
(c) Reduced to $5\sin(5x-0.5t+\theta)$ and classified as Travelling Wave1 Mark

Question 14.14
NCERT Exercise
A wire stretched between two rigid supports vibrates in its fundamental mode with a frequency of $45\text{ Hz}$. The mass of the wire is $3.5 \times 10^{-2}\text{ kg}$ and its linear mass density is $4.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ kg m}^{-1}$. What is (a) the speed of a transverse wave on the string, and (b) the tension in the wire?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given Data:
• Fundamental frequency $\nu_1 = 45\text{ Hz}$
• Total mass of wire $M = 3.5 \times 10^{-2}\text{ kg}$
• Linear mass density $\mu = 4.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ kg m}^{-1}$

1. Length of the wire ($L$):
$$L = \frac{M}{\mu} = \frac{3.5 \times 10^{-2}\text{ kg}}{4.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ kg m}^{-1}} = \frac{3.5}{4.0} = \mathbf{0.875\text{ m}}$$

2. (a) Speed of a transverse wave ($v$):
In the fundamental mode of a string fixed at both ends, the string forms one single loop of length $L = \frac{\lambda_1}{2} \implies \lambda_1 = 2L = 2 \times 0.875\text{ m} = 1.75\text{ m}$.
$$v = \nu_1 \lambda_1 = (45\text{ s}^{-1}) \times (1.75\text{ m}) = \mathbf{78.75\text{ m s}^{-1}}$$

3. (b) Tension in the wire ($T$):
From $v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} \implies T = \mu v^2$:
$$T = (4.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ kg m}^{-1}) \times (78.75\text{ m s}^{-1})^2$$
$$T = 0.04 \times 6201.56 = \mathbf{248.06\text{ N} \approx 248\text{ N}}$$

$$\mathbf{\text{Summary: } (a)\; \text{Wave speed } v = 78.75\text{ m s}^{-1},\; (b)\; \text{Tension } T = 248\text{ N.}}$$

(a) Evaluating wave speed $v = \nu\lambda = 78.75\text{ m/s}$1 Mark
(b) Evaluating tension $T = \mu v^2 = 248\text{ N}$1 Mark
Question 14.15
NCERT Exercise
A metre-long tube open at one end, with a movable piston at the other end, shows resonance with a fixed frequency source (a tuning fork of frequency $340\text{ Hz}$) when the tube length is $25.5\text{ cm}$ or $79.3\text{ cm}$. Estimate the speed of sound in air at the temperature of the experiment. The edge effects may be neglected.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given Data:
• Frequency of tuning fork $\nu = 340\text{ Hz}$
• First resonant air column length $L_1 = 25.5\text{ cm} = 0.255\text{ m}$
• Second resonant air column length $L_2 = 79.3\text{ cm} = 0.793\text{ m}$

1. Resonance in a closed pipe:
A pipe closed at one end (by the piston) resonates at odd multiples of quarter wavelengths:
• First resonance (Fundamental mode): $L_1 + e = \frac{\lambda}{4}$
• Second resonance (First overtone): $L_2 + e = \frac{3\lambda}{4}$
where $e$ is the end correction at the open mouth.

2. Eliminating end effects:
Subtracting the two resonant equations eliminates $e$ completely:
$$L_2 - L_1 = \frac{3\lambda}{4} - \frac{\lambda}{4} = \frac{\lambda}{2}$$
$$\frac{\lambda}{2} = 79.3\text{ cm} - 25.5\text{ cm} = 53.8\text{ cm} = 0.538\text{ m}$$
$$\lambda = 2 \times 0.538\text{ m} = \mathbf{1.076\text{ m}}$$

3. Speed of sound in air ($v$):
$$v = \nu \lambda = (340\text{ Hz}) \times (1.076\text{ m}) = \mathbf{365.84\text{ m s}^{-1} \approx 366\text{ m s}^{-1}}$$

$$\mathbf{\text{The estimated speed of sound in air is approximately } 366\text{ m s}^{-1}\text{.}}$$

Question 14.16
NCERT Exercise
A steel rod $100\text{ cm}$ long is clamped at its middle. The fundamental frequency of longitudinal vibrations of the rod is given to be $2.53\text{ kHz}$. What is the speed of sound in steel?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given Data:
• Length of steel rod $L = 100\text{ cm} = 1.0\text{ m}$
• Fundamental frequency $\nu = 2.53\text{ kHz} = 2.53 \times 10^3\text{ Hz} = 2530\text{ Hz}$

1. Boundary Conditions for a Rod Clamped at Middle:
• Since the rod is rigidly clamped at its centre, the centre is a **displacement node ($N$)**.
• Both ends of the rod are free to vibrate, so both ends are **displacement antinodes ($A$)**.
• In the fundamental mode, the rod has an antinode at each end and one node at the centre ($A - N - A$).
• The distance between two consecutive antinodes is $\frac{\lambda}{2}$:
$$L = \frac{\lambda}{2} \implies \lambda = 2L = 2 \times 1.0\text{ m} = \mathbf{2.0\text{ m}}$$

2. Speed of Sound in Steel ($v$):
$$v = \nu \lambda = (2.53 \times 10^3\text{ s}^{-1}) \times (2.0\text{ m}) = \mathbf{5.06 \times 10^3\text{ m s}^{-1} = 5060\text{ m s}^{-1} = 5.06\text{ km s}^{-1}}$$

$$\mathbf{\text{The speed of sound in steel is } 5.06 \times 10^3\text{ m s}^{-1} \text{ (or } 5.06\text{ km s}^{-1}\text{).}}$$

Question 14.17
NCERT Exercise
A pipe $20\text{ cm}$ long is closed at one end. Which harmonic mode of the pipe is resonantly excited by a $430\text{ Hz}$ source? Will the same source be in resonance with the pipe if both ends are open? (speed of sound in air is $340\text{ m s}^{-1}$).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given Data:
• Length of pipe $L = 20\text{ cm} = 0.20\text{ m}$
• Source frequency $\nu = 430\text{ Hz}$
• Speed of sound $v = 340\text{ m s}^{-1}$

1. Pipe Closed at One End:
A closed organ pipe supports only **odd harmonics** ($n = 1, 3, 5, \dots$):
$$\nu_n = (2m - 1) \frac{v}{4L} \quad (m = 1, 2, 3, \dots)$$
• Fundamental frequency ($m = 1$, 1st harmonic):
$$\nu_1 = \frac{v}{4L} = \frac{340\text{ m s}^{-1}}{4 \times 0.20\text{ m}} = \frac{340}{0.80} = \mathbf{425\text{ Hz}}$$
• Next harmonic ($m = 2$, 3rd harmonic):
$$\nu_3 = 3 \times 425 = 1275\text{ Hz}$$
Since the source frequency $430\text{ Hz}$ is very close to $425\text{ Hz}$ (well within resonance bandwidth), **the pipe resonates in its fundamental mode (1st harmonic)**.

2. Pipe Open at Both Ends:
An open organ pipe supports **all integer harmonics** ($n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$):
$$\nu_n' = n \frac{v}{2L}$$
• Fundamental frequency ($n = 1$):
$$\nu_1' = \frac{v}{2L} = \frac{340\text{ m s}^{-1}}{2 \times 0.20\text{ m}} = \frac{340}{0.40} = \mathbf{850\text{ Hz}}$$
• Second harmonic ($n = 2$): $\nu_2' = 2 \times 850 = 1700\text{ Hz}$.
Since the fundamental resonant frequency is $850\text{ Hz}$, the source frequency $430\text{ Hz}$ is roughly half of the lowest possible resonance.
Therefore, **the same source will NOT be in resonance with the open pipe**.

$$\mathbf{\text{Conclusion: Closed pipe resonates in fundamental mode (1st harmonic); open pipe will NOT resonate.}}$$

Question 14.18
NCERT Exercise
Two sitar strings A and B playing the note ‘Ga’ are slightly out of tune and produce beats of frequency $6\text{ Hz}$. The tension in the string A is slightly reduced and the beat frequency is found to reduce to $3\text{ Hz}$. If the original frequency of A is $324\text{ Hz}$, what is the frequency of B?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given Data:
• Original frequency of string A: $\nu_A = 324\text{ Hz}$
• Initial beat frequency: $\nu_b = 6\text{ Hz}$

1. Possible values for frequency of B ($\nu_B$):
$$\nu_b = |\nu_A - \nu_B| = 6\text{ Hz}$$
This yields two possibilities for $\nu_B$:
• Possibility 1: $\nu_B = \nu_A + 6 = 324 + 6 = 330\text{ Hz}$
• Possibility 2: $\nu_B = \nu_A - 6 = 324 - 6 = 318\text{ Hz}$

2. Analyzing the Effect of Reducing Tension in String A:
The fundamental frequency of a stretched string is given by $\nu = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} \implies \nu \propto \sqrt{T}$.
When tension in string A is reduced, its frequency $\nu_A$ **decreases** (say from $324\text{ Hz}$ down to $321\text{ Hz}$).

• If $\nu_B$ were $330\text{ Hz}$:
As $\nu_A$ drops below $324\text{ Hz}$, the frequency difference $|\nu_A - \nu_B| = |321 - 330| = 9\text{ Hz}$ (beat frequency would **increase**). But the problem states the beat frequency reduced to $3\text{ Hz}$, so $\nu_B = 330\text{ Hz}$ is ruled out.

• If $\nu_B$ is $318\text{ Hz}$:
As $\nu_A$ drops from $324\text{ Hz}$ to $321\text{ Hz}$, the new difference is $|321 - 318| = 3\text{ Hz}$, which exactly matches the observation.

$$\mathbf{\text{The frequency of string B is } 318\text{ Hz.}}$$

Question 14.19
NCERT Exercise
Explain why (or how):
(a) In a sound wave, a displacement node is a pressure antinode and vice versa.
(b) Bats can ascertain distances, directions, nature, and sizes of the obstacles without any “eyes”.
(c) A violin note and a sitar note may have the same frequency, yet we can distinguish between the two notes.
(d) Solids can support both longitudinal and transverse waves, but only longitudinal waves can propagate in gases.
(e) The shape of a pulse gets distorted during propagation in a dispersive medium.
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
(a) Displacement Node vs Pressure Antinode:
• A displacement node is a point where medium particles have zero displacement from equilibrium. However, particles on either side move towards this point (creating compression) or away from it (creating rarefaction). Thus, the strain $\Delta V/V$ and pressure variation $\Delta P = -B \frac{dy}{dx}$ are **maximum**, making it a **pressure antinode**.
• Conversely, at a displacement antinode, particles oscillate with maximum amplitude in phase with neighboring particles, resulting in zero compression/rarefaction gradient ($\,dy/dx = 0$), which corresponds to zero pressure variation (**pressure node**).

(b) Bat Echolocation:
• Bats emit high-frequency ultrasonic clicks ($20\text{ kHz} - 100\text{ kHz}$) with very small wavelengths (millimetre scale).
• By timing the echo return delay $\Delta t$, they calculate distance $d = \frac{v \Delta t}{2}$.
• By detecting phase and amplitude differences between both ears, they resolve angular direction.
• By analyzing the frequency spectrum and absorption of the reflected wave, they determine obstacle nature and texture.

(c) Timbre of Musical Instruments:
• Even when a violin and a sitar play the identical fundamental note (same frequency $\nu_1$), they produce different numbers, relative strengths, and harmonic amplitudes of **overtones** (higher harmonics).
• The superposition of these distinct overtones creates a unique composite waveform, giving each instrument its characteristic **quality (timbre)**.

(d) Wave Propagation in Solids vs Gases:
• Transverse waves require a restoring shear stress (resistance to change of shape), which depends on the **shear modulus ($G$)**.
• Solids possess both shear modulus ($G > 0$) and bulk modulus ($B > 0$), so they support both transverse and longitudinal waves.
• Gases and fluids possess **zero shear modulus** ($G = 0$) because fluid layers slide past each other without resistance. They only resist volume compression ($B > 0$), allowing **only longitudinal waves** to propagate.

(e) Pulse Distortion in a Dispersive Medium:
• A wave pulse is composed of a Fourier packet of multiple harmonic waves of differing wavelengths and frequencies.
• In a dispersive medium, wave velocity depends on frequency ($v = f(\lambda)$).
• Different frequency components travel at different speeds, spreading out over time and altering their relative phase alignment, which **distorts the shape of the pulse**.
