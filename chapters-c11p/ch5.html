Question 5.1
NCERT Exercise
The sign of work done by a force on a body is important to understand. State carefully if the following quantities are positive or negative:
(a) work done by a man in lifting a bucket out of a well by means of a rope tied to the bucket.
(b) work done by gravitational force in the above case,
(c) work done by friction on a body sliding down an inclined plane,
(d) work done by an applied force on a body moving on a rough horizontal plane with uniform velocity,
(e) work done by the resistive force of air on a vibrating pendulum in bringing it to rest.
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition of Work: $W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = F d \cos \theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between force $\vec{F}$ and displacement $\vec{d}$.
• If $0^\circ \le \theta < 90^\circ$, work is positive ($+W$).
• If $90^\circ < \theta \le 180^\circ$, work is negative ($-W$).

• (a) Work done by the man: The muscular lifting force exerted by the man is vertically upwards, and the displacement of the bucket is also vertically upwards ($\theta = 0^\circ$). Therefore, the work done is positive.

• (b) Work done by gravitational force: The gravitational force acts vertically downwards, while displacement of the bucket is vertically upwards ($\theta = 180^\circ$). $\cos 180^\circ = -1$. Therefore, the work done is negative.

• (c) Work done by friction on sliding body: As the body slides downwards along the inclined plane, the kinetic friction force acts up the incline, opposing the motion ($\theta = 180^\circ$). Therefore, the work done is negative.

• (d) Work done by applied force: The applied force acts in the direction of motion to maintain uniform velocity against friction ($\theta = 0^\circ$). Therefore, the work done is positive.

• (e) Work done by resistive air drag on pendulum: The air drag opposes the instantaneous velocity and motion of the pendulum bob at all points of its path ($\theta = 180^\circ$). Therefore, the work done is negative.

Question 5.2
NCERT Exercise
A body of mass $2\text{ kg}$ initially at rest moves under the action of an applied horizontal force of $7\text{ N}$ on a table with coefficient of kinetic friction $= 0.1$. Compute the
(a) work done by the applied force in $10\text{ s}$,
(b) work done by friction in $10\text{ s}$,
(c) work done by the net force on the body in $10\text{ s}$,
(d) change in kinetic energy of the body in $10\text{ s}$, and interpret your results. (Take $g = 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$).
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given: $m = 2\text{ kg}$, $u = 0$, $F = 7\text{ N}$, $\mu_k = 0.1$, $t = 10\text{ s}$, $g = 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$.

1. Forces and Acceleration:
Normal reaction $N = mg = 2 \times 9.8 = 19.6\text{ N}$.
Kinetic friction force: $f_k = \mu_k N = 0.1 \times 19.6 = 1.96\text{ N}$.
Net accelerating force: $F_{net} = F - f_k = 7 - 1.96 = 5.04\text{ N}$.
Acceleration: $a = \frac{F_{net}}{m} = \frac{5.04\text{ N}}{2\text{ kg}} = 2.52\text{ m s}^{-2}$.

2. Displacement in $10\text{ s}$ ($s$):
$$s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2 = 0 + \frac{1}{2}(2.52)(10)^2 = 1.26 \times 100 = 126\text{ m}$$

• (a) Work done by applied force:
$$W_F = F \times s = 7\text{ N} \times 126\text{ m} = \mathbf{882\text{ J}}$$

• (b) Work done by friction:
$$W_f = -f_k \times s = -1.96\text{ N} \times 126\text{ m} = \mathbf{-246.96\text{ J} \approx -247\text{ J}}$$

• (c) Work done by net force:
$$W_{net} = F_{net} \times s = 5.04\text{ N} \times 126\text{ m} = \mathbf{635.04\text{ J} \approx 635\text{ J}}$$
(Alternatively: $W_{net} = W_F + W_f = 882 - 246.96 = 635.04\text{ J}$).

• (d) Change in kinetic energy ($\Delta K$):
Velocity at $t = 10\text{ s}$: $v = u + at = 0 + 2.52(10) = 25.2\text{ m s}^{-1}$.
$$\Delta K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - 0 = \frac{1}{2}(2)(25.2)^2 = (25.2)^2 = \mathbf{635.04\text{ J}}$$

• Interpretation:
$$\mathbf{W_{net} = \Delta K = 635.04\text{ J}}$$ This perfectly verifies the Work-Energy Theorem: the net work done by all forces equals the change in kinetic energy of the body.

Question 5.3
NCERT Exercise
Given in Fig. 5.11 are examples of some potential energy functions in one dimension. The total energy of the particle is indicated by a cross on the ordinate axis. In each case, specify the regions, if any, in which the particle cannot be found for the given energy. Also, indicate the minimum total energy the particle must have in each case. Think of simple physical contexts for which these potential energy shapes are relevant.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Fundamental Principle:
Total mechanical energy is $E = K + V(x)$, where kinetic energy $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \ge 0$.
Therefore, a classical particle can exist only in regions where $E \ge V(x)$.
$$\mathbf{\text{The particle cannot exist in regions where } V(x) > E \text{ (as } K < 0 \text{ is physically impossible).}}$$
The minimum total energy $E_{min}$ is the global minimum of the potential energy curve $V(x)_{min}$.

• (a) Potential well with $V = 0$ for $0 < x < a$ and $V = V_0$ elsewhere:
- If $E < V_0$, the particle cannot be found in regions $x < 0$ and $x > a$.
- It is confined to the region $0 \le x \le a$.
- Minimum total energy $E_{min} = 0$.
- Physical context: A particle trapped in a 1D finite potential box (or a bead on a wire bounded between two stops).

• (b) Potential with $V > E$ everywhere:
- Since $V(x) > E$ for all values of $x$, the particle cannot exist anywhere.
- Minimum total energy $E_{min} = V_{min}$.

• (c) Symmetric potential well with barriers:
- The particle cannot exist where $V(x) > E$, i.e., in the barrier regions $x < -b$ and $x > a$.
- It is confined between $-a \le x \le a$ (or within the central well).
- Minimum total energy $E_{min} = -V_1$.

• (d) Harmonic potential well $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$:
- Particle cannot be found outside turning points $|x| > x_0$ where $\frac{1}{2}kx_0^2 = E$.
- Minimum total energy $E_{min} = 0$ at $x = 0$.
- Physical context: A mass attached to a spring oscillating in SHM.

Question 5.4
NCERT Exercise
The potential energy function for a particle executing simple harmonic motion is given by $V(x) = k x^2 / 2$, where $k$ is the force constant of the oscillator. For $k = 0.5\text{ N m}^{-1}$, the graph of $V(x)$ versus $x$ is shown in Fig. 5.12. Show that a particle of total energy $1\text{ J}$ moving under this potential must ‘turn back’ when it reaches $x = \pm 2\text{ m}$.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given:
• Potential energy function: $V(x) = \frac{1}{2} k x^2$
• Force constant: $k = 0.5\text{ N m}^{-1}$
• Total mechanical energy: $E = 1\text{ J}$

1. Conservation of Mechanical Energy:
$$E = K + V(x) = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + \frac{1}{2}kx^2$$
At the turning points, the particle instantaneously comes to rest to reverse its direction, so kinetic energy becomes zero ($K = 0$ and $v = 0$):
$$E = V(x) \implies 1\text{ J} = \frac{1}{2}(0.5) x^2$$
$$1 = 0.25 x^2 \implies x^2 = \frac{1}{0.25} = 4$$
$$x = \pm \sqrt{4} = \mathbf{\pm 2\text{ m}}$$

2. Physical Reason for Turning Back:
For any position $|x| > 2\text{ m}$, the potential energy $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}(0.5)x^2 > 1\text{ J} = E$.
This would require kinetic energy $K = E - V(x) < 0$, which is physically impossible for a classical particle ($v^2 < 0$).
$$\mathbf{\text{Therefore, the particle must turn back at } x = +2\text{ m} \text{ and } x = -2\text{ m}.}$$

Question 5.5
NCERT Exercise
Answer the following:
(a) The casing of a rocket in flight burns up due to friction. At whose expense is the heat energy required for burning obtained? The rocket or the atmosphere?
(b) Comets move around the sun in highly elliptical orbits. The gravitational force on the comet due to the sun is not normal to the comet’s velocity in general. Yet the work done by the gravitational force over every complete orbit of the comet is zero. Why?
(c) An artificial satellite orbiting the earth in very thin atmosphere loses its energy gradually due to dissipation against atmospheric resistance, however small. Why then does its speed increase progressively as it comes closer and closer to the earth?
(d) In Fig. 5.13(i) the man walks $2\text{ m}$ carrying a mass of $15\text{ kg}$ on his hands. In Fig. 5.13(ii), he walks the same distance pulling the rope behind him. The rope goes over a pulley, and a mass of $15\text{ kg}$ hangs at its other end. In which case is the work done greater?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• (a) Rocket casing burning up: The heat energy is produced at the expense of the mechanical energy (kinetic and gravitational potential energy) of the rocket itself. As the rocket slows down slightly due to atmospheric air friction, its kinetic/potential energy is dissipated as thermal energy.

• (b) Zero work over complete comet orbit: The gravitational force is a conservative force. By definition, the work done by any conservative force around any closed loop is identically zero ($\oint \vec{F}_{grav} \cdot d\vec{r} = 0$), regardless of whether the path is circular or highly eccentric elliptical.

• (c) Increasing speed of descending satellite: As the satellite spirals closer to Earth due to atmospheric drag, its orbital radius $r$ decreases. Its gravitational potential energy ($U = -\frac{GMm}{r}$) becomes substantially more negative (decreases). By the Virial Theorem for orbital motion, the orbital velocity is $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$. As $r$ decreases, orbital speed $v$ must increase. Half of the lost potential energy is converted into kinetic energy (speeding it up), while the other half is dissipated as heat against friction.

• (d) Comparison of work done by the man:
- Case (i): The man holds the $15\text{ kg}$ mass vertically upwards, while his displacement is horizontal ($\theta = 90^\circ$). Work done against gravity is $W_1 = F d \cos 90^\circ = \mathbf{0}$.
- Case (ii): The man pulls the rope horizontally, lifting the $15\text{ kg}$ load upwards by $2\text{ m}$. Tension in rope $T = mg = 15 \times 9.8 = 147\text{ N}$. Displacement $d = 2\text{ m}$ in direction of pull ($\theta = 0^\circ$). Work done: $W_2 = T d = 147 \times 2 = \mathbf{294\text{ J}}$.
$$\mathbf{\text{The work done is greater in Case (ii).}}$$
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)

(b) Gravitational force is conservative $\implies \oint F \cdot dr = 0$1 Mark
(c) Orbital speed $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$ increases as radius $r$ shrinks1 Mark
(d) Case (i) $W=0$ (perpendicular), Case (ii) $W=294\text{ J}$, greater in (ii)1 Mark
Question 5.6
NCERT Exercise
Underline the correct alternative:
(a) When a conservative force does positive work on a body, the potential energy of the body increases/decreases/remains unaltered.
(b) Work done by a body against friction always results in a loss of its kinetic/potential energy.
(c) The rate of change of total momentum of a many-particle system is proportional to the external force/sum of the true internal forces on the system.
(d) In an inelastic collision of two bodies, the quantities which do not change after the collision are the total kinetic energy/total linear momentum/total energy of the system of two bodies.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• (a) decreases: The relationship between conservative force and potential energy is $\Delta U = -W_c$. When conservative force does positive work ($W_c > 0$), potential energy decreases ($\Delta U < 0$). For example, when a body falls under gravity, gravity does positive work and gravitational PE decreases.

• (b) kinetic energy: Work done against friction is dissipated as non-recoverable thermal energy, reducing the speed and thus directly causing a loss of kinetic energy.

• (c) external force: By Newton’s Third Law, internal forces between constituent particles cancel out pairwise in vector addition ($\sum \vec{F}_{int} = \mathbf{0}$). Only net external forces can change the total linear momentum of the system: $\frac{d\vec{P}_{total}}{dt} = \vec{F}_{ext}$.

• (d) total linear momentum/total energy: In an inelastic collision, mechanical kinetic energy is not conserved (partially converted into heat, sound, deformation). However, total linear momentum (in absence of external forces) and total energy (by the universal conservation of energy) remain strictly unchanged.

Question 5.7
NCERT Exercise
State if each of the following statements is true or false. Give reasons for your answer.
(a) In an elastic collision of two bodies, the momentum and energy of each body is conserved.
(b) Total energy of a system is always conserved, no matter what internal and external forces on the body are present.
(c) Work done in the motion of a body over a closed loop is zero for every force in nature.
(d) In an inelastic collision, the final kinetic energy is always less than the initial kinetic energy of the system.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• (a) False: In an elastic collision, the total momentum and total kinetic energy of the system are conserved. But individual bodies transfer momentum and kinetic energy to each other during the collision; hence, the momentum and energy of each separate body change.

• (b) True: The universal Law of Conservation of Energy states that the total energy of an isolated system (taking all forms of energy into account—mechanical, heat, chemical, radiation, mass-energy) is strictly invariant.

• (c) False: The work done in a closed loop is zero only for conservative forces (such as gravity, electrostatic force, elastic spring force). For non-conservative dissipative forces like friction or viscous drag, the work done over a closed loop is negative and non-zero ($\oint \vec{f}_k \cdot d\vec{r} < 0$).

• (d) False (in general): Usually in an inelastic collision, kinetic energy is lost as heat/deformation ($K_f < K_i$). However, in "super-elastic" or explosive collisions where chemical or internal potential energy is released (e.g., a compressed spring releasing or an explosive shell bursting), the final kinetic energy can be greater than initial kinetic energy ($K_f > K_i$).

Question 5.8
NCERT Exercise
Answer carefully, with reasons:
(a) In an elastic collision of two billiard balls, is the total kinetic energy of the balls conserved during the short time of collision of the balls (i.e. when they are in contact)?
(b) Is the total linear momentum conserved during the short time of an elastic collision of two balls?
(c) What are the answers to (a) and (b) for an inelastic collision?
(d) If the potential energy of two billiard balls depends only on the separation distance between their centres, is the collision elastic or inelastic? (Note, we are talking here of potential energy corresponding to the force during collision, not gravitational potential energy).
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• (a) No: During the short duration of collision while the balls are in contact, they undergo temporary elastic deformation. Part of the kinetic energy is temporarily stored as elastic potential energy of deformation. Total kinetic energy is conserved only after the balls separate and fully regain their original shape.

• (b) Yes: During the collision, the contact forces between the balls are internal action-reaction pairs ($\vec{F}_{12} = -\vec{F}_{21}$). Since no external horizontal force acts on the system, total linear momentum is conserved at every single instant during the collision.

• (c) For an inelastic collision:
- Answer to (a): No. Kinetic energy is not conserved during or after the collision.
- Answer to (b): Yes. Total linear momentum remains conserved at every instant because internal forces cancel out.

• (d) Elastic collision: If the inter-particle potential energy depends solely on the separation distance $r$ ($V = V(r)$), the interaction force is central and conservative ($\vec{F} = -\frac{dV}{dr}\hat{r}$). In a conservative force field, mechanical energy is fully conserved, meaning the collision is strictly elastic.

Question 5.9
NCERT Exercise
A body is initially at rest. It undergoes one-dimensional motion with constant acceleration. The power delivered to it at time $t$ is proportional to
(i) $t^{1/2}$,
(ii) $t$,
(iii) $t^{3/2}$,
(iv) $t^2$. [Choose the correct alternative].
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Correct Option: (ii) $t$

Derivation:
Given: Initial velocity $u = 0$, constant acceleration $a = \text{constant}$, mass $m = \text{constant}$.
• Applied force: $F = m a = \text{constant}$.
• Velocity at time $t$: $v = u + at = 0 + at = at$.
Instantaneous power delivered is given by:
$$P = F \times v = (ma) \times (at) = m a^2 t$$
Since $m$ and $a$ are constants:
$$P \propto t$$
$$\mathbf{\text{Therefore, the power delivered is directly proportional to } t.}$$

Question 5.10
NCERT Exercise
A body is moving unidirectionally under the influence of a source of constant power. Its displacement in time $t$ is proportional to
(i) $t^{1/2}$,
(ii) $t$,
(iii) $t^{3/2}$,
(iv) $t^2$. [Choose the correct alternative].
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Correct Option: (iii) $t^{3/2}$

Derivation:
Power $P = \text{constant}$.
$$P = F v = \left(m \frac{dv}{dt}\right) v = m v \frac{dv}{dt} = P$$
Integrating both sides with initial condition $v(0) = 0$:
$$m \int_0^v v dv = P \int_0^t dt \implies m \frac{v^2}{2} = P t$$
$$v^2 = \frac{2P}{m} t \implies v = \sqrt{\frac{2P}{m}} t^{1/2}$$
Now, velocity is the rate of displacement: $v = \frac{dx}{dt}$.
$$\frac{dx}{dt} = C t^{1/2} \quad \left(\text{where } C = \sqrt{\frac{2P}{m}}\right)$$
Integrating with respect to time:
$$x = C \int_0^t t^{1/2} dt = C \left[\frac{t^{3/2}}{3/2}\right] = \frac{2C}{3} t^{3/2}$$
$$x \propto t^{3/2}$$
$$\mathbf{\text{Therefore, displacement is proportional to } t^{3/2}.}$$

Question 5.12
NCERT Exercise
An electron and a proton are detected in a cosmic ray experiment, the first with kinetic energy $10\text{ keV}$, and the second with $100\text{ keV}$. Which is faster, the electron or the proton? Obtain the ratio of their speeds. (electron mass $= 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}$, proton mass $= 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$, $1\text{ eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$).
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given:
• Kinetic energy of electron: $K_e = 10\text{ keV} = 10 \times 10^3 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J} = 1.6 \times 10^{-15}\text{ J}$
• Kinetic energy of proton: $K_p = 100\text{ keV} = 100 \times 10^3 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J} = 1.6 \times 10^{-14}\text{ J}$
• Masses: $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}$, $m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$

1. Speed Relation:
$$K = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 \implies v = \sqrt{\frac{2K}{m}}$$

2. Speed of Electron ($v_e$):
$$v_e = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-15}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}} = \sqrt{0.3513 \times 10^{16}} = 0.5927 \times 10^8 = \mathbf{5.93 \times 10^7\text{ m s}^{-1}}$$

3. Speed of Proton ($v_p$):
$$v_p = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-14}}{1.67 \times 10^{-27}}} = \sqrt{1.916 \times 10^{13}} = \mathbf{4.38 \times 10^6\text{ m s}^{-1}}$$

4. Ratio of Speeds:
$$\frac{v_e}{v_p} = \frac{5.93 \times 10^7}{4.38 \times 10^6} \approx \mathbf{13.54}$$

$$\therefore \mathbf{\text{The electron is faster than the proton, by a factor of approximately } 13.5.}$$

Question 5.13
NCERT Exercise
A rain drop of radius $2\text{ mm}$ falls from a height of $500\text{ m}$ above the ground. It falls with decreasing acceleration (due to viscous resistance of the air) until at half its original height, it attains its maximum (terminal) speed, and moves with uniform speed thereafter. What is the work done by the gravitational force on the drop in the first and second half of its journey? What is the work done by the resistive force in the entire journey if its speed on reaching the ground is $10\text{ m s}^{-1}$?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given:
• Radius $r = 2\text{ mm} = 2 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}$
• Height $h = 500\text{ m}$
• Density of water $\rho = 10^3\text{ kg m}^{-3}$
• Terminal speed on reaching ground $v = 10\text{ m s}^{-1}$
• $g = 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$

1. Mass of raindrop ($m$):
$$m = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho = \frac{4}{3} \times 3.1416 \times (2 \times 10^{-3})^3 \times 10^3 = 4.1888 \times 8 \times 10^{-6} \approx 3.351 \times 10^{-5}\text{ kg}$$

2. Work done by gravitational force:
Gravity is constant throughout ($F = mg$). Distance in each half $= h/2 = 250\text{ m}$.
• First half ($W_{g1}$):
$$W_{g1} = mg \left(\frac{h}{2}\right) = 3.351 \times 10^{-5} \times 9.8 \times 250 = \mathbf{0.0821\text{ J}}$$
• Second half ($W_{g2}$):
$$W_{g2} = mg \left(\frac{h}{2}\right) = \mathbf{0.0821\text{ J}}$$
Total work done by gravity over full journey:
$$W_g = W_{g1} + W_{g2} = 2 \times 0.0821 = \mathbf{0.1642\text{ J}}$$

3. Work done by resistive force ($W_r$):
By Work-Energy Theorem for entire journey ($u = 0$, $v = 10\text{ m s}^{-1}$):
$$W_{net} = W_g + W_r = \Delta K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - 0$$
$$\Delta K = \frac{1}{2}(3.351 \times 10^{-5})(10)^2 = 1.6755 \times 10^{-3}\text{ J} = 0.001676\text{ J}$$
$$W_r = \Delta K - W_g = 0.001676 - 0.1642 = \mathbf{-0.1625\text{ J}}$$

$$\therefore W_{g1} = W_{g2} = \mathbf{0.082\text{ J}}, \quad W_r = \mathbf{-0.163\text{ J}}.$$

Question 5.14
NCERT Exercise
A molecule in a gas container hits a horizontal wall with speed $200\text{ m s}^{-1}$ and angle $30^\circ$ with the normal, and rebounds with the same speed. Is momentum conserved in the collision? Is the collision elastic or inelastic?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• Conservation of Linear Momentum:
- For the molecule alone: The component of velocity normal to the wall reverses from $+u \cos 30^\circ$ to $-u \cos 30^\circ$, so the momentum of the molecule changes by $\Delta p = -2 m u \cos 30^\circ$. Its momentum is not conserved individually.
- For the molecule + container wall system: The wall (rigidly connected to the massive container/Earth) exerts a normal force on the molecule, and by Newton’s Third Law, the molecule exerts an equal and opposite force on the wall. Since no external horizontal force acts on the combined system, total linear momentum of the system (molecule + wall) is strictly conserved.

• Elastic or Inelastic:
Since the rebound speed ($200\text{ m s}^{-1}$) is identical to the initial incident speed, the kinetic energy of the molecule before and after the collision is identical ($K_f = K_i = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$).
No kinetic energy is dissipated as heat or permanent deformation.
$$\mathbf{\text{Therefore, the collision is strictly elastic.}}$$

Two identical ball bearings in contact with each other and at rest on a frictionless table are hit head-on by a third ball bearing of the same mass moving initially with a speed $v$. If the collision is elastic, which of the situations shown in Fig. 5.14 is a possible result after collision?
Case 1: Ball 1 comes to rest, balls 2 and 3 move together with speed $v/2$.
Case 2: Balls 1 and 2 come to rest, ball 3 moves off with speed $v$.
Case 3: All three balls move together with speed $v/3$.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Let each ball have mass $m$.
Initial state: Ball 1 moves with speed $v$; Balls 2 and 3 are at rest.
• Initial momentum: $P_i = m v$.
• Initial kinetic energy: $K_i = \frac{1}{2} m v^2$.

For an elastic collision, both momentum and kinetic energy must be conserved:

• Case 1 (Ball 1 stops, 2 and 3 move with $v/2$):
- Momentum: $P_f = (2m)(v/2) = mv = P_i$ (Conserved).
- Kinetic energy: $K_f = \frac{1}{2}(2m)(v/2)^2 = m \frac{v^2}{4} = \frac{1}{4}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}K_i \ne K_i$ (Not conserved!).

• Case 2 (Balls 1 and 2 stop, ball 3 moves with $v$):
- Momentum: $P_f = m v = P_i$ (Conserved).
- Kinetic energy: $K_f = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = K_i$ (Conserved!).

• Case 3 (All three move together with $v/3$):
- Momentum: $P_f = (3m)(v/3) = mv = P_i$ (Conserved).
- Kinetic energy: $K_f = \frac{1}{2}(3m)(v/3)^2 = \frac{3}{18}mv^2 = \frac{1}{6}mv^2 = \frac{1}{3}K_i \ne K_i$ (Not conserved!).

$$\mathbf{\text{Conclusion: Only Case 2 is the physically possible result for an elastic collision.}}$$

A bullet of mass $0.012\text{ kg}$ and horizontal speed $70\text{ m s}^{-1}$ strikes a block of wood of mass $0.4\text{ kg}$ and instantly comes to rest with respect to the block. The block is suspended from the ceiling by means of thin wires. Calculate the height to which the block rises. Also, estimate the amount of heat produced in the block. (Take $g = 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$).
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Given:
• Mass of bullet $m = 0.012\text{ kg}$
• Speed of bullet $u = 70\text{ m s}^{-1}$
• Mass of wooden block $M = 0.4\text{ kg}$
• Total mass after embedding: $M_{total} = M + m = 0.4 + 0.012 = 0.412\text{ kg}$

1. Completely Inelastic Collision (Momentum Conservation):
$$m u = (M + m) V$$
$$V = \frac{m u}{M + m} = \frac{0.012 \times 70}{0.412} = \frac{0.84}{0.412} \approx \mathbf{2.039\text{ m s}^{-1}}$$

2. Height to which block rises ($h$):
By conservation of mechanical energy as the combined block swings upward:
$$\frac{1}{2}(M + m)V^2 = (M + m)gh \implies h = \frac{V^2}{2g}$$
$$h = \frac{(2.039)^2}{2 \times 9.8} = \frac{4.157}{19.6} \approx \mathbf{0.212\text{ m} = 21.2\text{ cm}}$$

3. Heat energy produced in the block ($Q$):
Heat produced equals the loss in mechanical kinetic energy during collision:
• Initial kinetic energy: $K_i = \frac{1}{2} m u^2 = \frac{1}{2}(0.012)(70)^2 = 0.006 \times 4900 = 29.4\text{ J}$.
• Kinetic energy immediately after collision:
$$K_f = \frac{1}{2}(M + m)V^2 = \frac{1}{2}(0.412)(2.039)^2 = 0.206 \times 4.157 = 0.856\text{ J}$$
• Heat produced:
$$Q = K_i - K_f = 29.4 - 0.856 = \mathbf{28.544\text{ J} \approx 28.5\text{ J}}$$

$$\therefore \text{Height of rise } h = \mathbf{21.2\text{ cm}}, \quad \text{Heat produced } Q = \mathbf{28.5\text{ J}}.$$
