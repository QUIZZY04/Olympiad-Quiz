Question 1.1
NCERT Exercise
Fill in the blanks:
(a) The volume of a cube of side $1\text{ cm}$ is equal to ______ $\text{m}^3$.
(b) The surface area of a solid cylinder of radius $2.0\text{ cm}$ and height $10.0\text{ cm}$ is equal to ______ $(\text{mm})^2$.
(c) A vehicle moving with a speed of $18\text{ km h}^{-1}$ covers ______ $\text{m}$ in $1\text{ s}$.
(d) The relative density of lead is $11.3$. Its density is ______ $\text{g cm}^{-3}$ or ______ $\text{kg m}^{-3}$.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• (a) Volume of cube:
Side $a = 1\text{ cm} = 10^{-2}\text{ m}$.
$$\text{Volume } V = a^3 = (10^{-2}\text{ m})^3 = \mathbf{10^{-6}\text{ m}^3}$$
• (b) Total surface area of a solid cylinder:
Radius $r = 2.0\text{ cm} = 20\text{ mm}$, height $h = 10.0\text{ cm} = 100\text{ mm}$.
$$\text{Surface Area } A = 2\pi r(r + h) = 2 \times 3.1416 \times 20 \times (20 + 100) = 40\pi \times 120 = 4800\pi\text{ mm}^2$$
$$A = 4800 \times 3.1416 = 15079.6\text{ mm}^2 = \mathbf{1.5 \times 10^4\text{ (mm)}^2}$$
• (c) Speed conversion:
$$18\text{ km h}^{-1} = 18 \times \frac{5}{18}\text{ m s}^{-1} = 5\text{ m s}^{-1}$$
Distance covered in $1\text{ s} = \text{speed} \times \text{time} = 5\text{ m s}^{-1} \times 1\text{ s} = \mathbf{5\text{ m}}$
• (d) Density of lead:
$$\text{Density} = \text{Relative Density} \times \text{Density of water}$$
In CGS: $11.3 \times 1\text{ g cm}^{-3} = \mathbf{11.3\text{ g cm}^{-3}}$
In SI: $11.3 \times 10^3\text{ kg m}^{-3} = \mathbf{1.13 \times 10^4\text{ kg m}^{-3}}$

Question 1.2
NCERT Exercise
Fill in the blanks by suitable conversion of units:
(a) $1\text{ kg m}^2\text{ s}^{-2} = \text{______ g cm}^2\text{ s}^{-2}$
(b) $1\text{ m} = \text{______ ly}$ (light year)
(c) $3.0\text{ m s}^{-2} = \text{______ km h}^{-2}$
(d) $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N m}^2\text{ kg}^{-2} = \text{______ (cm)}^3\text{ s}^{-2}\text{ g}^{-1}$
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• (a) $1\text{ kg m}^2\text{ s}^{-2}$ (Joule to erg):
$$1\text{ kg} = 10^3\text{ g}, \quad 1\text{ m} = 10^2\text{ cm} \implies 1\text{ m}^2 = 10^4\text{ cm}^2$$
$$1\text{ kg m}^2\text{ s}^{-2} = 10^3\text{ g} \times 10^4\text{ cm}^2 \times 1\text{ s}^{-2} = \mathbf{10^7\text{ g cm}^2\text{ s}^{-2}}$$
• (b) $1\text{ m}$ to light year (ly):
$$1\text{ ly} = \text{speed of light} \times 1\text{ year} = (3 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}) \times (365.25 \times 24 \times 3600\text{ s}) = 9.46 \times 10^{15}\text{ m}$$
$$1\text{ m} = \frac{1}{9.46 \times 10^{15}}\text{ ly} = \mathbf{1.057 \times 10^{-16}\text{ ly}}$$
• (c) $3.0\text{ m s}^{-2}$ to $\text{km h}^{-2}$:
$$1\text{ m} = 10^{-3}\text{ km}, \quad 1\text{ s} = \frac{1}{3600}\text{ h} \implies 1\text{ s}^{-2} = (3600)^2\text{ h}^{-2}$$
$$3.0\text{ m s}^{-2} = 3.0 \times 10^{-3} \times (3600)^2 = 3.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 1.296 \times 10^7 = \mathbf{3.888 \times 10^4\text{ km h}^{-2}}$$
• (d) Universal Gravitational Constant $G$:
$$1\text{ N} = 1\text{ kg m s}^{-2} \implies 1\text{ N m}^2\text{ kg}^{-2} = 1\text{ m}^3\text{ kg}^{-1}\text{ s}^{-2}$$
$$G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\text{ m}^3\text{ kg}^{-1}\text{ s}^{-2} = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times (10^2\text{ cm})^3 \times (10^3\text{ g})^{-1} \times 1\text{ s}^{-2}$$
$$= 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 10^6 \times 10^{-3}\text{ cm}^3\text{ g}^{-1}\text{ s}^{-2} = \mathbf{6.67 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm}^3\text{ s}^{-2}\text{ g}^{-1}}$$

Question 1.3
NCERT Exercise
A calorie is a unit of heat or energy and it equals about $4.2\text{ J}$ where $1\text{ J} = 1\text{ kg m}^2\text{ s}^{-2}$. Suppose we employ a system of units in which the unit of mass equals $\alpha\text{ kg}$, the unit of length equals $\beta\text{ m}$, the unit of time is $\gamma\text{ s}$. Show that a calorie has a magnitude $4.2\,\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-2}\gamma^2$ in terms of the new units.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• Dimensional formula of energy (heat/calorie):
$$[\text{Energy}] = [M^1 L^2 T^{-2}]$$
Comparing with $[M^a L^b T^c]$, we have $a = 1, b = 2, c = -2$.

• System 1 (SI Units):
$n_1 = 4.2$, $M_1 = 1\text{ kg}$, $L_1 = 1\text{ m}$, $T_1 = 1\text{ s}$.

• System 2 (New Units):
$n_2 = ?$, $M_2 = \alpha\text{ kg}$, $L_2 = \beta\text{ m}$, $T_2 = \gamma\text{ s}$.

• Using conversion formula $n_2 = n_1 \left(\frac{M_1}{M_2}\right)^a \left(\frac{L_1}{L_2}\right)^b \left(\frac{T_1}{T_2}\right)^c$:
$$n_2 = 4.2 \left(\frac{1\text{ kg}}{\alpha\text{ kg}}\right)^1 \left(\frac{1\text{ m}}{\beta\text{ m}}\right)^2 \left(\frac{1\text{ s}}{\gamma\text{ s}}\right)^{-2}$$
$$n_2 = 4.2 (\alpha^{-1}) (\beta^{-2}) (\gamma^2) = \mathbf{4.2\,\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-2}\gamma^2}$$
• Hence, $1\text{ calorie} = 4.2\,\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-2}\gamma^2\text{ (new units)}$. (Hence Proved).

Question 1.4
NCERT Exercise
Explain this statement clearly: "To call a dimensional quantity 'large' or 'small' is meaningless without specifying a standard for comparison". In view of this, re-frame the following statements wherever necessary:
(a) atoms are very small things
(b) a jet plane moves with great speed
(c) the mass of Jupiter is very large
(d) the air inside this room contains a large number of molecules
(e) a proton is much more massive than an electron
(f) the speed of sound is much smaller than the speed of light.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• Explanation: A physical quantity possessing dimensions cannot be judged as absolute 'large' or 'small'. A magnitude can only be large or small relative to some chosen reference standard of the same dimensions (e.g., $1\text{ cm}$ is huge compared to an atom $\sim 10^{-10}\text{ m}$, but minuscule compared to the radius of Earth $\sim 10^6\text{ m}$).

• Reframed Statements:
(a) Atoms are very small compared to the tip of a sharp pin or the wavelength of visible light.
(b) A jet plane moves with a speed much greater than that of a superfast express train.
(c) The mass of Jupiter is very large compared to the mass of Earth (about 318 times larger).
(d) The air inside this room contains a large number of molecules compared to the number of molecules in one mole of gas ($N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$).
(e) Already well-framed: A proton is much more massive than an electron (approx. $1836$ times more massive; comparison standard is explicitly specified).
(f) Already well-framed: The speed of sound ($340\text{ m s}^{-1}$) is much smaller than the speed of light ($3 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}$) in air.

Question 1.5
NCERT Exercise
A new unit of length is chosen such that the speed of light in vacuum is unity. What is the distance between the Sun and the Earth in terms of the new unit if light takes $8\text{ min and } 20\text{ s}$ to cover this distance?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• Given speed of light in the new system of units: $c = 1\text{ (new unit of length) s}^{-1}$.
• Time taken by light from Sun to Earth:
$$t = 8\text{ min } 20\text{ s} = (8 \times 60) + 20 = 480 + 20 = 500\text{ s}$$
• Distance $d$ between Sun and Earth:
$$d = \text{speed} \times \text{time} = c \times t$$
$$d = 1\text{ (new unit) s}^{-1} \times 500\text{ s} = \mathbf{500\text{ new units of length}}$$

Question 1.6
NCERT Exercise
Which of the following is the most precise device for measuring length:
(a) a vernier callipers with 20 divisions on the sliding scale
(b) a screw gauge of pitch 1 mm and 100 divisions on the circular scale
(c) an optical instrument that can measure length to within a wavelength of light?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• The precision of a measuring instrument is determined by its Least Count (LC): the smaller the least count, the higher the precision.

• (a) Vernier Callipers:
Assuming $1\text{ MSD} = 1\text{ mm}$, $20\text{ VSD} = 19\text{ MSD}$:
$$LC = 1\text{ MSD} - 1\text{ VSD} = \frac{1}{20}\text{ mm} = 0.05\text{ mm} = 5 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}$$
• (b) Screw Gauge:
$$LC = \frac{\text{Pitch}}{\text{Number of circular divisions}} = \frac{1\text{ mm}}{100} = 0.01\text{ mm} = 10^{-5}\text{ m}$$
• (c) Optical Instrument:
Can measure within a wavelength of visible light $\lambda$ (average $\lambda \approx 6000\text{ AA} = 6 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}$):
$$LC \approx 10^{-7}\text{ m}$$
• Comparing least counts: $10^{-7}\text{ m} < 10^{-5}\text{ m} < 5 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}$.
• Therefore, (c) the optical instrument is the most precise device.

Question 1.7
NCERT Exercise
A student measures the thickness of a human hair by looking at it through a microscope of magnification 100. He makes 20 observations and finds that the average width of the hair in the field of view of the microscope is $3.5\text{ mm}$. What is the estimate on the thickness of hair?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• Given magnification of microscope $m = 100$.
• Observed average apparent width in the field of view $= 3.5\text{ mm}$.
• The linear magnification formula is:
$$m = \frac{\text{Apparent size}}{\text{Real size}}$$
• Therefore, the real thickness of the human hair is:
$$\text{Real thickness} = \frac{\text{Apparent width}}{m} = \frac{3.5\text{ mm}}{100} = \mathbf{0.035\text{ mm}} = \mathbf{3.5 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}}$$

Question 1.8
NCERT Exercise
Answer the following:
(a) You are given a thread and a metre scale. How will you estimate the diameter of the thread?
(b) A screw gauge has a pitch of $1.0\text{ mm}$ and $200$ divisions on the circular scale. Do you think it is possible to increase the accuracy of the screw gauge arbitrarily by increasing the number of divisions on the circular scale?
(c) The mean diameter of a thin brass rod is to be measured by vernier callipers. Why is a set of 100 measurements of the diameter expected to yield a more reliable estimate than a set of 5 measurements only?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• (a) Estimating diameter of thread:
Wrap the thread closely and uniformly around a cylindrical pencil or metre rod such that consecutive turns touch each other without overlapping. Count the number of turns $n$ (say, $n = 50$). Measure the total length $L$ of the wound coil using the metre scale. The diameter $d$ of the thread is estimated by:
$$d = \frac{L}{n}$$
• (b) Increasing circular scale divisions arbitrarily:
No. While increasing divisions theoretically decreases the least count ($LC = \text{pitch}/N$), in practice, human eyesight cannot resolve extremely narrow division marks without optical microscopes. Additionally, mechanical constraints like backlash error, pitch imperfections, and thermal expansion set practical physical limits.

• (c) 100 measurements vs 5 measurements:
Random errors occur randomly in magnitude and direction. According to the statistical theory of errors, the random error is inversely proportional to the square root of the number of observations ($\text{Random Error} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$). For $n = 100$, random error is reduced by a factor of $\frac{\sqrt{100}}{\sqrt{5}} = \sqrt{20} \approx 4.5$ compared to $n = 5$, yielding a far more reliable mean estimate.

• The linear magnification $m_L$ is the square root of areal magnification:
$$m_L = \sqrt{m_A} = \sqrt{8857.14} \approx \mathbf{94.11} \approx \mathbf{94.1}$$

Question 1.11
NCERT Exercise
The length, breadth and thickness of a rectangular sheet of metal are $4.234\text{ m}, 1.005\text{ m}$, and $2.01\text{ cm}$ respectively. Give the area and volume of the sheet to correct significant figures.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• Given dimensions:
- Length $l = 4.234\text{ m}$ (4 significant figures)
- Breadth $b = 1.005\text{ m}$ (4 significant figures)
- Thickness $t = 2.01\text{ cm} = 0.0201\text{ m}$ (3 significant figures)
Least number of significant figures is 3 (in thickness $t$).

• 1. Total Surface Area:
$$A = 2(l \cdot b + b \cdot t + t \cdot l)$$
$$l \cdot b = 4.234 \times 1.005 = 4.25517\text{ m}^2$$
$$b \cdot t = 1.005 \times 0.0201 = 0.0202005\text{ m}^2$$
$$t \cdot l = 0.0201 \times 4.234 = 0.0851034\text{ m}^2$$
$$\text{Sum} = 4.25517 + 0.02020 + 0.08510 = 4.36047\text{ m}^2$$
$$A = 2 \times 4.36047 = 8.72094\text{ m}^2$$
Rounding to 3 significant figures: $\mathbf{A = 8.72\text{ m}^2}$

• 2. Volume:
$$V = l \times b \times t = 4.234 \times 1.005 \times 0.0201 = 0.0855289\text{ m}^3$$
Rounding off to 3 significant figures:
$$\mathbf{V = 0.0855\text{ m}^3} \quad (\text{or } 8.55 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}^3)$$

Question 1.13
NCERT Exercise
A physical quantity $P$ is related to four observables $a, b, c$ and $d$ as follows: $P = \frac{a^3 b^2}{\sqrt{c}\,d}$. The percentage errors of measurement in $a, b, c$ and $d$ are $1\%, 3\%, 4\%$ and $2\%$, respectively. What is the percentage error in the quantity $P$? If the value of $P$ calculated using the above relation turns out to be $3.763$, to what value should you round off the result?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• Given formula: $P = \frac{a^3 b^2}{c^{1/2} d} = a^3 b^2 c^{-1/2} d^{-1}$.
• The relative error in $P$ is given by logarithmic differentiation / error propagation rule:
$$\frac{\Delta P}{P} = 3\left(\frac{\Delta a}{a}\right) + 2\left(\frac{\Delta b}{b}\right) + \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\Delta c}{c}\right) + \left(\frac{\Delta d}{d}\right)$$
• Percentage error in $P$:
$$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \times 100\% = 3(1\%) + 2(3\%) + \frac{1}{2}(4\%) + 1(2\%)$$
$$= 3\% + 6\% + 2\% + 2\% = \mathbf{13\%}$$
• Rounding off value of $P$:
The calculated value is $P = 3.763$.
Since the percentage error is $13\%$ (which has two significant figures), the absolute uncertainty is roughly:
$$\Delta P = 0.13 \times 3.763 \approx 0.49$$
The uncertainty is in the first decimal place. Hence, the value of $P$ can only be quoted with precision to the tenths place.
Rounding off $3.763$ gives $\mathbf{3.8}$.

Question 1.14
NCERT Exercise
A book with many printing errors contains four different formulas for the displacement $y$ of a particle undergoing a certain periodic motion:
(a) $y = a \sin \frac{2\pi t}{T}$
(b) $y = a \sin vt$
(c) $y = \frac{a}{T} \sin \frac{t}{a}$
(d) $y = (a\sqrt{2}) \left(\sin \frac{2\pi t}{T} + \cos \frac{2\pi t}{T}\right)$
($a =$ maximum displacement of the particle, $v =$ speed of the particle, $T =$ time-period of motion). Rule out the wrong formulas on dimensional grounds.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• Principle of Homogeneity & Dimensional Requirements:
1. The argument of any trigonometric function (sine or cosine) must be dimensionless ($[M^0 L^0 T^0]$).
2. Both sides of the equation must have the dimensions of displacement ($[L]$).

• (a) $y = a \sin \frac{2\pi t}{T}$:
- Angle: $\left[\frac{2\pi t}{T}\right] = \frac{[T]}{[T]} = [M^0 L^0 T^0]$ (Dimensionless ✓)
- LHS: $[y] = [L]$; RHS: $[a] = [L]$ (✓). Formula is dimensionally correct.

• (b) $y = a \sin vt$:
- Angle: $[vt] = [L T^{-1}][T] = [L] \neq [M^0 L^0 T^0]$ (Not dimensionless ✗).
- Formula is dimensionally WRONG.

• (c) $y = \frac{a}{T} \sin \frac{t}{a}$:
- Angle: $\left[\frac{t}{a}\right] = \frac{[T]}{[L]} = [L^{-1} T] \neq [M^0 L^0 T^0]$ (✗).
- Amplitude: $\left[\frac{a}{T}\right] = [L T^{-1}] \neq [y]$ (✗).
- Formula is dimensionally WRONG.

• (d) $y = (a\sqrt{2}) \left(\sin \frac{2\pi t}{T} + \cos \frac{2\pi t}{T}\right)$:
- Angles: $\left[\frac{2\pi t}{T}\right] = [M^0 L^0 T^0]$ (Dimensionless ✓)
- Dimensions of amplitude: $[a\sqrt{2}] = [L] = [y]$ (✓). Formula is dimensionally correct.

• Conclusion: Formulas (b) and (c) are ruled out as wrong on dimensional grounds.

Question 1.15
NCERT Exercise
A famous relation in physics relates 'moving mass' $m$ to the 'rest mass' $m_0$ of a particle in terms of its speed $v$ and the speed of light $c$. A boy recalls the relation almost correctly but forgets where to put the constant $c$. He writes: $m = \frac{m_0}{(1 - v^2)^{1/2}}$. Guess where to put the missing $c$.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• By the principle of homogeneity of dimensions:
- In the expression $(1 - v^2)^{1/2}$, the quantity subtracted from the pure number $1$ must be dimensionless.
- However, speed squared has dimensions $[v^2] = [L^2 T^{-2}]$.
- To make $v^2$ dimensionless, it must be divided by another quantity with dimensions $[L^2 T^{-2}]$.
- The speed of light $c$ has dimensions of speed $[L T^{-1}]$, so $c^2$ has dimensions $[L^2 T^{-2}]$.
- Thus, the dimensionless term is $\frac{v^2}{c^2}$.

• Checking overall equation dimensions with this modification:
$$m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}$$
LHS has dimensions $[M]$, and RHS has dimensions $[M]/[1] = [M]$.

• Hence, the correct formula is: $\mathbf{m = \frac{m_0}{\left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)^{1/2}}}$, i.e., $c^2$ must be placed in the denominator under $v^2$.

The unit of length convenient on the atomic scale is known as an angstrom and is denoted by $\text{\AA}$: $1\text{ \AA} = 10^{-10}\text{ m}$. The size of a hydrogen atom is about $0.5\text{ \AA}$. What is the total atomic volume in $\text{m}^3$ of a mole of hydrogen atoms?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• Radius of hydrogen atom $r = 0.5\text{ \AA} = 0.5 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}$.
• Volume of a single spherical hydrogen atom:
$$V_1 = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = \frac{4}{3} \times 3.1416 \times (0.5 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m})^3$$
$$V_1 = \frac{4}{3} \times 3.1416 \times (0.125 \times 10^{-30}) = 0.5236 \times 10^{-30}\text{ m}^3$$
• One mole of hydrogen atoms contains Avogadro's number of atoms: $N_A = 6.023 \times 10^{23}$.
• Total atomic volume of one mole of hydrogen atoms:
$$V = N_A \times V_1 = (6.023 \times 10^{23}) \times (0.5236 \times 10^{-30}\text{ m}^3)$$
$$V = 3.1537 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}^3 \approx \mathbf{3.15 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}^3}$$

Question 1.18
NCERT Exercise
Explain this common observation clearly: If you look out of the window of a fast moving train, the nearby trees, houses etc. seem to move rapidly in a direction opposite to the train's motion, but the distant objects (hill tops, the Moon, the stars etc.) seem to be stationary.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• This phenomenon is an optical consequence of parallax and angular velocity.

• Line of Sight & Parallax:
The apparent motion of any object depends on the rate at which our line of sight to the object rotates as we move.

• Nearby Objects:
For nearby trees and houses, the distance $r$ from the observer is very small. When the train moves a distance $\Delta x$, the angle $\Delta \theta \approx \frac{\Delta x}{r}$ subtended at the eye changes very rapidly ($d\theta/dt = v/r$ is large). Hence, the line of sight swings rapidly backwards, making nearby objects appear to rush backward at great speed.

• Distant Objects:
For distant hills, the Moon, or stars, the distance $r$ is extraordinarily large ($r \to \infty$). The change in angle $\Delta \theta \approx \frac{\Delta x}{r} \approx 0$ is negligibly small. Because the line of sight remains virtually unchanged in direction, distant objects appear completely stationary.

Question 1.19
NCERT Exercise
The principle of parallax is used in the determination of distances of very distant stars. The baseline $AB$ is the line joining Earth's two locations six months apart in its orbit around the Sun. If the parallax angle of the nearest star is $0.76''$ (seconds of arc), calculate its distance in parsec and light years.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• By definition of a parsec (pc):
$1\text{ parsec}$ is the distance at which an astronomical unit ($1\text{ AU}$) subtends a parallax angle of $1''$ (one second of arc).
$$d\text{ (in parsecs)} = \frac{1}{\theta\text{ (in arcseconds)}}$$
• Given parallax angle $\theta = 0.76''$:
$$d = \frac{1}{0.76} \approx \mathbf{1.316\text{ pc}} \approx \mathbf{1.32\text{ pc}}$$
• Conversion to Light Years:
$$1\text{ pc} = 3.0857 \times 10^{16}\text{ m}$$
$$1\text{ ly} = 9.46 \times 10^{15}\text{ m}$$
$$1\text{ pc} = \frac{3.0857 \times 10^{16}}{9.46 \times 10^{15}} = 3.26\text{ ly}$$
$$d = 1.316 \times 3.26\text{ ly} = \mathbf{4.29\text{ ly}}$$
• Hence, the star's distance is $\mathbf{1.32\text{ pc}}$ or $\mathbf{4.29\text{ ly}}$.

Question 1.21
NCERT Exercise
Precise measurements of physical quantities are a need of science. For example, to ascertain the speed of an aircraft, one must have an accurate method to find its positions at closely separated instants of time. State similar instances in modern science where precision is paramount.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• Instances in modern science and technology where extreme precision measurement is vital:

• 1. Global Positioning System (GPS):
Satellite navigation depends on ultra-stable cesium and rubidium atomic clocks measuring time to within a few nanoseconds ($10^{-9}\text{ s}$). An error of just $1\,\mu\text{s}$ would cause a navigational positional drift of $300\text{ m}$.

• 2. Detection of Gravitational Waves (LIGO):
Laser interferometry measures mirror displacements as minuscule as $10^{-19}\text{ m}$ (one-ten-thousandth the diameter of a proton) over a $4\text{ km}$ baseline to detect spacetime ripples.

• 3. Semiconductor Nanofabrication:
Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV) photolithography creates microchip transistors with channel dimensions down to $2\text{ nm}$ to $3\text{ nm}$, requiring sub-nanometre positioning precision.

• 4. Mass Spectrometry in Medicine:
Determining isotope masses to parts-per-billion precision enables identification of biomolecules, disease biomarkers, and pharmaceutical drug purity.

Question 1.22
NCERT Exercise
Just as precise measurements are necessary in science, it is equally important to be able to make rough estimates of quantities using rudimentary ideas and common observations. Think of ways by which you can estimate:
(a) the total mass of rain-bearing clouds over India during the Monsoon
(b) the mass of an elephant
(c) the wind speed during a storm
(d) the number of strands of hair on your head
(e) the number of air molecules in your classroom.
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
• (a) Mass of rain-bearing clouds:
India receives average annual monsoon rainfall of $h \approx 100\text{ cm} = 1\text{ m}$.
Geographical area of India $A \approx 3.3 \times 10^6\text{ km}^2 = 3.3 \times 10^{12}\text{ m}^2$.
Total volume of water precipitated: $V = A \times h = 3.3 \times 10^{12}\text{ m}^3$.
Mass of water (clouds) $= V \times \rho = 3.3 \times 10^{12}\text{ m}^3 \times 10^3\text{ kg m}^{-3} \approx \mathbf{3.3 \times 10^{15}\text{ kg}}$ (billions of tonnes).

• (b) Mass of an elephant:
Use Archimedes' Principle. Drive the elephant onto a large flat-bottomed boat in calm water. Mark the initial water line and the depressed water line after the elephant boards. The volume of displaced water is $\Delta V = A_{\text{boat}} \times \Delta h$. The mass of the elephant is $M = \rho_{\text{water}} \times \Delta V$ (typically $\sim 3000\text{ kg}$ to $5000\text{ kg}$).

• (c) Wind speed during a storm:
Observe the deflection angle $\theta$ of a known pendulum or buoyant balloon. Alternatively, release a floating balloon and measure the time $t$ taken to traverse a known distance $d$ between two landmarks: $v = d/t$.

• (d) Number of strands of hair on your head:
Area of scalp $A \approx 200\text{ cm}^2$. Measure the hair density in a small area of $1\text{ cm}^2$ using a magnifying glass (typically $\sim 500$ hairs/$\text{cm}^2$). Total hair count $\approx 200 \times 500 = \mathbf{10^5\text{ hairs}}$ ($1\text{ lakh}$).

• (e) Number of air molecules in your classroom:
Room dimensions $\approx 6\text{ m} \times 5\text{ m} \times 4\text{ m} = 120\text{ m}^3 = 1.2 \times 10^5\text{ L}$.
At STP, $22.4\text{ L}$ of air contains $N_A = 6.023 \times 10^{23}$ molecules.
Number of moles $= \frac{1.2 \times 10^5}{22.4} \approx 5.35 \times 10^3\text{ moles}$.
Total molecules $N \approx (5.35 \times 10^3) \times (6.023 \times 10^{23}) \approx \mathbf{3.2 \times 10^{27}\text{ molecules}}$.
