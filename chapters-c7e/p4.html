P4

Chivvy

Honeycomb (Poem) • Poet: Michael Rosen — The constant barrage of adult commands and the irony of childhood independence

📜 Complete Poem Text & Stanzas

Grown-ups say things like: Speak up Don’t talk with your mouth full Don’t stare Don’t point Don’t pick your nose Sit up Say please Less noise Shut the door behind you Don’t drag your feet Haven’t you got a hankie? Take your hands out of your pockets Pull your socks up Stand up straight Say thank you Don’t interrupt No one thinks you’re funny Take your elbows off the table Can’t you make your own mind up about anything?
Key Poetic Devices:
  • Imperative Mode: The entire poem is composed of commanding imperative verbs ('Speak up', 'Don't stare', 'Sit up', 'Say please', 'Pull your socks up').
  • Irony / Paradox: The stinging concluding irony: After giving eighteen rigid, continuous commands dictating every physical reflex, the adult suddenly demands: 'Can't you make your own mind up about anything?'
  • Free Verse: Unrhymed conversational free verse mimicking natural adult speech rhythms.
Chapter Summary & Core Theme
Michael Rosen's 'Chivvy' (derived from the colloquial British term 'to chivvy', meaning to urge or nag someone incessantly to do something) captures the non-stop stream of instructions adults impose upon growing children. From table manners ('Don't talk with your mouth full') and body posture ('Sit up', 'Pull your socks up') to social decorum ('Say please', 'Say thank you', 'Don't point'), children are constantly corrected. The supreme irony appears in the final couplet: after forbidding children from exercising any independent agency, adults exasperatedly reprimand them for being incapable of making their own decisions.
🎯 Central Theme: The suffocating micro-management of children by well-meaning adults and the conflicting expectations of obedience versus individuality.
✍️ Working with the Poem
Working with the Poem — Q1 (i to iii)
Discuss these questions in small groups before you write the answers:
(i) When is a grown-up likely to say this? "Don't talk with your mouth full."
(ii) When are you likely to be told this? "Say thank you."
(iii) When do you think an adult would say this? "No one thinks you're funny."
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
(i) A grown-up says "Don't talk with your mouth full" during meals when a child begins speaking while chewing food, which is both poor table etiquette and a choking hazard.
(ii) A child is told "Say thank you" whenever they receive a gift, treat, compliment, help, or favor from someone, instilling courteous social manners.
(iii) An adult says "No one thinks you're funny" when a child misbehaves, makes silly faces, tells inappropriate jokes, or acts foolishly in front of guests or teachers in a desperate bid to seek attention.

(ii) Receiving presents, help, or favours 1 Mark

Working with the Poem — Q2
The last two lines of the poem are not prohibitions or instructions. What are they? The adult says: "Can't you make your own mind up about anything?" Why is this paradoxical or ironic?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
1. What the last two lines are: The last two lines are a rhetorical reprimand—an exasperated question directed at the child for lacking decisiveness and self-reliance.
2. Why it is paradoxical and ironic:
  • Throughout the child's life, adults regulate every minute action—how to sit, how to stand, where to put hands, how to blow the nose, and when to speak.
  • When children are trained like automatons never to think for themselves, it is hypocritical and absurd for adults to suddenly expect them to be mature, confident, and independent decision-makers. The very system of constant 'chivvying' destroys the independent thinking adults complain is missing.
CBSE Step-by-Step Marking Scheme
Identifying the rhetorical / reprimanding question format 1 Mark
Deep explanation of the paradox: micro-management destroys independent agency 2 Marks
Working with the Poem — Q3
Why do grown-ups give so many instructions to children? Is it helpful or irritating?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
  • Why adults do it: Grown-ups instruct children out of genuine care and social responsibility. They want their children to learn good manners, hygiene, safety, respect, and social etiquette so that they grow into responsible, cultured, and well-adjusted citizens.
  • Helpful or irritating: While basic guidance is undeniably essential and helpful for safety and social development, excessive, repetitive nagging ('chivvying') becomes irritating, suppresses creativity, and harms a child's natural self-confidence.
CBSE Step-by-Step Marking Scheme
Grown-ups' motive: safety, social socialization, and etiquette 1 Mark
Balanced assessment: necessary in moderation, suffocating when excessive 1 Mark
💡 Competency-Based & HOTS Questions (CBSE 2026-27 Pattern) Analytical Thinking
Value-Based Reflection / CBSE HOTS
If you were a parent or teacher, how would you strike a healthy balance between teaching discipline and nurturing child independence?

A balanced approach involves three fundamental principles:

  1. Explain the 'Why': Instead of issuing abrupt authoritarian decrees ('Don't do this!'), explain the rational benefit behind the rule (e.g., explaining that chewing with an open mouth causes choking and looks untidy).
  2. Provide Safe Autonomy: Give children age-appropriate choices (e.g., choosing their own clothes, books, hobbies, and managing homework schedules) so they cultivate decision-making confidence.
  3. Differentiate Safety from Quirkiness: Intervene firmly for health, safety, and morality, but let harmless personal quirks (like humming or sitting casually) flourish without unnecessary criticism.
