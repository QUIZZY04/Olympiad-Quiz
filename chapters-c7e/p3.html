The Shed

Honeycomb (Poem) • Poet: Frank Flynn — Conquering childhood fears and irrational superstitions through courage

📜 Complete Poem Text & Stanzas

There’s a shed at the bottom of our garden With a spider’s web hanging across the door, The hinges are rusty and creak in the wind. When I’m in bed I lie and I listen, I’ll open that door one day. There’s a dusty old window around at the side With three cracked panes of glass, I often think there’s someone staring at me Through that window each time that I pass, I’ll peep through that window one day. My brother says there’s a ghost in the shed Who hides under the rotten floorboards, And if I ever dare to set foot inside He’ll jump out and chop off my head, But I’ll take a peek one day. I know that there isn’t really a ghost, My brother tells lies to keep the shed for his den; There isn’t anyone staring or making strange noises And the spider has been gone from his web since I don't know when, I’ll go into that shed one day soon, But not just yet...
Key Poetic Devices:
  • Sensory Imagery: Auditory imagery ('creak in the wind', 'listen', 'strange noises') and visual imagery ('spider's web', 'rusty hinges', 'dusty old window', 'cracked panes').
  • Repetition / Refrain: The repeated determined resolution: 'I'll open that door one day' / 'I'll peep through that window one day' / 'I'll go into that shed one day soon'.
  • Suspense & Atmosphere: Creating an ominous, gothic atmosphere of mystery followed by rational disillusionment.
Chapter Summary & Core Theme
Frank Flynn's poem describes an old neglected shed located at the far corner of a family garden. With cobwebs across the door, rusty creaking hinges, and cracked dusty windowpanes, the shed appears spooky to a young child. The child's elder brother exploits this setting by telling terrifying tales of a hidden ghost beneath the rotten floorboards who will chop off anyone's head who steps inside. However, as the child grows older and rational, they recognize that the brother is fabricating lies to preserve the shed as his private hideout (den). While the speaker knows ghosts are fictitious, lingering childish hesitation remains: 'I'll go into that shed one day soon, / But not just yet...'
🎯 Central Theme: The psychological boundary between childish fear of the unknown and adolescent curiosity and rationality.
✍️ Working with the Poem
Working with the Poem — Q1 (i to iv)
Answer the following questions:
(i) Who is the speaker in the poem?
(ii) Is she/he afraid or curious, or both?
(iii) What is she/he planning to do soon?
(iv) "But not just yet..." suggests doubt, fear, hesitation, laziness or something else? Choose the word which seems right to you. Tell why you chose it.
4 Marks
Detailed Solution
(i) Who is the speaker: The speaker is a young, imaginative schoolchild who lives in the house with their older brother and observes the neglected shed at the bottom of the garden.
(ii) Afraid or curious: The speaker is both curious and afraid. Their intense curiosity drives them to repeatedly resolve to inspect the shed, peep through the window, and open the door. Yet the eerie creaking, the cracked window, and the brother's horrific ghost tales instill a lingering subconscious fear.
(iii) What the speaker plans to do: The speaker plans to gather up enough courage, go inside the mysterious shed, and explore it thoroughly on their own.
(iv) Meaning of "But not just yet...": The phrase primarily suggests hesitation mixed with residual fear. Although the child's rational intellect understands that ghosts do not exist and that the brother is lying, the subconscious mind is not entirely ready to banish the lingering childhood anxiety. The child wants to postpone the moment until they feel completely brave.

Working with the Poem — Extra Textual Q
Why did the speaker's brother tell ghost stories about the shed?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
The speaker's older brother told terrifying ghost stories—claiming a ghost slept under the rotten floorboards waiting to chop off heads—deliberately to frighten the younger speaker away. His motive was purely selfish: he wanted to keep the shed entirely for himself as his private secret clubhouse or play 'den' without any disturbance from his younger sibling.

Competency-Based Case Study: Overcoming Irrational Fears
How does the speaker demonstrate emotional growth and rational critical thinking from Stanza 1 to Stanza 4?

The poem chronicles a child's transition from superstitious fear to scientific reasoning:

  • Initial Phase (Stanzas 1–2): The child relies on imagination and sensory cues—the creaking door sounds like a menacing call; shadows behind cracked windows feel like staring eyes.
  • Climactic Confrontation (Stanza 3): The child encounters mythological terror through the brother's gory folklore (a head-chopping ghost).
  • Rational Triumph (Stanza 4): The child deconstructs the superstition through logic: the spider web is already gone, no sounds occur during daylight, and the brother's motive is exposed as sibling territoriality. The child replaces panic with poised patience.
