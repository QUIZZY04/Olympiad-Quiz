In-Text 4.1
NCERT In-Text
Silver atom has completely filled d orbitals ($4d^{10}$) in its ground state. How can you say that it is a transition element?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition of Transition Element:
A transition element is defined as an element which has partially filled (incompletely filled) $d$-orbitals in its ground state or in any one of its common oxidation states.

Explanation for Silver ($Z = 47$):
The ground state electronic configuration of silver is $[ ext{Kr}] 4d^{10} 5s^1$.
Although the silver atom has completely filled $4d$ orbitals ($4d^{10}$) in its neutral ground state, in its $+2$ oxidation state ($ ext{Ag}^{2+}$, as in $ ext{AgF}_2$), its electronic configuration is: $$\text{Ag}^{2+}: [\text{Kr}] 4d^9$$ Since the $d$-subshell is incompletely filled ($4d^9$) in the $+2$ oxidation state, silver is classified as a transition element.

In-Text 4.2
NCERT In-Text
In the series $\text{Sc}$ ($Z = 21$) to $\text{Zn}$ ($Z = 30$), the enthalpy of atomisation of zinc is the lowest, i.e., $126\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$. Why?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Reason:
Enthalpy of atomisation depends directly on the strength of metallic bonding in the crystal lattice. Strong metallic bonding arises from the involvement of unpaired electrons from both $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ orbitals in forming interatomic metallic bonds.

Case of Zinc ($Z = 30$):
Zinc has the electronic configuration $[ ext{Ar}] 3d^{10} 4s^2$. All electrons in the $3d$ subshell are fully paired ($3d^{10}$).
Due to the absence of unpaired $d$-electrons, the $d$-electrons do not participate effectively in interatomic metallic bonding. Hence, metallic bonding in zinc is exceptionally weak, accounting for its lowest enthalpy of atomisation ($126\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$).

In-Text 4.3
NCERT In-Text
Which of the $3d$ series of the transition metals exhibits the largest number of oxidation states and why?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Answer: Manganese ($ ext{Mn}$, $Z = 25$) exhibits the largest number of oxidation states in the $3d$ series.

Reason:
The electronic configuration of manganese is $[ ext{Ar}] 3d^5 4s^2$.
Manganese has the maximum number of unpaired electrons (5 unpaired electrons in $3d$ and 2 electrons in $4s$). Because the energy difference between $3d$ and $4s$ orbitals is very small, both $3d$ and $4s$ electrons can participate in bond formation.
Consequently, manganese exhibits all oxidation states from $+2$ to $+7$ ($+2, +3, +4, +5, +6, +7$).

In-Text 4.4
NCERT In-Text
The $E^\circ(\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M})$ value for copper is positive ($+0.34\text{ V}$). What is possibly the reason for this?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Thermodynamic Analysis:
The standard electrode potential $E^\circ(\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M})$ depends on three thermodynamic enthalpy terms: $$\Delta H = \Delta_a H^\circ + \Delta_i H_1^\circ + \Delta_i H_2^\circ + \Delta_{\text{hyd}} H^\circ$$
  1. Enthalpy of atomisation: $\text{Cu}(s) \rightarrow \text{Cu}(g)$ ($Delta_a H^circ$ is very high for copper).
  2. Ionisation enthalpies: $\text{Cu}(g) \rightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+}(g) + 2e^-$ (sum of first and second ionisation enthalpies $\Delta_i H_1^\circ + \Delta_i H_2^\circ$ is high).
  3. Enthalpy of hydration: $\text{Cu}^{2+}(g) + aq \rightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq)$ (exothermic $\Delta_{\text{hyd}} H^\circ$).
Conclusion: The high enthalpy of atomisation and high ionisation enthalpies of copper are not completely balanced by its hydration enthalpy. Therefore, the overall $\Delta H$ for the transformation $\text{Cu}(s) \rightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^-$ is positive, making $E^\circ(\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu})$ positive ($+0.34\text{ V}$) and rendering copper chemically unreactive towards non-oxidising acids.

In-Text 4.5
NCERT In-Text
How would you account for the irregular variation of ionisation enthalpies (first and second) in the first series of the transition elements?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Reasons for Irregular Variations:
  • Extra Stability of Half-filled and Fully-filled Subshells: Ionisation enthalpy involves removing an electron from $4s$ or $3d$. Disruption of stable half-filled ($d^5$) or completely filled ($d^{10}$) configurations requires extraordinarily high energy. For example, second ionisation enthalpy of $\text{Cr}$ ($3d^5$) and $\text{Cu}$ ($3d^{10}$) are abnormally high because an electron must be removed from stable $d^5$ and $d^{10}$ configurations respectively.
  • Varying Shielding Effect: As nuclear charge increases across the series, electrons enter $(n-1)d$ orbitals, which shield the outer $4s$ electrons imperfectly. This irregular increase in effective nuclear charge causes fluctuations.
  • Exchange Energy: Configurations with more parallel spins (like $d^5$) possess high exchange energy stabilization, altering the energy required to remove an electron.

In-Text 4.6
NCERT In-Text
Why is the highest oxidation state of a metal exhibited in its oxide or fluoride only?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Reason:
Oxygen and fluorine are the most electronegative elements with small atomic sizes.
  • Fluorine: Stabilises the highest oxidation state primarily through high lattice enthalpy in ionic fluorides or high bond enthalpies in higher covalent fluorides (e.g., $\text{VF}_5, \text{CrF}_6$).
  • Oxygen: Has a superior ability to stabilise high oxidation states (even higher than fluorine, e.g., $\text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7$ exists whereas $\text{MnF}_7$ does not) due to its unique capability to form multiple bonds ($p\pi - d\pi$ bonding) with transition metals.

In-Text 4.7
NCERT In-Text
Which is a stronger reducing agent $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ or $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ and why?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Answer: $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ is a much stronger reducing agent than $\text{Fe}^{2+}$.

Reason:
A reducing agent undergoes oxidation by losing electrons:
$$\text{Cr}^{2+} (d^4) \rightarrow \text{Cr}^{3+} (d^3) + e^-$$ $$\text{Fe}^{2+} (d^6) \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+} (d^5) + e^-$$ In aqueous solution, crystal field splitting divides the $d$-orbitals into lower energy $t_{2g}$ and higher energy $e_g$ sets. The $d^3$ configuration of $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ has a half-filled $t_{2g}^3$ subshell, which is exceptionally stable in aqueous medium.
In contrast, $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ possesses a $d^5$ configuration, which does not possess this specific octahedral crystal field stabilization in solution ($E^\circ_{\text{Cr}^{3+}/\text{Cr}^{2+}} = -0.41\text{ V}$, while $E^\circ_{\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}} = +0.77\text{ V}$). The negative potential of chromium makes $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ readily oxidisable and hence a strong reducing agent.

Calculate the 'spin only' magnetic moment of $\text{M}^{2+}(aq)$ ion ($Z = 27$).
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Electronic Configuration of Element ($Z = 27$, Cobalt):
$$\text{M} (Z = 27): [\text{Ar}] 3d^7 4s^2$$ $$\text{M}^{2+}: [\text{Ar}] 3d^7$$
2. Number of Unpaired Electrons ($n$):
The $3d$ subshell has 5 orbitals. Filling 7 electrons according to Hund's rule: $$\uparrow\downarrow, \uparrow\downarrow, \uparrow, \uparrow, \uparrow \implies n = 3 \text{ unpaired electrons}$$
3. Spin-Only Magnetic Moment Formula: $$\mu = \sqrt{n(n + 2)} \text{ BM}$$ $$\mu = \sqrt{3(3 + 2)} = \sqrt{15} \approx \mathbf{3.87\text{ BM}}$$ Answer: The spin-only magnetic moment is $3.87\text{ Bohr Magnetons (BM)}$.

In-Text 4.9
NCERT In-Text
Explain why $\text{Cu}^+$ ion is not stable in aqueous solutions?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Reason:
In aqueous solution, $\text{Cu}^+$ undergoes disproportionation into $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ and $\text{Cu}(s)$: $$2\text{Cu}^+(aq) \rightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)$$ Thermodynamic Explanation:
Although the second ionisation enthalpy of copper is high, the hydration enthalpy of divalent $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ is much more negative (exothermic) than that of monovalent $\text{Cu}^+$ due to the smaller ionic radius and higher charge density of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$.
This highly favourable hydration enthalpy of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ more than compensates for the second ionisation enthalpy of copper, making $\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq)$ thermodynamically much more stable than $\text{Cu}^+(aq)$.

In-Text 4.10
NCERT In-Text
Actinoid contraction is greater from element to element than lanthanoid contraction. Why?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Reason:
Both contractions occur due to imperfect shielding of nuclear charge by electrons added to inner $f$-orbitals.
In actinoids, the differentiating electrons enter the $5f$ orbitals, whereas in lanthanoids, electrons enter the $4f$ orbitals.
The $5f$ orbitals are more diffused and extend further into space than the $4f$ orbitals. Consequently, the shielding effect of $5f$ electrons is poorer than that of $4f$ electrons.
As a result, the effective nuclear charge experienced by the outermost electrons increases more rapidly across the actinoid series, leading to a greater actinoid contraction from element to element compared to lanthanoid contraction.

NCERT Exercise
Why are $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ compounds more stable than $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ towards oxidation to their $+3$ state?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Electronic Configurations:
$$\text{Mn}^{2+}: [\text{Ar}] 3d^5 \quad (\text{exactly half-filled, extra stable})$$ $$\text{Fe}^{2+}: [\text{Ar}] 3d^6 \quad (\text{one electron more than half-filled})$$
Oxidation to $+3$ state: $$\text{Mn}^{2+} (3d^5) \xrightarrow{-e^-} \text{Mn}^{3+} (3d^4)$$ Oxidation of $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ requires removing an electron from a stable half-filled $3d^5$ subshell, requiring high third ionisation enthalpy. Hence, $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ resists oxidation.

$$\text{Fe}^{2+} (3d^6) \xrightarrow{-e^-} \text{Fe}^{3+} (3d^5)$$ In contrast, loss of one electron from $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ leads to a stable half-filled $3d^5$ configuration of $\text{Fe}^{3+}$. Therefore, $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ is readily oxidised to $\text{Fe}^{3+}$, while $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ compounds remain stable.

Exercise 4.3
NCERT Exercise
Explain briefly how $+2$ state becomes more and more stable in the first half of the first row transition elements with increasing atomic number?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Reason:
In the first transition series ($ ext{Sc}$ to $ ext{Mn}$), the $+2$ oxidation state arises from the loss of the two outer $4s$ electrons: $$\text{M} \rightarrow \text{M}^{2+} + 2e^-$$ As the atomic number increases from $\text{Sc}$ ($Z = 21$) to $\text{Mn}$ ($Z = 25$):
  1. Electrons are progressively added to the inner $3d$ subshell: $\text{Sc}^{2+} (3d^1), \text{Ti}^{2+} (3d^2), \text{V}^{2+} (3d^3), \text{Cr}^{2+} (3d^4), \text{Mn}^{2+} (3d^5)$.
  2. The effective nuclear charge steadily increases, binding the $3d$ electrons more tightly and making further removal of $3d$ electrons to yield higher oxidation states ($+3, +4$, etc.) increasingly difficult.
  3. At manganese, the $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ ion attains an exceptionally stable half-filled $3d^5$ configuration.
Hence, the third ionisation enthalpy increases progressively, making the $+2$ state increasingly stable from $ ext{Sc}$ to $ ext{Mn}$.

Exercise 4.7
NCERT Exercise
What is lanthanoid contraction? What are the consequences of lanthanoid contraction?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition:
The steady and progressive decrease in the atomic and ionic radii of the lanthanoid elements with increasing atomic number from Lanthanum ($ ext{La}^{3+}$, $103\text{ pm}$) to Lutetium ($ ext{Lu}^{3+}$, $86\text{ pm}$) is called Lanthanoid Contraction.
Cause: Due to the diffused shape and poor shielding effect of $4f$ electrons, effective nuclear charge increases steadily.

Consequences of Lanthanoid Contraction:
  1. Similarity in Radii of $4d$ and $5d$ Transition Elements: Elements of the second and third transition series belonging to the same vertical group possess nearly identical atomic and ionic radii (e.g., $\text{Zr} = 160\text{ pm} \approx \text{Hf} = 159\text{ pm}$; $\text{Nb} \approx \text{Ta}$; $\text{Mo} \approx \text{W}$). Because of this, they show remarkably similar chemical and physical properties and occur together in nature.
  2. Difficulty in Separation of Lanthanoids: Because the ionic radii of lanthanoids decrease very gradually, their chemical properties and hydration enthalpies are almost identical, making their separation in pure state extremely challenging (requiring ion-exchange techniques).
  3. Variation in Basic Strength of Hydroxides: As ionic radius decreases from $\text{La}^{3+}$ to $\text{Lu}^{3+}$, the covalent character of the $\text{M}-\text{OH}$ bond increases according to Fajan's rules. Consequently, basic strength decreases: $$\text{La(OH)}_3 \text{ is the most basic} > \dots > \text{Lu(OH)}_3 \text{ is the least basic}$$

Exercise 4.8
NCERT Exercise
What are the characteristics of the transition elements and why are they called transition elements? Which of the $d$-block elements may not be regarded as the transition elements?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Why Called Transition Elements:
They occupy the central block of the periodic table between the highly electropositive $s$-block elements and the electronegative $p$-block elements, representing a transition or bridge in physical and chemical properties between $s$ and $p$ blocks.

2. Characteristic Properties:
  • Variable oxidation states.
  • Formation of coloured ions due to $d-d$ electronic transitions.
  • Paramagnetic behaviour resulting from unpaired $d$-electrons.
  • Tendency to form complex compounds (coordination complexes).
  • Catalytic properties and ability to form interstitial compounds and alloys.

3. Elements Not Regarded as Transition Elements:
Zinc ($ ext{Zn}$), Cadmium ($ ext{Cd}$), and Mercury ($ ext{Hg}$) of Group 12 are not regarded as transition elements because they have completely filled $(n-1)d^{10}$ subshells in their ground states as well as in their common oxidation states ($ ext{Zn}^{2+}, ext{Cd}^{2+}, ext{Hg}^{2+}$).

Exercise 4.9
NCERT Exercise
In what way is the electronic configuration of the transition elements different from that of the non-transition elements?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Comparison of Electronic Configurations:
  • Transition Elements ($d$-block):
    The differentiating electron enters the penultimate $(n-1)d$ subshell. Their general electronic configuration is: $$\mathbf{(n-1)d^{1-10} ns^{1-2}}$$ Both the outermost $ns$ and penultimate $(n-1)d$ electrons participate in chemical bonding, leading to variable valencies.
  • Non-Transition Elements ($s$- and $p$-block):
    The differentiating electron enters the outermost principal shell ($ns$ for $s$-block, $np$ for $p$-block). Their general electronic configurations are: $$\mathbf{ns^{1-2}} \quad (s\text{-block}) \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{ns^2 np^{1-6}} \quad (p\text{-block})$$ Only the outermost valence shell electrons participate in bonding, and oxidation states typically vary by units of 2 (due to inert pair effect).

Exercise 4.10
NCERT Exercise
What are the different oxidation states exhibited by the lanthanoids?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Oxidation States of Lanthanoids:
  1. Principal Oxidation State ($+3$): The $+3$ oxidation state is the common and predominant oxidation state exhibited by all lanthanoids ($ ext{Ln}^{3+}$), formed by the loss of two $6s$ electrons and one $5d$ or $4f$ electron.
  2. $+2$ Oxidation State: Exhibited when it leads to empty ($4f^0$), half-filled ($4f^7$), or fully-filled ($4f^{14}$) subshells:
    • $\text{Eu}^{2+} ([\text{Xe}] 4f^7)$: Exceptionally stable half-filled $f$-shell; strong reducing agent.
    • $\text{Yb}^{2+} ([\text{Xe}] 4f^{14})$: Completely filled $f$-shell; reducing agent.
    • $\text{Sm}^{2+} ([\text{Xe}] 4f^6)$.

Exercise 4.12
NCERT Exercise
What are interstitial compounds? Why are such compounds well known for transition metals?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition:
Interstitial compounds are those which are formed when small non-metal atoms like $ ext{H}, ext{C}, ext{N}$ or $ ext{B}$ get trapped in the interstitial voids or crystal lattice cavities of transition metals (e.g., $\text{TiC}, \text{Mn}_4\text{N}, \text{Fe}_3\text{H}, \text{TiH}_{1.7}$).

Why Well Known for Transition Metals:
Transition metal atoms crystallise in close-packed structures ($ ext{fcc, bcc, hcp}$) leaving interstitial voids. Due to their relatively large atomic radii, the size of these interstitial sites is suitable to accommodate small non-metal atoms without distorting the metal lattice.

Key Characteristics:
• Non-stoichiometric in composition.
• Extremely high melting points (higher than pure metals).
• Very hard (some borides approach diamond in hardness).
• Retain metallic electrical and thermal conductivity.
• Chemically inert.
