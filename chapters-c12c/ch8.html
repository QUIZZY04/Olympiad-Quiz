Arrange the following compounds in increasing order of their boiling points:
$$\text{CH}_3\text{CHO}, \quad \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH}, \quad \text{CH}_3\text{OCH}_3, \quad \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_3$$

In-Text 8.4
NCERT In-Text
Would you expect benzaldehyde to be more reactive or less reactive in nucleophilic addition reactions than propanal? Explain your answer.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Answer: Benzaldehyde is less reactive than propanal.

Scientific Reason:
Nucleophilic addition to a carbonyl group occurs by attack of the nucleophile on the electrophilic carbonyl carbon ($>\text{C}^{\delta+}=\text{O}^{\delta-}$). The higher the magnitude of positive charge on carbon, the faster the reaction.

  1. Resonance Effect in Benzaldehyde: In benzaldehyde ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CHO}$), the carbonyl carbon is directly conjugated with the benzene ring. Due to the electron-donating resonance effect ($+R$) of the benzene ring, $\pi$-electrons delocalize into the carbonyl carbon: $$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5-\text{CH}=\text{O} \longleftrightarrow \text{C}_6\text{H}_5^+=\text{CH}-\text{O}^-$$ This resonance donation significantly reduces the electrophilicity (positive charge density) of the carbonyl carbon.
  2. Propanal ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CHO}$): In propanal, the ethyl group exhibits only a weak $+I$ inductive effect, which diminishes positive charge to a far smaller extent than the powerful resonance delocalization in benzaldehyde. Furthermore, benzaldehyde is more sterically crowded than propanal.

Exercise 8.1
NCERT Exercise
What is meant by the following terms? Give an example of the reaction in each case:
(i) Cyanohydrin
(ii) Acetal
(iii) Semicarbazone
(iv) Aldol
(v) Hemiacetal
(vi) Oxime
(vii) Ketal
(viii) Imine
(ix) 2,4-DNP-derivative
(x) Schiff's base
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definitions and Chemical Examples:
  1. Cyanohydrin: Compounds containing a hydroxyl group and a cyano group on the same carbon [$>\text{C(OH)(CN)}$].
    Example: $\text{CH}_3\text{CHO} + \text{HCN} \rightarrow \mathbf{\text{CH}_3\text{CH(OH)CN}}$ (Acetaldehyde cyanohydrin).
  2. Acetal: Gem-dialkoxy compounds produced by the reaction of an aldehyde with two equivalents of a monohydric alcohol in presence of dry $\text{HCl}$ gas [$R-\text{CH(OR')}_2$].
    Example: $\text{CH}_3\text{CHO} + 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \xrightarrow{\text{dry HCl}} \mathbf{\text{CH}_3\text{CH(OC}_2\text{H}_5)_2}$ (Acetaldehyde diethyl acetal).
  3. Semicarbazone: Derivatives formed by the condensation of an aldehyde or ketone with semicarbazide in weakly acidic medium [$>\text{C}=\text{N}-\text{NH}-\text{CONH}_2$].
    Example: Acetone semicarbazone [$(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{C}=\text{N}-\text{NH}-\text{CONH}_2$].
  4. Aldol: A $\beta$-hydroxy aldehyde or ketone formed by self-condensation of two molecules of carbonyl compound having $\alpha$-hydrogen in presence of dilute base.
    Example: 3-Hydroxybutanal [$\text{CH}_3\text{CH(OH)CH}_2\text{CHO}$].
  5. Hemiacetal: An alkoxy alcohol formed by addition of one mole of alcohol to an aldehyde [$R-\text{CH(OH)(OR')}$].
    Example: $\text{CH}_3\text{CHO} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \xrightarrow{\text{HCl}} \mathbf{\text{CH}_3\text{CH(OH)(OC}_2\text{H}_5)}$.
  6. Oxime: Formed by condensation of aldehydes/ketones with hydroxylamine [$>\text{C}=\text{N}-\text{OH}$].
    Example: Acetaldoxime [$\text{CH}_3\text{CH}=\text{N}-\text{OH}$].
  7. Ketal: Gem-dialkoxy compounds formed by the reaction of a ketone with a dihydric alcohol (like ethylene glycol) in presence of dry $\text{HCl}$.
    Example: Ethylene ketal of propan-2-one.
  8. Imine: Compounds containing a carbon-nitrogen double bond ($>\text{C}=\text{N}-R$) formed from ammonia or primary amines.
  9. 2,4-DNP-Derivative: Orange/yellow crystalline solids formed with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine, used for characterising carbonyl compounds.
  10. Schiff's Base: An azomethine ($>\text{C}=\text{N}-\text{Ar}$) formed by the reaction of an aldehyde/ketone with a primary aromatic amine ($ ext{ArNH}_2$).

Exercise 8.15
NCERT Exercise
Explain the mechanism of nucleophilic addition to a carbonyl group. Why are aldehydes generally more reactive than ketones towards nucleophilic additions?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Mechanism of Nucleophilic Addition:
  • The carbonyl carbon is $sp^2$ hybridised and coplanar. Due to higher electronegativity of oxygen, the $>\text{C}=\text{O}$ bond is strongly polarised ($>\text{C}^{\delta+}=\text{O}^{\delta-}$).
  • Step 1 (Slow, RDS): The nucleophile ($:\text{Nu}^-$) attacks the planar electrophilic carbon from a direction perpendicular to the plane, converting the $sp^2$ hybridised carbon into a tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate ($sp^3$).
  • Step 2 (Fast): The alkoxide intermediate captures a proton ($ ext{H}^+$) from the medium to yield the neutral addition product.

2. Why Aldehydes are More Reactive than Ketones:
  1. Electronic Factor: Ketones have two electron-donating alkyl groups ($+I$ effect) attached to the carbonyl carbon, which significantly diminish the partial positive charge on carbon. Aldehydes have only one alkyl group (or none in $ ext{HCHO}$); hence, their carbonyl carbon is more electrophilic.
  2. Steric Factor: Ketones possess two bulky alkyl groups surrounding the carbonyl carbon, hindering the approach of the nucleophile. In aldehydes, the presence of one small hydrogen atom causes minimal steric crowding in the tetrahedral transition state.

Exercise 8.20

Although phenoxide ion has more number of resonating structures than carboxylate ion, carboxylic acid is a stronger acid than phenol. Why?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Comparison of Resonating Structures of Conjugate Bases:
  1. Carboxylate Ion ($R-\text{COO}^-$): Has two equivalent resonance structures where the negative charge is delocalized exclusively over two highly electronegative oxygen atoms: $$R-\text{C}(=\text{O})-\text{O}^- \longleftrightarrow R-\text{C}(\text{O}^-)=\text{O}$$ Because oxygen is highly electronegative, having the negative charge on oxygen provides extraordinarily high resonance stabilization.

However, in three of these structures, the negative charge resides on less electronegative carbon atoms of the aromatic ring (ortho and para carbons). Carbons are far less electronegative than oxygen and cannot accommodate negative charge effectively.
Conclusion: The two equivalent resonance structures of the carboxylate ion with negative charge on electronegative oxygen provide far greater thermodynamic stabilization than the non-equivalent structures of the phenoxide ion with negative charge on carbons. Hence, carboxylic acids ($pK_a \approx 4-5$) are much stronger acids than phenols ($pK_a \approx 10$).

Exercise 8.22
NCERT Exercise
Give reasons: (i) Dialkylcadmium is used for the preparation of ketones from acid chlorides and not Grignard reagents. (ii) Benzaldehyde does not reduce Fehling's solution.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Explanations:
  1. Dialkylcadmium vs Grignard Reagent:
    Grignard reagents ($R-\text{MgX}$) are extraordinarily strong nucleophiles. When reacted with an acid chloride, they initially produce a ketone, but the formed ketone is still very electrophilic and immediately reacts with a second molecule of Grignard reagent to yield a tertiary alcohol: $$R'\text{COCl} + R\text{MgX} \rightarrow R'\text{COR} \xrightarrow{R\text{MgX}} R'R_2\text{C-OH}$$ In contrast, dialkylcadmium ($R_2\text{Cd}$) is much less reactive (cadmium is less electropositive than magnesium). It is nucleophilic enough to react with the highly reactive acid chloride, but does not react with the ketone formed. Hence, the reaction stops cleanly at the ketone stage: $$2R'\text{COCl} + R_2\text{Cd} \rightarrow \mathbf{2R'\text{COR}} + \text{CdCl}_2$$
  2. Benzaldehyde and Fehling's Solution:
    Fehling's reagent is a milder oxidising agent than Tollens' reagent. In aromatic aldehydes like benzaldehyde, the carbonyl carbon is stabilised by $+R$ resonance donation from the benzene ring, making it less electrophilic and harder to oxidise. Therefore, aromatic aldehydes cannot be oxidised by weaker Fehling's solution (though they are easily oxidised by stronger Tollens' reagent).

Exercise 8.26
NCERT Exercise
Give reasons: (i) Melting points of even-carbon aliphatic carboxylic acids are higher than those of the immediately preceding and succeeding odd-carbon homologues (Alternation effect). (ii) Formic acid is stronger than acetic acid.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Explanations:
  1. Alternation Effect in Melting Points:
    In aliphatic carboxylic acids, the carbon chain adopts a zig-zag conformation.
    • In acids with an even number of carbon atoms, the terminal $-\text{CH}_3$ group and the terminal $-\text{COOH}$ group lie on opposite sides of the zig-zag carbon chain. This arrangement allows the molecules to fit much more compactly and symmetrically into the crystal lattice, resulting in stronger intermolecular lattice forces and higher melting points.
    • In acids with an odd number of carbon atoms, the $-\text{CH}_3$ and $-\text{COOH}$ groups lie on the same side of the chain, causing poor packing efficiency in the crystal lattice and resulting in lower melting points.
  2. Formic Acid ($ ext{HCOOH}$) Stronger than Acetic Acid ($ ext{CH}_3 ext{COOH}$):
    In acetic acid, the electron-releasing methyl group ($+\text{I}$ effect) pushes electron density towards the carboxylate group, destabilizing the acetate anion ($\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$) and reducing its acidity ($pK_a = 4.76$).
    In formic acid, only a hydrogen atom is attached, which exerts no electron-donating $+I$ effect. Hence, the formate anion ($\text{HCOO}^-$) is more stable than the acetate anion, making formic acid ($pK_a = 3.75$) about 10 times stronger than acetic acid.
