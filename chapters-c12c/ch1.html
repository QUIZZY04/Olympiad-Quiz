In-Text 1.1
NCERT In-Text
Calculate the mass percentage of benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$) and carbon tetrachloride ($\text{CCl}_4$) if $22\text{ g}$ of benzene is dissolved in $122\text{ g}$ of carbon tetrachloride.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Given Data:
Mass of benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$, solute) = $22\text{ g}$
Mass of carbon tetrachloride ($\text{CCl}_4$, solvent) = $122\text{ g}$

2. Total mass of solution:
$$\text{Total mass} = 22\text{ g} + 122\text{ g} = 144\text{ g}$$
3. Mass percentage formulas:
$$\text{Mass } \% \text{ of benzene} = \frac{\text{Mass of benzene}}{\text{Total mass of solution}} \times 100 = \frac{22}{144} \times 100 = \mathbf{15.28\%}$$
$$\text{Mass } \% \text{ of } \text{CCl}_4 = \frac{\text{Mass of } \text{CCl}_4}{\text{Total mass of solution}} \times 100 = \frac{122}{144} \times 100 = \mathbf{84.72\%}$$
Verification: $15.28\% + 84.72\% = 100.00\%$.

In-Text 1.2
NCERT In-Text
Calculate the mole fraction of benzene in solution containing $30\%$ by mass in carbon tetrachloride.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Interpretation of 30% by mass:
Let the mass of solution be $100\text{ g}$.
Then, mass of benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$) = $30\text{ g}$
Mass of carbon tetrachloride ($\text{CCl}_4$) = $100 - 30 = 70\text{ g}$

2. Molar masses:
$$M(\text{C}_6\text{H}_6) = 6(12) + 6(1) = 78\text{ g mol}^{-1}$$
$$M(\text{CCl}_4) = 12 + 4(35.5) = 12 + 142 = 154\text{ g mol}^{-1}$$

3. Number of moles:
$$n_{\text{benzene}} = \frac{30\text{ g}}{78\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.385\text{ mol}$$
$$n_{\text{CCl}_4} = \frac{70\text{ g}}{154\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.455\text{ mol}$$

4. Mole fraction of benzene ($x_{\text{benzene}}$):
$$x_{\text{benzene}} = \frac{n_{\text{benzene}}}{n_{\text{benzene}} + n_{\text{CCl}_4}} = \frac{0.385}{0.385 + 0.455} = \frac{0.385}{0.840} = \mathbf{0.458}$$
$$\text{Mole fraction of } \text{CCl}_4 = 1 - 0.458 = \mathbf{0.542}$$

Calculate the mass of urea ($\text{NH}_2\text{CONH}_2$) required in making $2.5\text{ kg}$ of $0.25\text{ molal}$ aqueous solution.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Understanding Molality ($0.25\text{ m}$):
$0.25\text{ molal}$ means $0.25\text{ mol}$ of urea is dissolved in $1000\text{ g}$ ($1\text{ kg}$) of water.

2. Molar mass of urea ($\text{NH}_2\text{CONH}_2$):
$$M = 2(14) + 4(1) + 12 + 16 = 28 + 4 + 12 + 16 = 60\text{ g mol}^{-1}$$
Mass of $0.25\text{ mol}$ of urea = $0.25 \times 60 = 15\text{ g}$

3. Total mass of solution:
$$\text{Mass of solution} = 1000\text{ g (solvent)} + 15\text{ g (solute)} = 1015\text{ g} = 1.015\text{ kg}$$

4. Unitary method to find mass of urea for $2.5\text{ kg}$ solution:
$1.015\text{ kg}$ of solution contains $15\text{ g}$ of urea.
$$2.5\text{ kg of solution requires } = \frac{15\text{ g}}{1.015\text{ kg}} \times 2.5\text{ kg} = \mathbf{36.95\text{ g } (\approx 37\text{ g})}$$

[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Understanding solubility $0.195\text{ m}$ at STP:
$0.195\text{ m}$ means $0.195\text{ mol}$ of $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ gas is dissolved in $1000\text{ g}$ of water.

$p = 0.987\text{ bar}$ (or $1\text{ bar}$ standard)

$$p = K_H \cdot x \implies K_H = \frac{p}{x}$$
$$K_H = \frac{0.987\text{ bar}}{0.0035} = \mathbf{282\text{ bar } (\approx 2.82 \times 10^2\text{ bar})}$$

Henry’s law constant for $\text{CO}_2$ in water is $1.67 \times 10^8\text{ Pa}$ at $298\text{ K}$. Calculate the quantity of $\text{CO}_2$ in $500\text{ mL}$ of soda water when packed under $2.5\text{ atm } \text{CO}_2$ pressure at $298\text{ K}$.

$K_H = 1.67 \times 10^8\text{ Pa}$

3. Moles of water in $500\text{ mL}$ soda water:
Taking density of soda water $\approx 1\text{ g mL}^{-1}$, mass of water = $500\text{ g}$.
$$n_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} = \frac{500}{18} = 27.78\text{ mol}$$

$$n_{\text{CO}_2} = x \times n_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} = 1.517 \times 10^{-3} \times 27.78 = 0.0421\text{ mol}$$
$$\text{Mass of } \text{CO}_2 = n \times M = 0.0421\text{ mol} \times 44\text{ g mol}^{-1} = \mathbf{1.854\text{ g } (\approx 1.85\text{ g})}$$

In-Text 1.8
NCERT In-Text
The vapour pressure of pure liquids $\text{A}$ and $\text{B}$ are $450$ and $700\text{ mm Hg}$ respectively, at $350\text{ K}$. Find out the composition of the liquid mixture if total vapour pressure is $600\text{ mm Hg}$. Also find the composition of the vapour phase.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Given:
$p_A^0 = 450\text{ mm Hg}$, $p_B^0 = 700\text{ mm Hg}$, $p_{\text{total}} = 600\text{ mm Hg}$.

2. Raoult's Law for liquid composition:
$$p_{\text{total}} = p_A + p_B = p_A^0 x_A + p_B^0 (1 - x_A) = p_B^0 + (p_A^0 - p_B^0) x_A$$
$$600 = 700 + (450 - 700) x_A$$
$$600 - 700 = -250 x_A \implies -100 = -250 x_A \implies x_A = \frac{100}{250} = \mathbf{0.40}$$
$$x_B = 1 - 0.40 = \mathbf{0.60}$$

3. Partial vapour pressures:
$$p_A = p_A^0 x_A = 450 \times 0.40 = 180\text{ mm Hg}$$
$$p_B = p_B^0 x_B = 700 \times 0.60 = 420\text{ mm Hg}$$

4. Composition of vapour phase ($y_A$ and $y_B$):
$$y_A = \frac{p_A}{p_{\text{total}}} = \frac{180}{600} = \mathbf{0.30}$$
$$y_B = \frac{p_B}{p_{\text{total}}} = \frac{420}{600} = \mathbf{0.70}$$

$$\frac{23.8 - p_1}{23.8} = 0.0173 \implies 23.8 - p_1 = 0.412\text{ mm Hg}$$
$$p_1 = 23.8 - 0.412 = \mathbf{23.388\text{ mm Hg } (\approx 23.39\text{ mm Hg})}$$

[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Given:

$$\Delta T_b = \frac{K_b \times w_2 \times 1000}{M_2 \times w_1}$$
$$0.37 = \frac{0.52 \times w_2 \times 1000}{342 \times 500}$$
$$0.37 = \frac{0.52 \times 2 \times w_2}{342} = \frac{1.04 \, w_2}{342}$$
$$w_2 = \frac{0.37 \times 342}{1.04} = \frac{126.54}{1.04} = \mathbf{121.67\text{ g}}$$

Question 1.3
NCERT Exercise
Define the following terms:
(i) Mole fraction
(ii) Molality
(iii) Molarity
(iv) Mass percentage
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
(i) Mole fraction ($x$):
It is the ratio of the number of moles of one component to the total number of moles of all the components present in the solution. For a binary solution of components A and B:
$$x_A = \frac{n_A}{n_A + n_B}, \quad x_A + x_B = 1$$
It is dimensionless and independent of temperature.

(ii) Molality ($m$):
It is defined as the number of moles of the solute dissolved per kilogram ($1000\text{ g}$) of the solvent.
$$m = \frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Mass of solvent in kg}} = \frac{w_2 \times 1000}{M_2 \times w_1\text{ (in g)}}$$
Unit: $\text{mol kg}^{-1}$ or $\text{m}$. It is independent of temperature.

(iii) Molarity ($M$):
It is defined as the number of moles of the solute dissolved per litre ($1000\text{ mL}$) of the solution.
$$M = \frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Volume of solution in litres}} = \frac{w_2 \times 1000}{M_2 \times V\text{ (in mL)}}$$
Unit: $\text{mol L}^{-1}$ or $\text{M}$. It depends on temperature because volume varies with temperature.

(iv) Mass percentage (w/w):
It is the mass of the solute present in $100\text{ g}$ of the solution.
$$\text{Mass } \% \text{ of component} = \frac{\text{Mass of component in solution}}{\text{Total mass of solution}} \times 100$$

Concentrated nitric acid used in laboratory work is $68\%$ nitric acid by mass in aqueous solution. What should be the molarity of such a sample of the acid if the density of the solution is $1.504\text{ g mL}^{-1}$?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Interpretation of 68% by mass:
$100\text{ g}$ of aqueous solution contains $68\text{ g}$ of nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$).

2. Molar mass of $\text{HNO}_3$:
$$M = 1 + 14 + 3(16) = 1 + 14 + 48 = 63\text{ g mol}^{-1}$$

Question 1.10
NCERT Exercise
What role does the molecular interaction play in a solution of alcohol and water?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Molecular Interactions in Alcohol-Water Solution:
  • Both alcohol (e.g., ethanol) and water molecules exhibit strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding in their pure states.
  • When alcohol and water are mixed, the alcohol molecules intersperse between water molecules, breaking some of the original hydrogen bonds between water-water and alcohol-alcohol molecules.
  • The resulting intermolecular attractive forces between alcohol and water molecules (A-B interactions) are weaker than the pure water-water (A-A) and alcohol-alcohol (B-B) interactions.
  • Consequently, the escaping tendency of liquid molecules increases, leading to higher vapour pressure than expected from Raoult's law. Thus, the solution exhibits positive deviation from Raoult’s law ($Delta_{\text{mix}}H > 0$ and $Delta_{\text{mix}}V > 0$).

Question 1.11
NCERT Exercise
Why do gases always tend to be less soluble in liquids as the temperature is raised?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Reason:
  1. Exothermic nature of dissolution: Dissolution of a gas in a liquid involves condensation of gas molecules into the liquid phase, which releases heat ($Delta H_{\text{sol}} < 0$, exothermic process): $$\text{Gas} + \text{Liquid Solvent} \rightleftharpoons \text{Solution} + \text{Heat}$$
  2. Le Chatelier's Principle: According to Le Chatelier’s principle, an increase in temperature shifts the equilibrium in the direction that absorbs heat (endothermic direction, i.e., backward). Therefore, gas molecules escape from the solution into the vapour phase, reducing solubility.
  3. Kinetic energy: Higher temperature increases the average kinetic energy of dissolved gas molecules, enabling them to overcome intermolecular attractive forces and escape the liquid surface.

Question 1.12
NCERT Exercise
State Henry’s law and mention some important applications.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Henry’s Law Statement:
At a constant temperature, the solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas present above the surface of the liquid or solution.
Alternatively (most common formulation by Dalton): The partial pressure of the gas in vapour phase ($p$) is directly proportional to the mole fraction of the gas ($x$) in the solution.
$$p = K_H \cdot x$$
where $K_H$ is Henry’s law constant.

2. Important Applications:
  • Soft Drinks and Soda Water: Bottles are sealed under high pressure to increase the solubility of $\text{CO}_2$ gas.
  • Scuba Divers (Prevention of Bends): At deep sea, high pressure increases the solubility of atmospheric gases (including $\text{N}_2$) in blood. When divers surface, pressure decreases and dissolved nitrogen bubbles out, blocking capillaries (painful condition called bends). To avoid this, diving tanks are diluted with helium ($11.7\%\text{ He}, 56.2\%\text{ N}_2, 32.1\%\text{ O}_2$).
  • High Altitude Climbers (Prevention of Anoxia): At high altitudes, the partial pressure of oxygen is lower than at ground level. This causes low blood oxygen concentration in climbers, causing weakness and impaired thinking, a condition known as anoxia.

Question 1.14
NCERT Exercise
What is meant by positive and negative deviations from Raoult's law and how is the sign of $\Delta_{\text{mix}}H$ related to positive and negative deviations from Raoult's law?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Positive Deviation:
When the vapour pressure of a solution is higher than that predicted by Raoult’s law ($p_A > p_A^0 x_A$ and $p_B > p_B^0 x_B$), it shows positive deviation.
• Cause: Solute-solvent (A-B) interactions are weaker than A-A and B-B interactions.
• Sign of $\Delta_{\text{mix}}H$: Positive ($\Delta_{\text{mix}}H > 0$, endothermic) because more energy is required to break stronger A-A/B-B bonds than is released on forming weaker A-B bonds.
• Example: Ethanol + Acetone, Carbon disulphide + Acetone.

2. Negative Deviation:
When the vapour pressure of a solution is lower than that predicted by Raoult’s law ($p_A < p_A^0 x_A$ and $p_B < p_B^0 x_B$), it shows negative deviation.
• Cause: Solute-solvent (A-B) interactions are stronger than A-A and B-B interactions.
• Sign of $\Delta_{\text{mix}}H$: Negative ($\Delta_{\text{mix}}H < 0$, exothermic) because stronger A-B interactions release more energy during bond formation.
• Example: Chloroform + Acetone (due to new intermolecular H-bonding), Phenol + Aniline.

Question 1.20
NCERT Exercise
A $5\%$ solution (by mass) of cane sugar in water has freezing point of $271\text{ K}$. Calculate the freezing point of $5\%$ glucose in water if freezing point of pure water is $273.15\text{ K}$.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Depression in freezing point for cane sugar solution:
Freezing point of pure water ($T_f^0$) = $273.15\text{ K}$
Freezing point of sugar solution ($T_f$) = $271.0\text{ K}$
$$\Delta T_f(\text{sugar}) = 273.15 - 271.0 = 2.15\text{ K}$$
$5\%$ cane sugar ($\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11}$, $M_2 = 342\text{ g mol}^{-1}$) means $5\text{ g}$ in $95\text{ g}$ water ($0.095\text{ kg}$).
$$\text{Molality } m_{\text{sugar}} = \frac{5}{342 \times 0.095} = 0.1539\text{ mol kg}^{-1}$$
$$\Delta T_f = K_f \cdot m \implies K_f = \frac{\Delta T_f}{m} = \frac{2.15}{0.1539} = 13.97\text{ K kg mol}^{-1}$$

2. Depression in freezing point for $5\%$ glucose solution:
$5\%$ glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$, $M_2 = 180\text{ g mol}^{-1}$) in $95\text{ g}$ water.
$$\text{Molality } m_{\text{glucose}} = \frac{5}{180 \times 0.095} = 0.2924\text{ mol kg}^{-1}$$
$$\Delta T_f(\text{glucose}) = K_f \cdot m_{\text{glucose}} = 13.97 \times 0.2924 = \mathbf{4.085\text{ K } (\approx 4.09\text{ K})}$$

3. Freezing point of glucose solution:
$$T_f = T_f^0 - \Delta T_f = 273.15\text{ K} - 4.09\text{ K} = \mathbf{269.06\text{ K}}$$

Question 1.21
NCERT Exercise
Two elements $\text{A}$ and $\text{B}$ form compounds having formula $\text{AB}_2$ and $\text{AB}_4$. When dissolved in $20\text{ g}$ of benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$), $1\text{ g}$ of $\text{AB}_2$ lowers the freezing point by $2.3\text{ K}$ whereas $1.0\text{ g}$ of $\text{AB}_4$ lowers it by $1.3\text{ K}$. The molar depression constant for benzene is $5.1\text{ K kg mol}^{-1}$. Calculate atomic masses of $\text{A}$ and $\text{B}$.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Formula for molar mass from depression in freezing point:
$$M_2 = \frac{K_f \times w_2 \times 1000}{\Delta T_f \times w_1}$$

2. Molar mass of $\text{AB}_2$:
$w_2 = 1.0\text{ g}$, $w_1 = 20\text{ g}$, $\Delta T_f = 2.3\text{ K}$, $K_f = 5.1\text{ K kg mol}^{-1}$
$$M_{\text{AB}_2} = \frac{5.1 \times 1.0 \times 1000}{2.3 \times 20} = \frac{5100}{46} = \mathbf{110.87\text{ g mol}^{-1}}$$

3. Molar mass of $\text{AB}_4$:
$w_2 = 1.0\text{ g}$, $w_1 = 20\text{ g}$, $\Delta T_f = 1.3\text{ K}$
$$M_{\text{AB}_4} = \frac{5.1 \times 1.0 \times 1000}{1.3 \times 20} = \frac{5100}{26} = \mathbf{196.15\text{ g mol}^{-1}}$$

4. Calculating atomic masses of A and B:
Let atomic mass of A be $a$ and that of B be $b$.
$$a + 2b = 110.87 \quad \text{--- (1)}$$
$$a + 4b = 196.15 \quad \text{--- (2)}$$
Subtracting (1) from (2):
$$2b = 196.15 - 110.87 = 85.28 \implies b = \frac{85.28}{2} = \mathbf{42.64\text{ u}}$$
Substitute $b = 42.64$ into (1):
$$a + 2(42.64) = 110.87 \implies a = 110.87 - 85.28 = \mathbf{25.59\text{ u}}$$
$$\mathbf{\text{Atomic mass of A} = 25.59\text{ u}, \quad \text{Atomic mass of B} = 42.64\text{ u}}$$

[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Formula for osmotic pressure:
$$\Pi = C R T$$
Since temperature ($T = 300\text{ K}$) and gas constant ($R$) are constant, $\Pi \propto C$:

2. Initial concentration of glucose ($C_1$):

$\Pi_1 = 4.98\text{ bar}$, $\Pi_2 = 1.52\text{ bar}$

3. Calculating new concentration ($C_2$):
$$C_2 = C_1 \times \frac{\Pi_2}{\Pi_1} = 0.20\text{ mol L}^{-1} \times \frac{1.52\text{ bar}}{4.98\text{ bar}} = \frac{0.304}{4.98} = \mathbf{0.061\text{ M } (\text{or } 0.061\text{ mol L}^{-1})}$$

Question 1.27
NCERT Exercise
If the solubility product of $\text{CuS}$ is $6 \times 10^{-16}$, calculate the maximum molarity of $\text{CuS}$ in aqueous solution.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Dissociation equilibrium of CuS:
$$\text{CuS}(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + \text{S}^{2-}(aq)$$
If $S$ is the solubility (maximum molarity) of $\text{CuS}$ in $\text{mol L}^{-1}$:
$[\text{Cu}^{2+}] = S$, $\quad [\text{S}^{2-}] = S$

2. Solubility Product Expression:
$$K_{sp} = [\text{Cu}^{2+}][\text{S}^{2-}] = S \times S = S^2$$
$$S = \sqrt{K_{sp}} = \sqrt{6 \times 10^{-16}} = \sqrt{6} \times 10^{-8} = \mathbf{2.45 \times 10^{-8}\text{ mol L}^{-1} (\text{or M})}$$

Conclusion: The maximum molarity of $\text{CuS}$ in aqueous solution is $\mathbf{2.45 \times 10^{-8}\text{ M}}$.

Question 1.36
NCERT Exercise
$100\text{ g}$ of liquid $\text{A}$ (molar mass $140\text{ g mol}^{-1}$) was dissolved in $1000\text{ g}$ of liquid $\text{B}$ (molar mass $180\text{ g mol}^{-1}$). The vapour pressure of pure liquid $\text{B}$ was found to be $500\text{ torr}$. Calculate the vapour pressure of pure liquid $\text{A}$ and its vapour pressure in the solution if the total vapour pressure of the solution is $475\text{ torr}$.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Moles and Mole fractions:
$$n_A = \frac{100\text{ g}}{140\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.7143\text{ mol}$$
$$n_B = \frac{1000\text{ g}}{180\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 5.5556\text{ mol}$$
$$\text{Total moles} = 0.7143 + 5.5556 = 6.2699\text{ mol}$$
$$x_A = \frac{0.7143}{6.2699} = 0.1139, \quad x_B = 1 - 0.1139 = 0.8861$$

2. Vapour pressure of B in solution ($p_B$):
$$p_B = p_B^0 x_B = 500\text{ torr} \times 0.8861 = 443.05\text{ torr}$$

3. Vapour pressure of A in solution ($p_A$):
$$p_{\text{total}} = p_A + p_B \implies p_A = p_{\text{total}} - p_B = 475 - 443.05 = \mathbf{31.95\text{ torr } (\approx 32\text{ torr})}$$

4. Vapour pressure of pure liquid A ($p_A^0$):
$$p_A = p_A^0 x_A \implies p_A^0 = \frac{p_A}{x_A} = \frac{31.95\text{ torr}}{0.1139} = \mathbf{280.5\text{ torr}}$$

Question 1.37
NCERT Exercise
Vapour pressures of pure acetone and chloroform at $328\text{ K}$ are $741.8\text{ mm Hg}$ and $632.8\text{ mm Hg}$ respectively. Assuming that they form ideal solution over the entire range of composition, plot $p_{\text{total}}$, $p_{\text{chloroform}}$, and $p_{\text{acetone}}$ as a function of $x_{\text{acetone}}$. Explain why the experimental total vapour pressure is always less than the calculated ideal values.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Ideal vs Experimental Comparison:
If the mixture were ideal, the total and partial vapour pressures would follow Raoult’s law straight lines:
$$p_{\text{total}} = p_{\text{chloroform}}^0 + (p_{\text{acetone}}^0 - p_{\text{chloroform}}^0) x_{\text{acetone}} = 632.8 + (741.8 - 632.8) x_{\text{acetone}}$$ However, the experimentally observed vapour pressures for all compositions are significantly lower than the calculated ideal values.

2. Chemical Reason (Negative Deviation):
  • Pure acetone molecules are held by dipole-dipole interactions, and chloroform molecules by dipole-dipole interactions.
  • When acetone and chloroform are mixed, a strong new intermolecular hydrogen bond forms between the acidic hydrogen atom of chloroform and the basic carbonyl oxygen of acetone: $$\text{Cl}_3\text{C}-\text{H} \cdots :\text{O}=\text{C}(\text{CH}_3)_2$$
  • These new solute-solvent (A-B) attractive forces are considerably stronger than the original A-A and B-B forces.
  • This reduces the escaping tendency of both components from the liquid surface, leading to negative deviation from Raoult's law with $\Delta_{\text{mix}}H < 0$ and $\Delta_{\text{mix}}V < 0$.

Benzene and toluene form nearly ideal solutions over the entire range of composition. Calculate the mole fraction of benzene in vapour phase if $80\text{ g}$ of benzene is mixed with $100\text{ g}$ of toluene.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Moles of components:
Benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$, $M = 78\text{ g mol}^{-1}$): $n_B = \frac{80\text{ g}}{78\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 1.0256\text{ mol}$
Toluene ($\text{C}_7\text{H}_8$, $M = 92\text{ g mol}^{-1}$): $n_T = \frac{100\text{ g}}{92\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 1.0870\text{ mol}$
$$\text{Total moles} = 1.0256 + 1.0870 = 2.1126\text{ mol}$$

2. Mole fractions in liquid phase ($x_B$ and $x_T$):
$$x_B = \frac{1.0256}{2.1126} = 0.4855$$
$$x_T = 1 - 0.4855 = 0.5145$$

3. Partial vapour pressures:
$$p_B = p_B^0 x_B = 50.71\text{ mm Hg} \times 0.4855 = 24.62\text{ mm Hg}$$
$$p_T = p_T^0 x_T = 32.06\text{ mm Hg} \times 0.5145 = 16.49\text{ mm Hg}$$
$$p_{\text{total}} = p_B + p_T = 24.62 + 16.49 = 41.11\text{ mm Hg}$$

At $298\text{ K}$ if the Henry’s law constants for oxygen and nitrogen are $3.30 \times 10^7\text{ mm}$ and $6.51 \times 10^7\text{ mm}$ respectively, calculate the composition of these gases in water.
[3 Marks]

1. Partial pressures of $\text{O}_2$ and $\text{N}_2$ in air:

$$p_{\text{N}_2} = \frac{79}{100} \times 7600 = 6004\text{ mm Hg}$$

$$x_{\text{O}_2} = \frac{p_{\text{O}_2}}{K_H(\text{O}_2)} = \frac{1520\text{ mm Hg}}{3.30 \times 10^7\text{ mm Hg}} = \mathbf{4.606 \times 10^{-5}}$$

Question 1.41
NCERT Exercise
Determine the osmotic pressure of a solution prepared by dissolving $25\text{ mg}$ of $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_4$ in $2\text{ litre}$ of water at $25^\circ\text{C}$, assuming that it is completely dissociated.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
1. Given:
Mass of $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_4$ ($w_2$) = $25\text{ mg} = 25 \times 10^{-3}\text{ g} = 0.025\text{ g}$
Volume $V = 2\text{ L}$
$T = 25^\circ\text{C} = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15\text{ K}$
$R = 0.0821\text{ L atm K}^{-1}\text{ mol}^{-1}$ (or $8.314\text{ kPa L K}^{-1}\text{ mol}^{-1}$)
Molar mass of $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_4$ ($M_2$) = $2(39) + 32 + 4(16) = 78 + 32 + 64 = 174\text{ g mol}^{-1}$

2. van 't Hoff factor ($i$) for complete dissociation:
$$\text{K}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow 2\text{K}^+ + \text{SO}_4^{2-}$$
Number of ions produced per formula unit ($n$) = $2 + 1 = 3$.
Since complete dissociation ($\alpha = 1$): $i = 3$.

3. Osmotic pressure calculation:
$$\Pi = i \cdot \frac{w_2 R T}{M_2 V}$$
$$\Pi = 3 \times \frac{0.025\text{ g} \times 0.0821\text{ L atm K}^{-1}\text{ mol}^{-1} \times 298.15\text{ K}}{174\text{ g mol}^{-1} \times 2\text{ L}}$
$$\Pi = \frac{1.8358}{348} = \mathbf{5.275 \times 10^{-3}\text{ atm}}$$
In Pascals ($1\text{ atm} = 101325\text{ Pa}$):
$$\Pi = 5.275 \times 10^{-3} \times 101325 = \mathbf{534.5\text{ Pa } (\approx 5.35 \times 10^{-3}\text{ bar})}$$
