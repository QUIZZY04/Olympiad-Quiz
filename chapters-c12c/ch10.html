In-Text 10.1
NCERT In-Text
Glucose or sucrose are soluble in water but cyclohexane or benzene (simple six-membered ring compounds) are insoluble in water. Explain.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Reason for Solubility Difference:
  1. Glucose and Sucrose: A molecule of glucose ($ ext{C}_6 ext{H}_{12} ext{O}_6$) contains five polar hydroxyl ($- ext{OH}$) groups, and sucrose ($ ext{C}_{12} ext{H}_{22} ext{O}_{11}$) contains eight $- ext{OH}$ groups. Because of these multiple polar hydroxyl groups, glucose and sucrose form extensive and strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds with polar water molecules ($ ext{H}_2 ext{O}$). This favorable hydration energy overcomes the solute-solute forces, making them readily soluble in water.
  2. Cyclohexane and Benzene: Cyclohexane ($ ext{C}_6 ext{H}_{12}$) and benzene ($ ext{C}_6 ext{H}_6$) are non-polar hydrocarbon molecules without any polar functional groups or hydrogen-bonding groups. They cannot form hydrogen bonds with water molecules and cannot disrupt the existing strong hydrogen-bonding network among water molecules. Hence, based on the principle "like dissolves like", they are insoluble in water.

Presence of multiple -OH groups in glucose/sucrose forming extensive H-bonds with water1 Mark
Non-polar nature of cyclohexane/benzene unable to form H-bonds with water1 Mark

In-Text 10.3
NCERT In-Text
How do you explain the absence of aldehyde group in the pentaacetate of D-glucose?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Cyclic Hemiacetal Structure of Glucose:
D-Glucose exists primarily in two cyclic hemiacetal forms ($alpha$-D-glucopyranose and $eta$-D-glucopyranose) in equilibrium with a very tiny fraction of open-chain form containing a free $- ext{CHO}$ group at C-1.

When D-glucose is acetylated with acetic anhydride, all five hydroxyl groups are esterified to form glucose pentaacetate. Crucially, the $- ext{OH}$ group at the anomeric carbon (C-1) is also acetylated to form an acetate ester: $$ ext{Anomeric } - ext{OH} ext{ at C-1} \overset{}{\longrightarrow}_2 ext{O}} ext{Anomeric } - ext{OCOCH}_3$$ Because the C-1 position is now converted into an acetate (acetal-like linkage) and no longer a free hemiacetal $- ext{OH}$, it cannot revert back (hydrolyse reversibly) into the open-chain free aldehyde form in solution.

Consequently, glucose pentaacetate does not react with hydroxylamine ($ ext{NH}_2 ext{OH}$) to form an oxime or react with Schiff's reagent, confirming the complete absence of a free aldehyde ($- ext{CHO}$) group.

Acetylation of anomeric -OH at C-1 locks the ring into cyclic acetate1 Mark

In-Text 10.4
NCERT In-Text
The melting points and solubility in water of amino acids are generally higher than that of the corresponding halo acids. Explain.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Dipolar Zwitterion Structure of Amino Acids:
  1. Zwitterion Formation: Amino acids contain both a basic amino group ($- ext{NH}_2$) and an acidic carboxyl group ($- ext{COOH}$). In aqueous solution and in the crystalline solid state, an internal proton transfer takes place: $$ ext{H}_2 ext{N}- ext{CH}(R)- ext{COOH} ightleftharpoons mathbf{H_3N^+- ext{CH}(R)- ext{COO}^-} quad ( ext{Zwitterion})$$ Thus, amino acids exist as dipolar internal salts rather than simple neutral covalent molecules.
  2. High Melting Points: Because of the presence of positive ($- ext{NH}_3^+$) and negative ($- ext{COO}^-$) charges, solid amino acids possess strong electrostatic (ionic) attractions between oppositely charged ends in their crystal lattice, similar to inorganic ionic salts. In contrast, halo acids (such as $ ext{CH}_3 ext{CH(Cl)COOH}$) are covalent molecules held by weak dipole-dipole forces. Hence, amino acids have much higher melting points.
  3. High Water Solubility: The dipolar ionic Zwitterions interact very strongly with polar water molecules through strong ion-dipole interactions (high hydration energy), making them highly soluble in water compared to halo acids.

In-Text 10.5
NCERT In-Text
Where does the water present in the egg go after boiling the egg?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Protein Denaturation and Entrapment of Water:
The raw egg white contains water-soluble globular proteins (chiefly albumin) suspended in water.

When the egg is boiled (heated):
  1. The thermal energy disrupts the weak hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and hydrophobic interactions stabilizing the secondary and tertiary structures of albumin proteins.
  2. The compact globular polypeptide chains unfold, denature, and coagulate into a random, fibrous, cross-linked three-dimensional network.
  3. During this denaturation and coagulation process, the water present in the egg white is adsorbed, bound and physically trapped within the porous interstices of the coagulated protein matrix via hydrogen bonding. It does not escape or evaporate; it becomes chemically and physically entrapped.

In-Text 10.6
NCERT In-Text
Why cannot vitamin C be stored in our body?
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Water Solubility and Excretion:
Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble vitamin containing multiple polar hydroxyl ($- ext{OH}$) groups that form extensive hydrogen bonds with water.

Unlike fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) which dissolve in lipids and are safely stored in adipose tissues and liver:
  • Water-soluble vitamins readily dissolve in body fluids and blood.
  • Any excess quantity of vitamin C beyond immediate metabolic requirements cannot be held by fatty tissues and is readily filtered by the kidneys and excreted in urine.
Therefore, vitamin C cannot be stored in the body and must be regularly supplied in the daily diet (citrus fruits, amla, tomatoes, green leafy vegetables).
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Vitamin C is water-soluble, so excess is regularly excreted in urine and not stored1 Mark
Exercise 10.1
NCERT Exercise
What are monosaccharides?
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition:
Monosaccharides are the simplest carbohydrate units that cannot be hydrolysed further into simpler units of polyhydroxy aldehydes or polyhydroxy ketones.

General Characteristics:
  • They generally have the molecular formula $( ext{CH}_2 ext{O})_n$, where $n = 3 ext{ to } 7$.
  • If they contain an aldehyde group ($-\text{CHO}$), they are called aldoses; if they contain a keto group ($>\text{C}=\text{O}$), they are called ketoses.
  • Examples: Glucose ($ ext{C}_6 ext{H}_{12} ext{O}_6$, an aldohexose), Fructose ($ ext{C}_6 ext{H}_{12} ext{O}_6$, a ketohexose), Ribose ($ ext{C}_5 ext{H}_{10} ext{O}_5$, an aldopentose).

Exercise 10.2
NCERT Exercise
What are reducing sugars?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition:
All carbohydrates which reduce Tollens' reagent (to metallic silver mirror) and Fehling's solution (to a red precipitate of $ ext{Cu}_2 ext{O}$) are termed reducing sugars.

Essential Chemical Requirement:
  • The molecule must possess a free (or potentially free via hemiacetal equilibrium) aldehyde ($-\text{CHO}$) or $alpha$-hydroxy ketone group.
  • Examples:
    • All monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose) are reducing sugars.
    • Disaccharides like maltose and lactose are reducing sugars because one of the hemiacetal anomeric carbons is free.
    • Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar because both anomeric carbons (C-1 of glucose and C-2 of fructose) are mutually bonded in the glycosidic link.

Exercise 10.3
NCERT Exercise
Write two main functions of carbohydrates in plants.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Two Main Functions of Carbohydrates in Plants:
  1. Storage of Energy (Reserve Food Material): Starch is the major storage polysaccharide in plants. Plants synthesise glucose during photosynthesis and store it as starch in seeds, tubers, and roots, which serves as a reserve energy source for plant growth and germination.
  2. Structural Framework: Cellulose is a fibrous polysaccharide that forms the rigid cell walls of plant cells and plant tissues (wood, cotton, bark), providing structural rigidity, mechanical strength, and protection to plant cells.

Exercise 10.5
NCERT Exercise
What do you understand by the term glycosidic linkage?
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition:
A glycosidic linkage is an oxide linkage ($-\text{C}-\text{O}-\text{C}-$) formed between two monosaccharide units through the loss of a water molecule during condensation.

Formation Mechanism:
  • It involves the hemiacetal (or hemiketal) hydroxyl group at the anomeric carbon (C-1 of aldose or C-2 of ketose) of one monosaccharide unit condensing with an $-\text{OH}$ group of another monosaccharide unit.
  • Example in Sucrose: An oxide link between C-1 of $alpha$-D-glucose and C-2 of $eta$-D-fructose ($alpha, eta$-1,2-glycosidic bond).
  • Example in Maltose: An oxide link between C-1 of one $alpha$-D-glucose unit and C-4 of another $alpha$-D-glucose unit ($alpha$-1,4-glycosidic linkage).
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Definition: ether/oxide linkage (-C-O-C-) joining two monosaccharide units via condensation1 Mark
Exercise 10.6
NCERT Exercise
What is glycogen? How is it different from starch?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
What is Glycogen?
Glycogen is a polysaccharide that serves as the primary carbohydrate storage compound in animals and humans (hence also known as animal starch). It is stored predominantly in the liver and muscles. When the body requires glucose, enzymes break down glycogen to glucose.

Differences between Glycogen and Starch:
Feature Glycogen Starch
Occurrence Found in animals, fungi, and bacteria. Found in plants (cereals, roots, tubers).
Components Single homogenous branched polymer of $alpha$-D-glucose. Consists of two fractions: water-soluble amylose (15–20%) and insoluble amylopectin (80–85%).
Branching Degree Highly branched with branching points ($alpha$-1,6) every 8–12 glucose units. Amylose is unbranched ($alpha$-1,4); amylopectin is moderately branched ($alpha$-1,6 every 24–30 units).

[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Structural Difference between Starch and Cellulose:
Both are linear/branched high polymers of glucose, but they differ fundamentally in the monomer anomer and the stereochemistry of the glycosidic linkage:
Property Starch Cellulose
Monomer Unit Consists of $alpha$-D-glucose units. Consists of $eta$-D-glucose units.

Exercise 10.9
NCERT Exercise
What happens when D-glucose is treated with the following reagents?
(i) $\text{HI}$ (prolonged heating)
(ii) Bromine water
(iii) Concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Reactions of D-Glucose:
  1. Reaction with $\text{HI}$ (prolonged heating):
    Prolonged heating with hydroiodic acid ($ ext{HI}$) and red phosphorus reduces all hydroxyl and aldehyde groups to saturated hydrocarbons, yielding n-hexane. This proves that all six carbons in glucose are linked in a straight, unbranched chain: $$\text{CHO}-(\text{CHOH})_4-\text{CH}_2\text{OH} + 14\text{HI} \overset{}{\longrightarrow} \mathbf{\text{CH}_3-(\text{CH}_2)_4-\text{CH}_3 \text{ (n-hexane)}} + 7\text{I}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
  2. Reaction with Bromine water (mild oxidising agent):
    Bromine water selectively oxidises the terminal aldehyde group ($-\text{CHO}$) to a carboxylic acid group ($-\text{COOH}$) without affecting the secondary alcohol groups, yielding gluconic acid: $$\text{CHO}-(\text{CHOH})_4-\text{CH}_2\text{OH} + [\text{O}] \overset{}{\longrightarrow}_2 / \text{H}_2\text{O}} \mathbf{\text{COOH}-(\text{CHOH})_4-\text{CH}_2\text{OH} \text{ (Gluconic acid)}}$$
  3. Reaction with Conc. $\text{HNO}_3$ (strong oxidising agent):
    Nitric acid oxidises both the terminal aldehyde group at C-1 and the terminal primary alcohol group at C-6 to carboxylic acid groups, forming a dicarboxylic acid called saccharic acid (glucaric acid): $$\text{CHO}-(\text{CHOH})_4-\text{CH}_2\text{OH} + 3[\text{O}] \overset{}{\longrightarrow} \text{HNO}_3} \mathbf{\text{COOH}-(\text{CHOH})_4-\text{COOH} \text{ (Saccharic acid)}} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Exercise 10.10
NCERT Exercise
Enumerate the reactions of D-glucose which cannot be explained by its open chain structure.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Reactions Inconsistent with Open Chain Structure:
Although glucose behaves like an open-chain aldehyde in many reactions, the following observations cannot be explained by the simple open-chain pentahydroxy aldehyde formula:
  1. Does not form 2,4-DNP adduct or Schiff's test: Despite having an aldehyde group, glucose does not give Schiff's test with Schiff's reagent and does not form an addition product with sodium hydrogen sulphite ($ ext{NaHSO}_3$).
  2. Pentaacetate does not form an oxime: The pentaacetate derivative of glucose does not react with hydroxylamine ($ ext{NH}_2 ext{OH}$), indicating the absence of a free $- ext{CHO}$ group.
  3. Existence of two isomeric crystalline forms ($alpha$ and $eta$): Glucose exists in two distinct stereoisomeric forms:
    • $alpha$-D-glucose (m.p. $419 ext{ K}$, specific rotation $+112^circ$) obtained by crystallisation from ethanol at $303 ext{ K}$.
    • $eta$-D-glucose (m.p. $423 ext{ K}$, specific rotation $+19^circ$) obtained by crystallisation from hot pyridine at $371 ext{ K}$.
  4. Mutarotation: When either pure $alpha$ or pure $eta$ form is dissolved in water, the specific optical rotation slowly changes over time until reaching an equilibrium value of $+52.7^circ$.

Exercise 10.12
NCERT Exercise
Define the following as related to proteins: (i) Peptide linkage (ii) Primary structure (iii) Denaturation.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definitions:
  1. Peptide Linkage: An amide linkage ($-\text{CO}-\text{NH}-$) formed between the carboxyl group ($-\text{COOH}$) of one $alpha$-amino acid and the amino group ($-\text{NH}_2$) of an adjacent $alpha$-amino acid via the elimination of a molecule of water: $$\text{H}_2\text{N}-\text{CH}(R_1)-\text{COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{N}-\text{CH}(R_2)-\text{COOH} \overset{}{\longrightarrow}_2\text{O}} \text{H}_2\text{N}-\text{CH}(R_1)-\mathbf{CO-NH}-\text{CH}(R_2)-\text{COOH}$$
  2. Primary Structure: The primary structure of a protein refers to the specific linear sequence of $alpha$-amino acids linked together by peptide bonds in one or more polypeptide chains. Any change in this specific sequence generates a different protein with altered biological activity (e.g., in sickle cell anaemia where glutamic acid is replaced by valine).
  3. Denaturation: The process by which a native protein loses its unique three-dimensional conformation (secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures) and biological activity due to physical changes (heating, pressure) or chemical changes ($ ext{pH}$ alteration), while the primary structure remains completely intact.

Exercise 10.13
NCERT Exercise
What are the common types of secondary structure of proteins?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Secondary Structure of Proteins:
The secondary structure of proteins refers to the shape or conformation in which a long polypeptide chain can exist due to regular folding stabilized by hydrogen bonds between the $> ext{C}= ext{O}$ and $- ext{NH}-$ groups of peptide bonds.

The two main types are:
  1. $alpha$-Helix Structure: A polypeptide chain twists into a right-handed screw (helix) such that all $- ext{NH}$ groups form intramolecular hydrogen bonds with the $> ext{C}= ext{O}$ groups of adjacent turns situated 4 amino acid residues ahead. (e.g., keratin in hair/wool, myosin in muscle).
  2. $eta$-Pleated Sheet Structure: Polypeptide chains are stretched out side by side to nearly maximum extension and laid parallel or antiparallel to one another, held together by intermolecular hydrogen bonds, resembling pleated drapery. (e.g., fibroin in silk).

Exercise 10.16
NCERT Exercise
How do you explain the amphoteric behaviour of amino acids?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Amphoteric Nature of Amino Acids via Zwitterion:
Amino acids contain both a basic amino group ($-\text{NH}_2$) and an acidic carboxyl group ($-\text{COOH}$). In aqueous solution, the carboxyl group loses a proton while the amino group accepts a proton, creating a dipolar internal salt known as a Zwitterion: $$\text{H}_2\text{N}-\text{CH}(R)-\text{COOH} \rightleftharpoons \mathbf{H_3N^+-\text{CH}(R)-\text{COO}^-}$$ Because of this dual ionic character, the Zwitterion can react with both acids and bases:
  1. In Acidic Medium ($ ext{Low pH}$): The basic carboxylate group ($-\text{COO}^-$) acts as a proton acceptor (base) and takes up $ ext{H}^+$ to form a cation: $$\text{H}_3\text{N}^+-\text{CH}(R)-\text{COO}^- + \text{H}^+ \rightleftharpoons \mathbf{H_3N^+-\text{CH}(R)-\text{COOH} \text{ (Cation)}}$$
  2. In Basic Medium ($ ext{High pH}$): The acidic ammonium group ($-\text{NH}_3^+$) acts as a proton donor (acid) and releases $ ext{H}^+$ to hydroxide ion ($^-\text{OH}$) to form an anion: $$\text{H}_3\text{N}^+-\text{CH}(R)-\text{COO}^- + ^-\text{OH} \rightleftharpoons \mathbf{H_2N-\text{CH}(R)-\text{COO}^- \text{ (Anion)}} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
Since amino acids react with both acids and bases, they exhibit amphoteric behaviour.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Formation of dipolar Zwitterion (H₃N⁺-CH(R)-COO⁻)1 Mark

Exercise 10.17
NCERT Exercise
What are enzymes?
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Definition:
Enzymes are biological catalysts produced by living cells that catalyze complex biochemical reactions with extraordinary efficiency and absolute stereochemical specificity.

Chemical Nature & Characteristics:
  • Chemically, almost all enzymes are globular proteins.
  • They speed up biochemical reactions by a factor of $10^6$ to $10^{12}$ by lowering the activation energy barrier ($E_a$).
  • They operate under mild physiological conditions ($ ext{pH} \approx 7.4$ and temperature $\approx 310 ext{ K}$ or $37^circ ext{C}$).
  • They are extremely specific: each enzyme catalyzes only one specific reaction or works on one specific substrate (e.g., maltase hydrolyses only maltose into glucose, not sucrose).

Exercise 10.18
NCERT Exercise
What is the effect of denaturation on the structure of proteins?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Structural Effects of Denaturation:
When a native protein is subjected to physical changes (such as change in temperature) or chemical changes (such as change in $ ext{pH}$):
  1. Destruction of Higher Orders of Structure: The weak hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and hydrophobic interactions stabilizing the secondary ($2^\circ$), tertiary ($3^\circ$), and quaternary ($4^\circ$) structures are ruptured. Globules unfold and helical structures uncoil.
  2. Preservation of Primary Structure: The strong covalent peptide bonds ($-\text{CO}-\text{NH}-$) linking adjacent amino acids are NOT broken under denaturation conditions. Therefore, the primary structure remains completely intact.
  3. Loss of Biological Activity: The destruction of the unique 3D active site conformation leads to the complete loss of biological function (e.g., coagulation of egg white on boiling; curdling of milk due to lactic acid bacteria).
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Rupture of 2° and 3° structures (unfolding/uncoiling) causing loss of biological activity1 Mark
1° structure (covalent peptide sequence) remains completely undamaged1 Mark
Exercise 10.19
NCERT Exercise
How are vitamins classified? Name the vitamin responsible for the coagulation of blood.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Classification of Vitamins:
Vitamins are classified into two broad groups based on their solubility:
  1. Fat-Soluble Vitamins: Vitamins that are soluble in fats and oils (lipids) but insoluble in water. They are stored in adipose (fat) tissues and the liver.
    Members: Vitamin A, Vitamin D, Vitamin E, and Vitamin K.
  2. Water-Soluble Vitamins: Vitamins that are soluble in water. Because they are water-soluble, excess quantities are not stored in the body and are regularly excreted in urine, requiring constant dietary replenishment.
    Members: B-group vitamins (B₁, B₂, B₆, B₁₂, etc.) and Vitamin C (ascorbic acid). (Note: Vitamin B₁₂ is an exception that is stored in the liver for several months).
Vitamin for Blood Coagulation:
Vitamin K (phylloquinone) is responsible for the coagulation/clotting of blood. Deficiency of Vitamin K increases blood clotting time and causes haemorrhage.

Exercise 10.20
NCERT Exercise
Why are vitamin A and vitamin C essential to us? Give their important sources.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Importance and Sources of Vitamin A and Vitamin C:
  1. Vitamin A (Retinol):
    • Why Essential: It is necessary for normal vision (synthesis of rhodopsin pigment in retina), maintenance of healthy epithelial tissues, and immune function. Its deficiency causes night blindness (nyctalopia) and xerophthalmia (hardening of the cornea).
    • Important Sources: Carrots, cod liver oil, milk, butter, egg yolk, papaya, and green leafy vegetables (spinach).
  2. Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid):
    • Why Essential: It is a powerful water-soluble antioxidant essential for collagen synthesis (connective tissue, bones, cartilage), wound healing, and resistance against infections. Its deficiency causes scurvy (bleeding gums and tooth loss).
    • Important Sources: Citrus fruits (oranges, lemons), amla (richest Indian gooseberry source), tomatoes, green chillies, and sprouted pulses.

Exercise 10.21
NCERT Exercise
What are nucleic acids? Mention their two important functions.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
What are Nucleic Acids?
Nucleic acids are biopolymers (polynucleotides) found in the nuclei of all living cells, composed of repeating nucleotide monomers linked together by $3', 5'-phosphodiester bridges. They are of two types: Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and Ribonucleic acid (RNA).

Two Important Functions:
  1. Heredity & Transmission of Genetic Information: DNA is the chemical basis of heredity and serves as the reserve repository of genetic instructions. It replicates during cell division, transmitting identical genetic blueprints from parents to offspring.
  2. Protein Biosynthesis: Nucleic acids direct and regulate protein synthesis in the cell. The genetic code stored in DNA is transcribed into messenger RNA ($ ext{mRNA}$), which is subsequently translated into specific sequences of amino acids forming functional enzymes and structural proteins.

Exercise 10.23
NCERT Exercise
The two strands in DNA are not identical but are complementary. Explain.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Complementary Nature of DNA Strands (Watson-Crick Model):
The two polynucleotide chains in the DNA double helix are anti-parallel and run in opposite directions ($5' \to 3'$ and $3' \to 5'$). They are not identical because their base sequences are different; instead, they are complementary due to specific hydrogen bonding between purine and pyrimidine base pairs:
  1. Specific Base Pairing Rule:
    • Adenine (A) (a purine) always pairs exclusively with Thymine (T) (a pyrimidine) through two hydrogen bonds ($A = T$).
    • Guanine (G) (a purine) always pairs exclusively with Cytosine (C) (a pyrimidine) through three hydrogen bonds ($G \equiv C$).
  2. Automatic Sequence Determination: Due to this strict base-pairing geometry, wherever adenine occurs on one strand, thymine is mandatory on the opposite strand; wherever guanine occurs, cytosine is mandatory on the opposite strand.
Therefore, the sequence of bases on one strand automatically dictates the unique, exact complementary sequence on the other strand.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)

Exercise 10.25
NCERT Exercise
What are the different types of RNA which are found in the cell?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution
Three Major Types of Cellular RNA:
The cell contains three major types of ribonucleic acids ($ ext{RNA}$), each playing a distinct coordinated role in protein biosynthesis:
  1. Messenger RNA ($ ext{mRNA}$):
    Synthesised in the nucleus as a complementary transcript of a specific DNA gene. It carries the transcribed genetic code (in triplets of bases called codons) from the chromosomal DNA to the ribosomes in the cytoplasm for translation.
  2. Transfer RNA ($ ext{tRNA}$) (also called Soluble or Adaptor RNA):
    Small, cloverleaf-shaped single-stranded RNA molecules that read the codons on $ ext{mRNA}$ via their specific anticodon loop and transfer the appropriate activated amino acids to the growing polypeptide chain at the ribosome.
  3. Ribosomal RNA ($ ext{rRNA}$):
    The most abundant class of RNA (making up ~80% of total cellular RNA). Along with ribosomal proteins, it forms the structural and catalytic framework of ribosomes where peptide bond formation is enzymatically catalyzed.
