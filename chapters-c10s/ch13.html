Question 1
What are biodegradable and non-biodegradable substances? Give two examples of each. How do they affect the environment?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Comparison: Biodegradable vs Non-biodegradable Substances

  1. Biodegradable Substances:
    • Substances that can be broken down into simpler, non-toxic harmless substances by the biological enzymatic action of microorganisms (saprophytic bacteria and fungi) over a reasonable period.
    • They originate from plant and animal sources.
    • Examples: Fruit and vegetable peels, paper, cotton cloth, cow dung, agricultural wastes.
    • Environmental Impact: If accumulated excessively, they produce foul odor and breed disease vectors, but otherwise recycle naturally into soil humus.
  2. Non-biodegradable Substances:
    • Substances that cannot be broken down or degraded by physical or microbial enzymatic action; they persist in the biosphere unchanged for hundreds of years.
    • They are synthetic man-made compounds.
    • Examples: Plastics, polythene bags, DDT, heavy metals (mercury, lead, cadmium), synthetic pesticides.
    • Environmental Impact: Choke drainage systems, poison soil and groundwater, harm grazing cattle, and undergo dangerous Biological Magnification across food chains.

Question 2
What are trophic levels? Give an example of a food chain and state the different trophic levels in it. Explain the 10% Law of energy flow and why food chains are limited to 3-4 steps.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Trophic Levels and the 10% Law of Energy Transfer:

1. What are Trophic Levels?

Each distinct feeding step or level in a food chain where transfer of food energy takes place is called a Trophic Level:

  • First Trophic Level ($T_1$): Producers (Green plants synthesizing food via photosynthesis).
  • Second Trophic Level ($T_2$): Primary Consumers / Herbivores (Deer, grasshopper, cow).
  • Third Trophic Level ($T_3$): Secondary Consumers / Small Carnivores (Frog, fox, snake).
  • Fourth Trophic Level ($T_4$): Tertiary Consumers / Top Carnivores (Lion, eagle, tiger).

2. The 10% Law (Lindeman's Law of Trophic Efficiency):

Only about $10\%$ of the energy entering a particular trophic level is available for transfer to the next trophic level. The remaining $90\%$ is lost to the environment as metabolic heat during cellular respiration, digestion, locomotion, growth, and reproduction.

Numerical Example:

If $20,000\text{ Joules}$ of solar energy falls on green plants, plants capture only $1\%$ ($200\text{ J}$):

$$\text{Producers (Plants): } 200\text{ J}$$ $$\text{Herbivore (Deer): } 10\% \text{ of } 200\text{ J} = 20\text{ J}$$ $$\text{Carnivore (Lion): } 10\% \text{ of } 20\text{ J} = \mathbf{2\text{ J}}$$

Consequence: Because energy diminishes drastically at each level, food chains rarely exceed 3 to 4 trophic levels, beyond which the available energy is insufficient to sustain viable consumer populations.

Question 3
What is biological magnification? Will the levels of this magnification be different at different levels of the ecosystem? In which trophic level is it maximum?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Biological Magnification (Biomagnification):

1. Definition:

The progressive increase in the concentration of toxic, non-biodegradable chemical pollutants (such as synthetic pesticides, DDT, heavy metals) per unit weight of organisms at successively higher trophic levels in a food chain is called Biological Magnification.

2. How Biomagnification Occurs:

  1. Non-biodegradable pesticides like DDT are sprayed on agricultural crops to protect them from insect pests.
  2. These chemical residues are washed by rainwater into soils and water bodies (ponds, lakes, rivers).
  3. They are absorbed by aquatic plants (phytoplankton) along with water and minerals. Because these chemicals are non-biodegradable and fat-soluble, they cannot be metabolized or excreted by organisms and accumulate in fatty tissues.
  4. When zooplankton eat phytoplankton, small fish eat zooplankton, large fish eat small fish, and fish-eating birds or humans consume large fish, the concentration of DDT multiplies progressively at each ascending trophic level.

3. Maximum Accumulation in Humans:

Since human beings occupy the apex (top) of multiple terrestrial and aquatic food chains, the highest concentration of these toxic, carcinogenic chemicals accumulates in human tissues, causing hormonal disruption, neurological disorders, and cancer.

Question 4
What is ozone and how does it affect any ecosystem? Write the photochemical equations for its formation. How is ozone depleted, and what steps have been taken by UNEP to limit this damage?
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

The Ozone Layer and its Environmental Depletion:

1. Nature and Formation of Ozone ($\text{O}_3$):

Ozone is a triatomic molecule made of three oxygen atoms ($\text{O}_3$). While ozone is a deadly poison at ground level, in the Stratosphere (15 to 30 km above Earth) it forms a vital protective shield that filters out lethal ultraviolet (UV) radiation emitted by the sun.

Photochemical Formation of Ozone in Stratosphere:

$$\text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{High-energy UV}} \text{O} + \text{O} \quad \text{(Splitting of oxygen molecule into nascent oxygen)}$$ $$\text{O} + \text{O}_2 \longrightarrow \text{O}_3 \quad \text{(Formation of Ozone)}$$

2. Biological Importance of Ozone Shield:

High-energy UV-B radiation is severely harmful to life on Earth. Without the stratospheric ozone layer, UV radiation reaching Earth would cause:

  • Skin cancer and melanoma in humans.
  • Severe cataracts and permanent damage to eyes.
  • Suppression of the human immune system.
  • Destruction of marine phytoplankton, causing collapse of oceanic food chains.

3. Cause of Ozone Depletion:

  • The depletion of the ozone layer was primarily caused by synthetic chemicals called Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), widely used as refrigerants in refrigerators, air conditioners, aerosol propellants, and fire extinguishers.
  • In the stratosphere, UV rays split CFCs, releasing highly reactive free chlorine atoms ($\text{Cl}^\bullet$). A single chlorine radical can catalytically destroy over 100,000 ozone molecules before being neutralized.

4. International Corrective Action — Montreal Protocol (1987):

In 1987, the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) succeeded in forging an international treaty called the Montreal Protocol, legally freezing and subsequently phasing out the global production and use of CFCs, mandating CFC-free refrigerators worldwide.

Case Study Question • Kulhad vs Plastic Cups on Railways
A few years ago, disposable plastic cups were banned on Indian trains and replaced by clay cups (kulhads). Later, disposable paper cups were introduced. (i) Why were disposable plastic cups banned? (ii) What was the environmental problem caused by mass-scale use of kulhads? (iii) Why are disposable paper cups considered a better alternative?

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. (i) Reason for Plastic Cup Ban: Plastic cups are non-biodegradable. Billions of discarded cups littered railway tracks, choked storm water drainage systems, poisoned soil, released toxic dioxins when burned, and were ingested by stray cows causing fatal intestinal blockages.
  2. (ii) Problem with Kulhads: Making billions of clay kulhads daily required digging up massive quantities of rich, fertile agricultural topsoil. This caused severe depletion of topsoil, land degradation, and reduced agricultural fertility, alongside air pollution from brick kilns.
  3. (iii) Advantage of Paper Cups: Disposable paper cups are manufactured from renewable plantation wood pulp. They are completely biodegradable, decompose naturally within a few weeks without polluting soil or choking drains, and can be recycled efficiently without stripping fertile topsoil.
