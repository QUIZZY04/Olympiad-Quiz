Chapter 6: Control and Coordination

In-Text Page 105 • Q1
What is the difference between a reflex action and walking?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Comparison:

  1. Reflex Action: An instantaneous, involuntary, and automatic motor response to a sensory stimulus executed without conscious thinking or cerebral processing. It is mediated primarily by the spinal cord (e.g., withdrawing hand on touching a hot plate).
  2. Walking: A voluntary action initiated, coordinated, and controlled consciously by the cerebral cortex and cerebellum of the brain according to the organism's conscious will and intention.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Reflex: involuntary, unconscious, mediated by spinal cord1 Mark
Walking: voluntary, learned, conscious, controlled by brain (cerebrum/cerebellum)1 Mark
In-Text Page 105 • Q2
What happens at the synapse between two neurons?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Transmission across Synapse:

A synapse is a microscopic junction gap separating the axon terminal of one neuron from the dendrite of the next neuron.

  1. When an electrical nerve impulse reaches the axon terminal, it triggers synaptic vesicles to release chemical messenger molecules called neurotransmitters (e.g., acetylcholine) into the synaptic cleft.
  2. These chemical molecules diffuse across the microscopic synaptic gap and bind to specific receptor sites on the dendrite of the adjacent neuron.
  3. This chemical binding sets off a new electrical impulse in the receiving neuron. Because neurotransmitters are released only from axon terminals, impulses travel strictly in a unidirectional manner across synapses.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Electrical impulse releases neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft1 Mark
Neurotransmitters diffuse across synapse, bind to dendrite receptors, and generate new impulse1 Mark

In-Text Page 105 • Q4
How do we detect the smell of an agarbatti (incense stick)?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Mechanism of Olfactory Perception:

  1. Volatile chemical fragrance molecules from the burning incense stick diffuse into the air and enter the nasal cavity during breathing.
  2. These molecules dissolve in the nasal mucus and bind to specialized sensory receptors called olfactory receptors located in the olfactory epithelium.
  3. Olfactory receptors generate electrical nerve impulses that travel via the olfactory nerve to the olfactory lobes of the forebrain (cerebrum).
  4. The forebrain interprets the sensory signals by comparing them with previously stored memories of smells, enabling conscious recognition of the incense fragrance.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Olfactory receptors in nasal epithelium detect volatile fragrance molecules and generate impulses1 Mark
Impulses sent via olfactory nerve to forebrain (cerebrum) for interpretation1 Mark
In-Text Page 105 • Q5
What is the role of the brain in reflex action?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Role of the Brain in Reflex Actions:

  1. The brain plays no direct role in initiating or executing spinal reflex actions. To ensure survival and avoid tissue damage, reflex arcs are completed inside the spinal cord so the motor response occurs within milliseconds without waiting for slow cerebral processing.
  2. However, as the sensory neuron enters the spinal cord, a parallel relay neuron sends an informational signal upward to the brain.
  3. The brain receives this sensory information, creates a conscious awareness of the event (e.g., perceiving pain), and stores the memory to modify future voluntary behaviour.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Spinal reflex is executed autonomously by spinal cord without prior brain intervention1 Mark
Brain receives secondary signal for conscious awareness of pain and memory recording1 Mark
In-Text Page 108 • Q1
What are plant hormones (phytohormones)?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Definition:

Plant hormones (phytohormones) are naturally occurring organic chemical compounds synthesized in minute quantities in one part of a plant (such as shoot tips, root tips, young leaves) and translocated to other parts where they regulate physiological processes including growth, cell division, cell elongation, flowering, and responses to environmental stimuli.

Examples: Auxins, Gibberellins, Cytokinins (growth promoters), and Abscisic acid (growth inhibitor).

CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Chemical substances synthesized in minute amounts regulating growth and physiological responses1 Mark
Examples: Auxins, Gibberellins, Cytokinins, Abscisic acid1 Mark

In-Text Page 108 • Q4
How do auxins promote the growth of a tendril around a support?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Thigmotropic Mechanism of Tendrils:

  1. Tendrils of climbing plants (like pea, bitter gourd) are sensitive to contact.
  2. When a tendril touches an external physical support, auxin diffuses away from the side in contact towards the side away from the support.
  3. The higher concentration of auxin on the non-contact side stimulates rapid cell elongation and faster growth on that side compared to the contact side.
  4. This unequal, differential growth rate causes the tendril to curve inwards and coil tightly around the support like a spring, securing the climbing plant.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Auxin diffuses away from contact side to the side away from support1 Mark
Higher auxin causes greater cell elongation on outer side, coiling tendril around support1 Mark
In-Text Page 108 • Q5
Design an experiment to demonstrate hydrotropism.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Hydrotropism Demonstration:

  1. Setup: Take a flat tray filled with dry soil. Sow germinating pea seeds in the soil. At one end of the tray, place a porous clay pot filled with water.
  2. Observation: Keep the setup undisturbed for 4 to 6 days. As the seeds germinate, observe the direction of root growth.
  3. Result: The radicles (young roots) initially grow downwards due to positive geotropism, but soon curve and grow horizontally towards the moist porous water pot.
  4. Conclusion: This directional curvature of plant roots towards moisture demonstrates positive hydrotropism (overcoming positive geotropism).
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Experimental setup with germinating seeds and porous water vessel in dry soil1 Mark
Observation showing roots curving towards water source proves positive hydrotropism1 Mark
In-Text Page 112 • Q1
How does chemical coordination take place in animals?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Endocrine Chemical Coordination:

  1. Chemical coordination in animals is mediated by specialized ductless glands called endocrine glands.
  2. These glands synthesize and secrete chemical messengers called hormones directly into the bloodstream without any ducts.
  3. The circulating blood transports hormones throughout the entire body. Hormones act specifically on distant target tissues and organs that bear specialized complementary receptor proteins on their cell surfaces.
  4. Upon binding to target receptors, hormones initiate specific biochemical responses (e.g., insulin regulating blood glucose uptake, adrenaline accelerating heart rate).
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Ductless endocrine glands secrete hormones directly into bloodstream1 Mark
Hormones travel via blood to specific target organs bearing complementary receptors1 Mark
In-Text Page 112 • Q2
Why is the use of iodised salt advisable?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Reason for Using Iodised Salt:

  1. Iodine is an essential dietary mineral required by the thyroid gland to synthesize the hormone thyroxin.
  2. Thyroxin regulates the basal metabolic rate (BMR) and orchestrates carbohydrate, protein, and fat metabolism for balanced physical growth and mental development.
  3. If iodine is deficient in our diet, the thyroid gland cannot synthesize sufficient thyroxin and enlarges in an attempt to capture more iodine, causing a disease called Goitre (characterized by a swollen neck).
  4. Therefore, consuming iodised common salt provides the daily micro-requirement of iodine and prevents goitre and thyroid dysfunction.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Iodine is necessary for thyroid gland to synthesize thyroxin hormone1 Mark
Deficiency causes thyroid enlargement resulting in goitre and metabolic slowdown1 Mark
In-Text Page 112 • Q3
How does our body respond when adrenaline is secreted into the blood?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Physiological "Fight or Flight" Response to Adrenaline:

When an individual faces stress, fear, or physical danger, the adrenal medulla secretes adrenaline directly into the blood, triggering rapid systemic adjustments:

  1. Accelerated Heart Rate: The heart beats faster and pumps with greater stroke volume, supplying more oxygen to skeletal muscles.
  2. Diversion of Blood Flow: Smooth muscles around arterioles in the digestive system and skin contract, diverting blood supply primarily to active skeletal muscles.
  3. Increased Breathing Rate: Diaphragm and rib muscles contract faster, increasing pulmonary oxygen uptake.
  4. Glycogen Breakdown: Liver converts stored glycogen into glucose, flooding the bloodstream with metabolic energy to fight or flee.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Heart beats faster delivering more oxygen to skeletal muscles1 Mark
Blood diverted from skin/gut to muscles and breathing rate accelerates for emergency action1 Mark
In-Text Page 112 • Q4
Why are some patients of diabetes treated by giving injections of insulin?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Reason for Insulin Injections:

  1. Insulin is a peptide hormone produced by the $\beta$-cells of the Islets of Langerhans in the pancreas. Its primary role is to promote the cellular uptake of glucose and stimulate the liver and muscles to convert excess glucose into stored glycogen, keeping blood sugar within normal limits ($70 - 140\text{ mg/dL}$).
  2. In patients suffering from diabetes mellitus, the pancreas fails to produce adequate insulin. As a result, glucose cannot enter cells, leading to dangerously elevated blood sugar levels (hyperglycaemia) and loss of glucose in urine.
  3. Administering insulin injections artificially supplements the hormone, lowering blood sugar levels to normal physiological ranges and preventing organ damage.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Insulin produced by pancreas regulates and lowers blood glucose levels1 Mark
Diabetic deficiency causes hyperglycaemia; insulin injections restore normal blood sugar1 Mark

NCERT Exercise • Q4
What is the function of receptors in our body? Think of situations where receptors do not work properly. What problems are likely to arise?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Role of Receptors and Malfunction:

  1. Function of Receptors: Receptors are specialized sensory structures located in our sense organs that detect environmental stimuli (light, sound, heat, pressure, taste, smell) and convert them into electrical nerve impulses to inform the central nervous system.
  2. Problems When Receptors Malfunction:
    • Photoreceptors (Eyes): Inability to perceive light signals, leading to partial or complete blindness and inability to navigate hazards.
    • Thermoreceptors / Nociceptors (Skin): If heat and pain receptors fail, a person touching a scalding hot surface will not feel pain or withdraw their hand, resulting in severe tissue burns and ulceration.
    • Gustatory & Olfactory Receptors (Tongue & Nose): Loss of taste and smell (as in severe viral respiratory infections), impairing appetite and preventing detection of spoiled food or leaking toxic gas.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Function: Detecting external stimuli and converting into nerve impulses1 Mark
Consequences of pain receptor failure: Severe burns/injuries due to lack of withdrawal reflexes1 Mark
Consequences of sensory receptor failure: Loss of environmental awareness/hazards1 Mark
NCERT Exercise • Q5
Draw the structure of a neuron and explain its function.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Neuron Structure and Function:

  1. Structural Components:
    • Cyton (Cell Body / Soma): Contains a prominent nucleus, neuroplasm, and Nissl's granules.
    • Dendrites: Fine, branched protoplasmic projections radiating from the cell body that receive sensory inputs and chemical signals from adjacent neurons.
    • Axon: A long cylindrical cytoplasmic process wrapped in an insulating myelin sheath (interrupted at Nodes of Ranvier) that conducts impulses rapidly away from the cyton.
    • Axon Terminal / Synaptic Knobs: Bulbous nerve endings that store and release neurotransmitters.
  2. Function: Neurons are specialized excitable cells that perceive stimuli, convert them into electrical nerve impulses, transmit impulses across synapses, and coordinate rapid communication between sensory receptors, the central nervous system, and effector muscles/glands.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Neuron structure description: Cyton (cell body), dendrites, axon with myelin sheath, terminals1.5 Marks
Function: Receiving, conducting, and transmitting electrical nerve impulses1.5 Marks
NCERT Exercise • Q6
How does phototropism occur in plants?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Mechanism of Phototropism:

  1. When growing plant shoots detect unilateral light coming from one side, the plant growth hormone auxin synthesized at the shoot tip diffuses towards the shaded side of the shoot.
  2. The elevated concentration of auxin on the darker/shaded side stimulates cells to elongate more rapidly than cells on the illuminated side.
  3. Because the shaded side grows faster than the sunlit side, the shoot bends smoothly towards the light source, exhibiting positive phototropism. Roots exhibit negative phototropism by growing away from light.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Auxin synthesised at shoot tip diffuses to the shaded side1 Mark
Higher auxin concentration accelerates cell elongation on shaded side causing bending towards light1 Mark
NCERT Exercise • Q7
Which signals will get disrupted in case of a spinal cord injury?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Disrupted Signals:

The spinal cord acts as the central communication highway between peripheral nerves and the brain. In the event of a spinal cord injury, the following signals will be severely disrupted:

  1. Spinal Reflex Arcs: Automatic involuntary reflex actions below the level of injury (such as knee-jerk, withdrawal reflex) will be blocked.
  2. Ascending Sensory Signals: Sensory impulses carrying sensations of touch, temperature, pain, and pressure from lower body regions cannot reach the brain, causing loss of sensation (anaesthesia).
  3. Descending Motor Signals: Voluntary motor commands from the cerebral cortex cannot reach peripheral muscles, resulting in paralysis of limbs and loss of bladder/bowel control.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Disruption of spinal reflex actions below injury level1 Mark
Disruption of ascending sensory signals (pain/touch) and descending motor signals (voluntary paralysis)1 Mark
NCERT Exercise • Q8
How does chemical coordination occur in plants?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Chemical Coordination in Plants:

  1. Plants do not have a nervous system or muscular tissue. Coordination is carried out entirely through chemical substances called phytohormones (plant hormones).
  2. Different hormones are produced in response to environmental stimuli:
    • Auxins: Direct tropic curvatures (phototropism, geotropism) by differential cell elongation.
    • Gibberellins: Promote stem elongation and break seed dormancy.
    • Cytokinins: Promote rapid cell division in fruits and seeds and delay leaf senescence.
    • Abscisic Acid (ABA): Stress hormone that inhibits growth, causes stomatal closure during drought, and induces leaf fall (abscission).
  3. These chemical signals diffuse through plant tissues, activating biochemical responses in target cells.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Phytohormones synthesised in cells diffuse to regulate physiological growth responses1 Mark
Specific hormones and roles: Auxins (tropism), Cytokinins (cell division), ABA (growth inhibition)1 Mark
NCERT Exercise • Q9
What is the need for a system of control and coordination in an organism?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Need for Control and Coordination:

  1. Integration of Diverse Organs: A multicellular organism contains trillions of cells organized into specialized organs (heart, lungs, digestive tract, muscles). For the organism to survive, these organs must work in harmony and precise synchrony rather than independently.
  2. Survival and Response to Stimuli: Animals must perceive environmental changes (predators, food, danger) and execute immediate coordinated movements to survive.
  3. Homeostasis: It maintains internal physiological stability (body temperature, blood glucose levels, blood pressure, osmoregulation) despite fluctuations in the external environment.

NCERT Exercise • Q11
Compare and contrast nervous and hormonal mechanisms for control and coordination in animals.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Nervous vs Hormonal Mechanisms:

Property Nervous Coordination Hormonal Coordination
Signal Medium Electrochemical nerve impulses transmitted along specialized axons. Chemical molecules (hormones) circulating in the bloodstream.

NCERT Exercise • Q12
What is the difference between the manner in which movement takes place in a sensitive plant and the movement in our legs?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Comparison of Movement Mechanisms:

  1. Movement in Sensitive Plant (Mimosa pudica):
    • Plants lack nervous tissue and specialized contractile muscle proteins.
    • Movement is mediated by chemical transmission of signals that alter the turgor pressure in the pulvinus cells at leaf bases. By losing water, cells become flaccid, causing the leaves to droop.
  2. Movement in Our Legs:
    • Initiated by conscious motor nerve impulses from the brain acting across neuromuscular junctions.
    • Leg movements rely on specialized skeletal muscle cells containing specialized contractile proteins (actin and myosin) that actively slide across one another, changing cell shape and contracting the muscle.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Plant movement: changes in cell turgor pressure (water loss/gain) without muscles1 Mark
Leg movement: conscious nervous control causing sliding of contractile proteins (actin/myosin) in muscle cells1 Mark

Case Study Question • Feedback Mechanism in Endocrine Regulation
The timing and amount of hormone released are strictly regulated by feedback mechanisms. (i) Explain the negative feedback mechanism regulating blood sugar levels using insulin. (ii) Which organ detects the rise in blood glucose and which cells secrete the corrective hormone? (iii) What happens to hormone secretion once blood glucose returns to normal?

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. (i) Negative Feedback Mechanism: When blood sugar levels rise after a meal, this physiological deviation acts as a signal to the pancreas. The pancreas secretes insulin, which stimulates body cells to take up glucose and liver cells to convert glucose into glycogen. As blood sugar drops back to the normal baseline, the stimulus for insulin secretion disappears, turning off hormone release.
  2. (ii) Detecting Organ and Secretory Cells: The Pancreas detects the elevated blood glucose, and its $\beta$-cells of the Islets of Langerhans secrete insulin.
  3. (iii) Response upon Normalisation: Once blood glucose levels fall back to the normal physiological range ($70 - 100\text{ mg/dL}$ fasting), negative feedback inhibits the $\beta$-cells, causing insulin secretion to decline sharply, preventing hypoglycaemia.
