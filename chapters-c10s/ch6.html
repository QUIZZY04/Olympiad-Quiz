Question 1
Draw the structure of a neuron and explain how nerve impulses are transmitted from one neuron to another across a synapse.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Structure and Nerve Impulse Transmission in a Neuron:

A Neuron is the specialized structural and functional conducting unit of the nervous system.

1. Structural Parts of a Neuron:

  1. Dendrites: Fine, branching cytoplasmic projections that detect environmental stimuli and receive signals from sensory receptors or neighboring neurons.
  2. Cell Body (Cyton / Soma): Contains the nucleus, neuroplasm, and organelles; integrates incoming graded potentials.
  3. Axon: A long, cylindrical cytoplasmic fiber insulated by a fatty myelin sheath that rapidly conducts electrical impulses away from the cell body toward synaptic terminals.
Figure: Diagram of a Neuron showing Dendrites, Cell Body, Axon, and Nerve Endings

2. How Impulse Travels & Synapse Transmission:

  • Generation: Information received at the dendritic tip sets off a local chemical reaction that creates an electrical impulse (action potential).
  • Conduction: The electrical impulse travels rapidly through the cyton and along the length of the axon to its terminal end.
  • At the Synapse: A synapse is a microscopic junction between the axonal terminal of one neuron and the dendrite of the next neuron. When the electrical impulse reaches the axonal tip, it triggers the release of specialized chemical messengers called neurotransmitters (e.g., Acetylcholine) into the synaptic cleft.
  • These chemical molecules diffuse across the synaptic gap and bind to specific receptors on the dendrite of the adjacent neuron, generating a fresh electrical impulse. This chemical step ensures strictly one-way (unidirectional) transmission of nerve impulses.

Question 2
What is a reflex action? Trace the path of a reflex arc when you accidentally touch a hot object.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Reflex Action and Reflex Arc:

1. Definition:

A Reflex Action is a rapid, automatic, involuntary, and immediate motor response to an environmental stimulus that occurs without conscious thought or intervention of the cerebrum (e.g. instantly withdrawing hand on touching a boiling hot pan, knee-jerk reflex).

2. Sequence of Components in a Reflex Arc:

The neural pathway traversed by nerve impulses during a reflex action is called a Reflex Arc:

  1. Receptor: Sensory organ detecting the stimulus (e.g. thermoreceptors in finger skin detecting heat).
  2. Sensory Neuron (Afferent neuron): Carries electrical nerve impulses from the receptor to the spinal cord.
  3. Relay Neuron (Interneuron): Located in the grey matter of the spinal cord; processes the sensory signal and immediately transfers the impulse to a motor neuron.
  4. Motor Neuron (Efferent neuron): Transmits the motor command impulse from the spinal cord to the target effector organ.
  5. Effector: Target muscle or gland (e.g. biceps muscle of arm) that contracts to pull the hand away instantly from danger.
Figure: Schematic Pathway of a Reflex Arc (Receptor → Sensory → Spinal Cord → Motor → Effector)

Evolutionary Advantage: Because thinking by the conscious brain is relatively slow, reflex arcs evolved in the spinal cord to provide rapid emergency responses to safeguard the body from severe physical trauma.

Question 3
What are the major parts of the human brain? Mention the primary functions of cerebrum, cerebellum, and medulla oblongata.
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Structure and Functional Regions of the Human Brain:

The human brain is housed safely inside the bony cranium (skull) and surrounded by three protective cerebrospinal fluid-filled membranes called meninges. It is divided into three primary regions:

1. Forebrain (Prosencephalon):

  • The Cerebrum is the largest and most complex part of the brain.
  • Key Functions:
    (a) Main site of conscious thinking, reasoning, logic, memory, intelligence, and speech.
    (b) Sensory processing areas for receiving visual, auditory, olfactory (smell), gustatory (taste), and touch sensations.
    (c) Initiates voluntary muscular movements (walking, writing, speaking).
    (d) Contains specialized association centres and hunger/satiety centers.

2. Midbrain (Mesencephalon):

  • Connects forebrain to hindbrain.
  • Controls involuntary ocular reflexes (pupil dilation, eye movement) and auditory reflexes in response to sudden visual or sound changes.

3. Hindbrain (Rhombencephalon):

  • (a) Cerebellum: Responsible for precision of voluntary actions (threading a needle, walking in a straight line, riding a bicycle) and maintaining body posture, balance, and equilibrium.
  • (b) Medulla Oblongata: Controls vital involuntary life autonomic reflexes including blood pressure, salivation, heartbeat, swallowing, coughing, and vomiting.
  • (c) Pons: Participates in the regulation and rhythm of respiration.

Question 4
Name the plant hormones responsible for the following: (i) Growth of stem, (ii) Promotion of cell division, (iii) Bending of shoot towards light, (iv) Wilting of leaves.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Major Phytohormones (Plant Hormones) and their Biological Functions:

  1. 1. Auxins:
    • Synthesized at growing shoot and root apices.
    • Promote cell elongation and stem growth.
    • Mediate Phototropism: When unidirectional light shines on a shoot, auxins diffuse to the shaded side, stimulating cells on the darker side to elongate faster, causing the shoot to bend towards light.
  2. 2. Gibberellins:
    • Promote stem elongation and breaking seed/bud dormancy.
    • Promote fruit growth.
  3. 3. Cytokinins:
    • Promote rapid cell division (cytokinesis).
    • Present in high concentrations in regions of rapid cell division (fruits and seeds); delay leaf senescence.
  4. 4. Abscisic Acid (ABA):
    • Acts as a growth inhibitor (stress hormone).
    • Causes wilting and falling of leaves (abscission) and induces stomatal closure during water drought.

Question 5
Why is the use of iodised salt advisable? Name the disease caused by deficiency of iodine in our diet.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Physiological Importance of Iodised Salt:

  1. Hormone Synthesis: The Thyroid gland (located in the neck) requires Iodine as an indispensable trace element to synthesize the hormone Thyroxine ($\text{T}_4$).
  2. Function of Thyroxine: Thyroxine regulates the body's basal carbohydrate, protein, and fat metabolism, providing optimal energy balance and ensuring balanced physical and mental growth in human beings.
  3. Consequence of Iodine Deficiency: If our diet is deficient in iodine, the thyroid gland cannot synthesize sufficient thyroxine. In an attempt to compensate and absorb more iodine from the blood, the thyroid gland hypertrophies and enlarges abnormally, causing a swollen neck condition known as Simple Goitre.
  4. Conclusion: Using iodised table salt (salt fortified with potassium iodate $\text{KIO}_3$) ensures an adequate daily supply of iodine to prevent goitre and support healthy brain development.

Case Study Question • Diabetes & Hormone Regulation
A 50-year-old person experiences frequent urination, excessive thirst, and unexplained weight loss. Blood tests reveal an elevated fasting blood glucose level of 240 mg/dL. The doctor diagnoses Diabetes Mellitus. (i) Name the hormone and the endocrine gland responsible for regulating blood glucose levels. (ii) Why are some diabetic patients treated with daily insulin injections? (iii) What feedback mechanism controls insulin secretion?

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. (i) Hormone and Gland: Hormone is Insulin, secreted by the beta cells of the Islets of Langerhans in the Pancreas.
  2. (ii) Insulin Injections: In diabetic patients, the pancreas produces insufficient insulin or cells become resistant to it. Glucose cannot be absorbed into body cells or converted into glycogen in the liver, remaining elevated in the blood. Daily insulin injections supply the missing hormone, promoting cellular glucose uptake and normalizing blood sugar.
  3. (iii) Feedback Mechanism: When blood glucose levels rise after a meal, the pancreatic beta cells detect the surge and secrete more insulin. As blood glucose falls back to normal range, insulin secretion is automatically downregulated. This self-regulating system is called a negative feedback mechanism.
