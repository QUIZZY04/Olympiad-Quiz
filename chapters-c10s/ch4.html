Question 1
What are the two properties of carbon which lead to the huge number of carbon compounds we see around us?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Two Unique Properties of Carbon:

Carbon forms an exceptionally large number of compounds (over 3 million organic compounds) due to two unique chemical properties:

  1. 1. Catenation:
    Catenation is the unique ability of carbon atoms to form strong covalent bonds with other carbon atoms to give rise to long open chains, branched chains, or closed ring structures:
    • Carbon can form single bonds ($\text{C}-\text{C}$), double bonds ($\text{C}=\text{C}$), and triple bonds ($\text{C}\equiv\text{C}$).
    • Carbon-carbon ($\text{C}-\text{C}$) bonds are exceptionally strong and stable (bond dissociation energy $\approx 348\text{ kJ/mol}$) due to the small atomic size of carbon, which enables the nucleus to hold the shared electron pairs firmly. (Silicon shows catenation up to 7-8 atoms, but those chains are highly unstable and reactive).
  2. 2. Tetravalency:
    Carbon has an atomic number of 6 with an electronic configuration of $2, 4$. Possessing 4 valence electrons, it has a valency of 4. It can share electrons with four other carbon atoms or with atoms of other monovalent elements like Hydrogen ($\text{H}$), Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$), Oxygen ($\text{O}$), Nitrogen ($\text{N}$), and Sulphur ($\text{S}$), yielding a vast spectrum of compounds with diverse functional properties.

Question 2
What is a homologous series? State any three characteristics of members of a homologous series.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Definition and Characteristics of a Homologous Series:

A Homologous Series is a group or family of structurally similar carbon compounds in which members possess the same functional group, exhibit similar chemical properties, and consecutive members differ by a fixed molecular unit.

Key Characteristics:

  1. Successive Difference: Any two consecutive members of a homologous series differ in their molecular formula by a $-\text{CH}_2-$ (methylene) group.
  2. Molecular Mass Difference: The molecular mass of any two adjacent members differs by exactly $14\text{ u}$ (Carbon = $12\text{ u}$, $2 \times \text{Hydrogen} = 2\text{ u}$).
  3. Same General Formula: All members share a common general formula (e.g. Alkanes: $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2}$, Alcohols: $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+1}\text{OH}$).
  4. Gradation in Physical Properties: Melting points, boiling points, and densities show a steady, regular increase with increasing molecular mass due to increasing van der Waals forces.
  5. Identical Chemical Properties: Because all members have the same functional group, their chemical reactions and chemical reactivity are essentially identical.

Question 3
How many structural isomers can you draw for pentane? Draw their structures and write their IUPAC names.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Structural Isomers of Pentane ($\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12}$):

Compounds having the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements of atoms are called structural isomers. Pentane has 3 structural isomers:

  1. 1. n-Pentane (Pentane):
    Unbranched straight carbon chain:
    $$\text{CH}_3-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_3$$ IUPAC Name: Pentane.
  2. 2. Isopentane (2-Methylbutane):
    Four-carbon parent chain with a methyl branch at carbon-2:
    $$\text{CH}_3-\text{CH}(\text{CH}_3)-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_3$$ IUPAC Name: 2-Methylbutane.
  3. 3. Neopentane (2,2-Dimethylpropane):
    Three-carbon parent chain with two methyl branches at carbon-2:
    $$\text{C}(\text{CH}_3)_4$$ IUPAC Name: 2,2-Dimethylpropane.

Question 4
How would you distinguish experimentally between an alcohol and a carboxylic acid?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Distinguishing Ethanol ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$) and Ethanoic Acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$):

  1. Test 1: Sodium Hydrogen Carbonate Test ($\text{NaHCO}_3$ / Baking Soda):
    • Ethanoic Acid: Reacts briskly with sodium hydrogen carbonate to produce brisk effervescence of colourless, odourless Carbon dioxide gas ($\text{CO}_2$), which turns lime water milky: $$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}(l) + \text{NaHCO}_3(s) \longrightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COONa}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g)\uparrow$$
    • Ethanol: Being an extremely weak neutral compound, ethanol does not react with sodium hydrogen carbonate; no effervescence is observed.
  2. Test 2: Litmus Paper Test:
    • Ethanoic Acid: Turns blue litmus paper red due to liberation of $\text{H}^+$ ions.
    • Ethanol: Neutral liquid; has no effect on either blue or red litmus paper.

Question 5
Explain the terms: (i) Esterification, (ii) Saponification. Write chemical equations for both reactions and state their practical applications.
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

1. Esterification Reaction:

When an alcohol reacts with a carboxylic acid in the presence of a few drops of concentrated sulphuric acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) as a dehydrating catalyst, a sweet, fruity-smelling compound called an Ester is formed:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}(l) + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}(l) \xrightarrow{\text{Conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4, \Delta} \text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5(l) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$ $$\text{(Ethanoic acid)} + \text{(Ethanol)} \longrightarrow \text{(Ethyl ethanoate - Ester)} + \text{(Water)}$$
  • Characteristics: Esters have pleasant, fruity smells and are widely used in making perfumes, artificial flavouring essences, and cosmetics.
  • Role of Conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$: It acts as an acid catalyst and a dehydrating agent that absorbs water, shifting the reversible equilibrium forward to maximize ester yield.

2. Saponification Reaction (Preparation of Soap):

When an ester is heated with an aqueous alkali (strong base like sodium hydroxide $\text{NaOH}$), the ester is hydrolyzed back into sodium salt of carboxylic acid and alcohol:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5(l) + \text{NaOH}(aq) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CH}_3\text{COONa}(aq) + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}(l)$$ $$\text{(Ethyl ethanoate)} + \text{(Sodium hydroxide)} \longrightarrow \text{(Sodium ethanoate)} + \text{(Ethanol)}$$
  • Since this reaction is used in the commercial preparation of soap by hydrolyzing higher fatty acid triglycerides (vegetable oils or animal fats) with $\text{NaOH}$, it is called Saponification.

Question 6
Explain the mechanism of the cleansing action of soaps. What is a micelle? Why does soap not form lather with hard water?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Structure and Cleansing Action of Soap Molecules:

1. Molecular Structure of Soap:

A soap molecule is a sodium or potassium salt of long-chain fatty acids (e.g. sodium stearate $\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO}^-\text{Na}^+$). It has two distinct parts:

  1. Hydrophobic Tail: A long non-polar hydrocarbon chain ($\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}-$). It is water-repelling (hydrophobic) and oil-soluble.
  2. Hydrophilic Head: A short ionic polar carboxylate group ($-\text{COO}^-\text{Na}^+$). It is water-loving (hydrophilic) and soluble in water.

2. Mechanism of Micelle Formation:

  • Most dirt and grease on dirty clothes is oily and hydrophobic in nature.
  • When soap is added to water, the hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails point inwards into the oily dirt droplet, dissolving in the oil.
  • The hydrophilic ionic heads point outwards toward the surrounding water.
  • This spherical aggregate of soap molecules trapping oil at its core is called a Micelle.
Figure: Soap Micelle Trapping Oily Dirt in Center with Hydrophilic Heads Outwards

3. Emulsion and Rinsing:

The negatively charged heads on the surface of micelles repel one another, preventing the oily globules from coalescing into larger droplets. When the cloth is agitated mechanically and rinsed with water, the micelles carrying the trapped oily dirt are washed away, leaving the fabric clean.

4. Why Soap is Ineffective in Hard Water:

Hard water contains dissolved calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) and magnesium ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$) ions. These ions react with soap molecules to form an insoluble greyish curdy precipitate called Scum:

$$2\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa} + \text{Ca}^{2+} \longrightarrow (\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca}\downarrow + 2\text{Na}^+$$

This scum sticks to cloth fibers and wastes soap. Synthetic detergents do not form scum because their sulphonate/sulphate salts with calcium and magnesium are water-soluble.

Case Study Question • Ethanol Consumption & Denatured Alcohol
Pure ethanol is used widely in chemical industries and pharmaceuticals. However, commercial industrial alcohol is deliberately converted into 'denatured alcohol'. Furthermore, alcohol consumption affects human health. (i) What is denatured alcohol and how is it prepared? (ii) What happens biologically when a person drinks adulterated alcohol containing methanol? (iii) Write the reaction of ethanol with sodium metal.

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. (i) Denatured Alcohol: Industrial ethanol is made unfit for drinking to prevent its misuse and tax evasion by adding poisonous substances such as Methanol ($\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$), pyridine, and copper sulphate dye (giving it a blue colour). This poisoned alcohol is called denatured alcohol.
  2. (ii) Biological Effect of Methanol: Methanol is oxidised in the human liver by alcohol dehydrogenase to methanal (formaldehyde). Methanal reacts rapidly with protoplasm, causing cellular coagulation similar to an egg curdling. It also damages the optic nerve, causing permanent blindness and respiratory arrest leading to death.
  3. (iii) Reaction with Sodium:
    $$2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}(l) + 2\text{Na}(s) \longrightarrow 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{ONa}(alc) + \text{H}_2(g)\uparrow$$ It forms sodium ethoxide with brisk effervescence of hydrogen gas.
