In-Text Page 133 • Q1
If a trait A exists in 10% of a population of an asexually reproducing species and a trait B exists in 60% of the same population, which trait is likely to have arisen earlier?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Deduction:

Trait B is likely to have arisen earlier.

Reason: In an asexually reproducing population, individuals reproduce by cell division where offspring are near-exact genetic clones of the parent with only minimal copying variations. A trait that originates earlier has had many more generations to replicate, accumulate, and spread across a larger fraction of the population ($60\%$). Conversely, Trait A, being present in only $10\%$ of the population, is a more recent mutation.

How does the creation of variations in a species promote survival?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Survival Value of Variations:

  1. Environmental niches are subject to drastic, unforeseen changes (such as sudden global warming, drought, freezing temperatures, or water acidity).
  2. If an entire population is genetically identical, an environmental catastrophe can wipe out all individuals simultaneously.
  3. However, if variations exist among individuals, some variants will possess pre-adapted traits (such as heat tolerance in bacteria) that allow them to survive the change. These surviving variants reproduce, ensuring that the species does not face extinction.

In-Text Page 138 • Q1
How do Mendel's experiments show that traits may be dominant or recessive?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Mendel's Monohybrid Cross:

  1. Parental Cross: Mendel crossed pure-breeding tall pea plants ($TT$) with pure-breeding dwarf pea plants ($tt$).
  2. $F_1$ Generation Observation: In the first filial ($F_1$) generation, all plants were tall. There were no medium-height or dwarf plants. $$TT \times tt \longrightarrow \text{All } Tt \text{ (Tall)}$$
  3. $F_2$ Generation (Self-pollination of $F_1$): When $F_1$ tall plants were self-pollinated ($Tt \times Tt$), both tall and dwarf plants reappeared in the $F_2$ generation in a strict $3 : 1$ ratio (75% tall, 25% dwarf).
  4. Deduction: The dwarf trait ($t$) was present in the $F_1$ generation but remained unexpressed in the presence of the tall allele ($T$).
    • The trait that expresses itself in $F_1$ is Dominant (Tall).
    • The trait that remains hidden in $F_1$ and reappears in $F_2$ is Recessive (Dwarf).
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Monohybrid cross TT x tt yields 100% tall plants in F1 generation1 Mark
Selfing F1 yields 3 tall : 1 dwarf phenotypic ratio in F21 Mark
Concludes: Expressed trait in F1 is dominant; hidden trait reappearing in F2 is recessive1 Mark
In-Text Page 138 • Q2
How do Mendel's experiments show that traits are inherited independently?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Mendel's Dihybrid Cross:

  1. Parental Cross: Mendel crossed a pea plant having Round Yellow seeds ($RRYY$) with a plant having Wrinkled Green seeds ($rryy$).
  2. $F_1$ Progeny: All $F_1$ plants produced Round Yellow seeds ($RrYy$) because round and yellow are dominant traits.
  3. $F_2$ Generation (Selfing $F_1$): When $F_1$ plants were selfed ($RrYy \times RrYy$), four distinct seed phenotypes were produced in the $F_2$ generation:
    • Round Yellow: 9 (Parental)
    • Round Green: 3 (Recombinant)
    • Wrinkled Yellow: 3 (Recombinant)
    • Wrinkled Green: 1 (Parental)
    Phenotypic dihybrid ratio = $9 : 3 : 3 : 1$.
  4. Conclusion (Law of Independent Assortment): The appearance of completely new non-parental combinations (Round Green and Wrinkled Yellow) proves that the inheritance of seed shape allele ($R/r$) is completely independent of the inheritance of seed colour allele ($Y/y$).
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Dihybrid cross RRYY x rryy yields 100% Round Yellow (RrYy) in F11 Mark
F2 selfing yields 9:3:3:1 ratio with new recombinant phenotypes (Round Green & Wrinkled Yellow)1 Mark
Deduces Law of Independent Assortment: Alleles assort independently into gametes1 Mark
In-Text Page 138 • Q3
A man with blood group A marries a woman with blood group O and their daughter has blood group O. Is this information enough to tell you which of the traits – blood group A or O – is dominant? Why or why not?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Genotypic Analysis:

  1. Answer: No, this information alone is not sufficient to establish which blood group allele is dominant.
  2. Case 1 (If A is dominant over O):
    • Mother with blood group O must be homozygous: $I^O I^O$.
    • Father with blood group A could be heterozygous: $I^A I^O$.
    • Their daughter inherits $I^O$ from father and $I^O$ from mother → daughter has blood group O ($I^O I^O$). This is consistent.
  3. Case 2 (If O were dominant over A):
    • Father with blood group A would be homozygous $I^A I^A$.
    • Mother with blood group O could be heterozygous $I^O I^A$.
    • Daughter could inherit $I^O$ from mother and $I^A$ from father → daughter has blood group O. This could also be theoretically possible.
  4. Without knowing the genotypic pedigree of other siblings or extended family, the cross alone does not deduce dominance. (From biological knowledge, $I^A$ is dominant and $I^O$ is recessive).
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Not enough information from a single cross1 Mark
Father could be heterozygous IAIO or mother could be heterozygous; both models explain daughter having group O1 Mark
In-Text Page 138 • Q4
How is the sex of the child determined in human beings?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Chromosomal Mechanism of Sex Determination:

  1. Human beings possess 23 pairs of chromosomes in somatic cell nuclei: 22 pairs of autosomes and 1 pair of sex chromosomes.
  2. Female (Homomorphic): Females have two identical X chromosomes ($XX$). All maternal ova produced during meiosis carry one X chromosome ($22 + X$).
  3. Male (Heteromorphic): Males have one X chromosome and one shorter Y chromosome ($XY$). Spermatogenesis produces two equal types of sperms in a $50 : 50$ ratio:
    • $50\%$ of sperms carry an $X$ chromosome ($22 + X$).
    • $50\%$ of sperms carry a $Y$ chromosome ($22 + Y$).
  4. Fertilisation:
    • If an ovum is fertilised by an X-bearing sperm, the zygote is $XX$ → develops into a Female child (Girl).
    • If an ovum is fertilised by a Y-bearing sperm, the zygote is $XY$ → develops into a Male child (Boy).
  5. Therefore, sex is genetically determined at the moment of fertilisation by the father's sperm with an exact $50\%$ theoretical probability for either sex.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Females are XX producing only X-type ova; Males are XY producing 50% X and 50% Y sperms1 Mark
Fertilisation: X sperm + X ovum -> XX female; Y sperm + X ovum -> XY male1 Mark
Father determines the sex of child with 1:1 statistical probability1 Mark

Correct Option: (c) TtWW

  3. Combining both traits, the genetic makeup of the tall parent is $TtWW$.

NCERT Exercise • Q2
A study found that children with light-coloured eyes are likely to have parents with light-coloured eyes. On this basis, can we say whether the light eye colour trait is dominant or recessive? Why or why not?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Scientific Analysis:

No, we cannot definitively conclude whether the light eye colour trait is dominant or recessive based solely on this observation.

Reason:

  • If light eye colour is a recessive trait ($bb$), two light-eyed parents ($bb \times bb$) will produce $100\%$ light-eyed children ($bb$).
  • If light eye colour were a dominant trait ($BB$ or $Bb$), two light-eyed parents could also produce light-eyed children ($BB \times BB \longrightarrow BB$).
  • To determine dominance conclusively, we require crosses between contrasting phenotypes (e.g., light-eyed parent $\times$ dark-eyed parent) across at least two generations to observe which trait masks the other in $F_1$ and reappears in $F_2$.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Cannot determine dominance without reciprocal contrasting crosses across two generations1 Mark
Homozygous recessive and homozygous dominant both produce identical offspring phenotypes1 Mark

Outline a project which aims to find the dominant coat colour in dogs.

Project Outline:

  1. Selection of Pure-breeding Parents: Select pure-breeding homozygous dogs of two distinct contrasting coat colours, for example, pure black coat ($BB$) and pure white coat ($bb$).
  2. First Generation ($F_1$ Cross): Cross the pure black dog with the pure white dog ($BB \times bb$). Observe the coat colour of all puppies in the $F_1$ litter.

  4. $F_2$ Verification: Interbreed $F_1$ black dogs ($Bb \times Bb$). The appearance of black and white puppies in approximately a $3 : 1$ ratio in the $F_2$ generation confirms that black is dominant and white is recessive.

NCERT Exercise • Q4
Explain how sexual reproduction gives rise to more viable variations than asexual reproduction. How does this affect the evolution of those organisms that reproduce sexually?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Origin of Variations in Sexual Reproduction:

  1. Biparental Inheritance: Sexual reproduction combines genetic material from two distinct parents, each carrying unique mutations and allele combinations.
  2. Meiotic Recombination & Independent Assortment: During gamete formation, homologous chromosomes undergo crossing over (reciprocal exchange of genetic segments) and maternal and paternal chromosomes assort independently, creating millions of genetically unique gametes.
  3. Random Fertilisation: Any male gamete can randomly fertilise any female gamete ($2^{23} \times 2^{23}$ combinations in humans), creating virtually infinite variation in the zygotes.
  4. Impact on Evolution: In contrast to asexual reproduction where variations arise solely from rare replication errors, sexual reproduction continuously generates vast pools of genetic diversity. Natural selection acts on these diverse variations, selecting advantageous adaptations and accelerating evolutionary divergence and speciation.

Biparental genetic contribution combines diverse alleles from two individuals1 Mark
Crossing over during meiosis and independent assortment generates unique gametes1 Mark
Rich variation pool accelerates natural selection and evolutionary adaptation1 Mark
NCERT Exercise • Q5
How is the equal genetic contribution of male and female parents ensured in the progeny?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Mechanism Ensuring Equal Genetic Contribution:

  1. Somatic body cells in human parents are diploid ($2n$), containing two complete sets of chromosomes (46 chromosomes).
  2. During gametogenesis, specialized germ cells undergo reductional cell division (Meiosis), halving the chromosome number. Consequently, both sperm (male gamete) and ovum (female gamete) are haploid ($n$), carrying exactly 23 chromosomes.
  3. During fertilisation, the haploid nucleus of the sperm fuses with the haploid nucleus of the ovum: $$n\text{ (from Father)} + n\text{ (from Mother)} \longrightarrow 2n\text{ (Zygote)}$$
  4. The resulting zygote receives exactly 23 maternal chromosomes and 23 paternal chromosomes, ensuring identical $50\%$ genetic contribution from both parents.
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Meiosis halves chromosome number producing haploid gametes (n = 23 chromosomes)1 Mark
Fertilisation restores diploid status (2n = 46), ensuring exact 50% contribution from each parent1 Mark
NCERT Exercise • Q6
Only variations that confer an advantage to an individual organism will survive in a population. Do you agree with this statement? Why or why not?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Critical Evaluation:

No, the statement is not completely accurate.

Reason:

  1. While it is true that variations offering an immediate adaptive advantage are positively favored by natural selection and spread rapidly in a population, not all surviving variations are advantageous.
  2. Neutral Variations: Many genetic variations are functionally neutral (they neither help nor hinder survival, such as free vs attached earlobes or different fingerprint patterns). These neutral traits persist in populations over generations without being eliminated.
  3. Genetic Drift: In small populations, chance random events (accidents, floods, fires) can cause non-advantageous or even disadvantageous alleles to become fixed purely by random sampling errors rather than adaptive survival.

NCERT Exercise • Q7
How did Mendel explain that traits may be dominant or recessive? Explain with monohybrid cross.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Law of Dominance:

Mendel crossed a homozygous tall pea plant ($TT$) with a homozygous dwarf plant ($tt$).

  • In the $F_1$ generation, all offspring plants possessed the hybrid genotype $Tt$, but exhibited the tall phenotype.
  • The dwarf trait seemed to disappear. When $F_1$ plants were self-pollinated ($Tt \times Tt$), the dwarf trait reappeared in $25\%$ of the $F_2$ plants ($3\text{ Tall} : 1\text{ Dwarf}$).
  • Mendel concluded that each trait is controlled by a pair of distinct unit factors (alleles). In a dissimilar pair ($Tt$), one factor masks the phenotypic expression of the other. The allele that expresses itself is called Dominant ($T$), and the masked allele is called Recessive ($t$).
CBSE Board Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Cross TT x tt yields all Tt (tall) in F1; selfing produces 3:1 in F21 Mark
In heterozygous pair Tt, the expressed allele is dominant and suppressed allele is recessive1 Mark

Definition and Number of Chromosomes:

Definitions:

  1. Alleles: Alternative forms of a given gene located at the same locus on homologous chromosomes that govern contrasting expressions of a trait (e.g., $T$ for tallness and $t$ for dwarfness).

Board Benchmark • Q20
State Mendel's Law of Segregation and Law of Independent Assortment.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Mendel's Laws of Inheritance:

  1. 1. Law of Segregation (Purity of Gametes): During gamete formation (meiosis), the paired alleles of a gene segregate (separate) from each other randomly such that each gamete receives only one allele of the pair with equal probability.
  2. 2. Law of Independent Assortment: When two pairs of contrasting traits are combined in a hybrid (dihybrid cross), the segregation of alleles of one gene pair into gametes is completely independent of the segregation of alleles of the other gene pair.

Case Study Question • Pedigree & Blood Group Inheritance
In a human maternity hospital, two babies got mixed up. Baby 1 has blood group O, and Baby 2 has blood group AB. Couple X has Father with blood group A and Mother with blood group B. Couple Y has Father with blood group AB and Mother with blood group O. (i) Determine the child of Couple Y with genetic justification. (ii) Can Couple X be the biological parents of Baby 1? Explain the required genotypes. (iii) Which blood group alleles exhibit codominance in humans?

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. (i) Couple Y: Father has group AB ($I^A I^B$) and Mother has group O ($I^O I^O$).
    Possible children: $I^A I^O$ (Group A) or $I^B I^O$ (Group B).
    Couple Y can never have a child with blood group O ($I^O I^O$) or AB ($I^A I^B$).
    Therefore, an error in hospital labeling occurred: Baby 1 cannot belong to Y. However, between Couple X ($I^A I^O \times I^B I^O$), all four blood groups (A, B, AB, O) can be born!
  2. (ii) Couple X: Yes, if Father is heterozygous A ($I^A I^O$) and Mother is heterozygous B ($I^B I^O$), they have a $25\%$ probability of having a child with blood group O ($I^O I^O$) and $25\%$ probability of blood group AB ($I^A I^B$).
  3. (iii) Codominance: Alleles $I^A$ and $I^B$ exhibit codominance: when both are present together ($I^A I^B$), both A and B antigens are expressed on RBCs, producing blood group AB.
