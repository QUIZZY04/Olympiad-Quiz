Question 1
How do inherited traits differ from acquired traits? Give two examples of each. Why are traits acquired during the lifetime of an individual not inherited?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Comparison: Inherited Traits vs Acquired Traits

  1. Inherited Traits:
    • These are traits determined by specific genes located on DNA inside reproductive germ cells (gametes).
    • They are transmitted from parents to their progeny from generation to generation.
    • Examples: Eye colour, skin pigmentation, natural hair texture, blood group, attached or free earlobes.
  2. Acquired Traits:
    • These are phenotypic characteristics developed by an individual during its lifetime in response to environmental conditions, learning, injury, or physical training.
    • They involve changes only in non-reproductive somatic cells, without altering the DNA nucleotide sequence of germ cells.
    • Therefore, acquired traits cannot be inherited by offspring.
    • Examples: Muscular physique from bodybuilding, loss of a limb or tail in an accident, knowledge of swimming or playing a musical instrument. (e.g., Even if tails of mice are surgically removed for 20 successive generations, tailless mice are never born because the surgery does not alter germ cell DNA).

Question 2
How did Mendel explain that traits may be dominant or recessive? Explain with the help of a monohybrid cross, showing F1 and F2 generations with phenotypic and genotypic ratios.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Mendel's Monohybrid Cross (Inheritance of One Pair of Contrasting Characters):

1. Cross Setup:

Mendel crossed pure-breeding homozygous Tall pea plants ($TT$) with pure-breeding homozygous Dwarf pea plants ($tt$).

2. First Filial Generation ($F_1$):

$$\text{Parents: } TT \times tt \longrightarrow \text{Gametes: } T \text{ and } t$$ $$F_1 \text{ Progeny: } Tt \quad (100\% \text{ Phenotypically Tall})$$
  • Even though the plants inherited both factors ($T$ and $t$), only the Tall trait was expressed. Therefore, the trait for Tallness ($T$) is Dominant, whereas the dwarf trait ($t$) is Recessive.

3. Second Filial Generation ($F_2$ by Self-pollination of $F_1$):

$$\text{Selfing } F_1: Tt \times Tt$$
Gametes $T$ $t$
$T$ $TT$ (Tall) $Tt$ (Tall)
$t$ $Tt$ (Tall) $tt$ (Dwarf)

4. $F_2$ Ratios:

  • Phenotypic Ratio: $\mathbf{3 \text{ Tall} : 1 \text{ Dwarf}}$ ($75\%$ Tall, $25\%$ Dwarf).
  • Genotypic Ratio: $\mathbf{1\, TT : 2\, Tt : 1\, tt}$ ($1 : 2 : 1$).
  • Conclusion: Both dominant and recessive traits are inherited independently, but recessive traits are expressed only in the homozygous condition ($tt$).

Question 3
How did Mendel's experiments show that traits are inherited independently? Describe the dihybrid cross between round yellow and wrinkled green pea plants with the 9:3:3:1 ratio.
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Mendel's Dihybrid Cross & Law of Independent Assortment:

1. Cross Setup:

Mendel crossed a pea plant having Round Yellow seeds ($RRYY$) with a plant having Wrinkled Green seeds ($rryy$).

  • $R$ = Round (dominant), $r$ = Wrinkled (recessive)
  • $Y$ = Yellow (dominant), $y$ = Green (recessive)
  • Gametes: $RY$ and $ry \implies F_1$ Generation: $\mathbf{RrYy}$ ($100\%$ Round Yellow seeds).

2. $F_2$ Generation (Selfing $RrYy \times RrYy$):

Each $F_1$ plant produces 4 types of gametes in equal proportions: $RY, Ry, rY, ry$.

A $4 \times 4$ Punnett square yields 16 combinations:

  1. Round Yellow ($R\_Y\_$): 9 plants
  2. Round Green ($R\_yy$): 3 plants (New combination)
  3. Wrinkled Yellow ($rrY\_$): 3 plants (New combination)
  4. Wrinkled Green ($rryy$): 1 plant

3. Classical $F_2$ Dihybrid Phenotypic Ratio:

$$\mathbf{9 : 3 : 3 : 1}$$

4. Law of Independent Assortment:

The appearance of new recombinant phenotypes (Round Green and Wrinkled Yellow) proves that when two pairs of contrasting traits are combined in a hybrid, the segregation and assortment of one pair of characters is completely independent of the other pair of characters during gamete formation.

Question 4
How is the sex of the child determined in human beings? Explain with a genetic cross diagram why women should not be blamed for giving birth to girl children.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Genetic Mechanism of Sex Determination in Human Beings:

1. Chromosome Complement:

  • Human somatic cells contain 23 pairs (46 chromosomes):
    • 22 pairs are identical in both sexes and are called Autosomes.
    • 1 pair (the 23rd pair) determines the sex and is called Sex Chromosomes (Allosomes).
  • Human females possess a perfectly matched pair of sex chromosomes: $\mathbf{XX}$.
  • Human males possess an unmatched pair consisting of one normal-sized $X$ and a shorter $Y$ chromosome: $\mathbf{XY}$.

2. Gamete Production & Cross:

  1. All female gametes (ova / eggs) carry only an $\mathbf{X}$ chromosome ($22 + X$). Females are homogametic.
  2. Male gametes (sperms) are of two types ($50\%$ carry an $\mathbf{X}$ chromosome, $50\%$ carry a $\mathbf{Y}$ chromosome). Males are heterogametic.
  3. Fertilisation Possibilities:
    • If an ovum ($X$) is fertilised by an $X$-bearing sperm: Zygote is $\mathbf{XX} \longrightarrow$ Female child (Girl).
    • If an ovum ($X$) is fertilised by a $Y$-bearing sperm: Zygote is $\mathbf{XY} \longrightarrow$ Male child (Boy).

3. Scientific Conclusion:

The probability of having a male or a female baby is strictly $50\% : 50\%$ ($1 : 1$). Because all eggs carry only an $X$ chromosome, it is the father's sperm ($X$ or $Y$) that determines the biological sex of the child. Holding the mother responsible for the birth of a girl child is completely scientifically baseless and unjust.

Case Study Question • Blood Group Inheritance
A man with blood group A marries a woman with blood group O and their daughter has blood group O. (i) Is this information enough to tell you which of the traits — blood group A or O — is dominant? (ii) What are the possible genotypes of the father and the daughter? (iii) If they have a second child, what is the probability that the child has blood group A?

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. (i) Sufficiency of Information: No, this single observation alone is not strictly sufficient to determine dominance without standard genetic theory. However, knowing that Blood group O is a recessive trait ($i$), the daughter can only have blood group O if she is homozygous recessive ($ii$), receiving one $i$ allele from the mother (who is $ii$) and one $i$ allele from the father. This implies that Blood group A is dominant ($I^A$) and Blood group O is recessive ($i$).
  2. (ii) Genotypes:
    • Daughter (Blood group O): $\mathbf{ii}$
    • Father (Blood group A): Heterozygous $\mathbf{I^A i}$
    • Mother (Blood group O): $\mathbf{ii}$
  3. (iii) Probability of Second Child having Blood Group A:
    Cross: $I^A i \times ii \longrightarrow 50\% \, I^A i \text{ (Blood group A)}$ and $50\% \, ii \text{ (Blood group O)}$.
    The probability is $50\%$ (or $1/2$).
