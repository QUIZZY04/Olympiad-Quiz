In-Text Page 81 • Q1
Why is diffusion insufficient to meet the oxygen requirements of multicellular organisms like humans?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Scientific Explanation:

  1. In unicellular organisms (like Amoeba), the entire surface area of the organism is in direct contact with the external environment; hence simple diffusion across the cell membrane is fast and sufficient to supply oxygen and remove waste.
  2. In large, complex multicellular organisms like humans, the body volume is massive and most tissue cells are located deep inside the body, far away from the surrounding atmosphere.
  3. Diffusion is a very slow physical transport process ($t \propto x^2$). Calculations show that if diffusion alone were to transport oxygen in human beings, it would take an estimated 3 years for a single molecule of oxygen to diffuse from our lungs to our toes.
  4. Therefore, multicellular organisms require a specialized respiratory surface (alveoli) coupled with a rapid, pressure-driven circulatory system containing respiratory pigments (haemoglobin) to deliver oxygen efficiently to all body cells.

What criteria do we use to decide whether something is alive?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Criteria for Living Organisms:

  1. Invisible Molecular Movements: The most fundamental and definitive criterion is the occurrence of molecular movements within cells and subcellular structures. Living organisms are highly organized structures made of tissues, cells, and organelles that constantly repair and maintain their order against entropic breakdown.
  2. Vital Life Processes: Living beings actively carry out essential life processes including nutrition (metabolic energy uptake), respiration (cellular energy release), transportation of nutrients/gases, excretion of toxic wastes, and response to external stimuli (sensitivity).
  3. Visible movements (such as locomotion, breathing movements, or plant phototropism) are common, but invisible maintenance molecular movements are the ultimate criterion.

What are outside raw materials used for by an organism?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Outside Raw Materials:

Since life on Earth depends on carbon-based molecules, the primary outside raw materials consumed by organisms are:

  1. Carbon-based Nutrients (Food): Heterotrophs consume organic matter (carbohydrates, proteins, fats) for energy and structural building blocks. Autotrophs absorb inorganic carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$).
  2. Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$): Required as a universal biochemical solvent for enzymatic cellular reactions, transport of metabolites, and photolysis in photosynthesis.
  3. Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$): Absorbed by aerobic organisms for cellular respiration to break down glucose into ATP.
  4. Minerals (Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Iron, Magnesium): Absorbed by plants from soil to synthesize amino acids, proteins, DNA, and chlorophyll.

Essential Life Processes:

Where do plants get each of the raw materials required for photosynthesis?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Sources of Photosynthetic Raw Materials:

  1. Carbon Dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$): Absorbed directly from the ambient atmosphere through tiny microscopic pores called stomata present on the epidermal surface of leaves by gaseous diffusion.
  2. Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$): Absorbed along with dissolved minerals from the rhizosphere soil by root hairs via osmosis, and transported upward to leaves through xylem vessels.

  4. Minerals (Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Iron, Magnesium): Absorbed as dissolved inorganic nitrates/nitrites and phosphates from the soil through roots.

Functions of Gastric Hydrochloric Acid ($\text{HCl}$):

  1. Activation of Pepsinogen: The gastric glands in the stomach wall secrete inactive proenzyme pepsinogen.   3. Food Softening: It softens tough fibrous components in food, facilitating mechanical churning and enzymatic digestion.

Role of Digestive Enzymes:

Digestive enzymes act as biological catalysts that hydrolyse complex, large, insoluble food macromolecules into small, simple, diffusible, and water-soluble micro-molecules that can be absorbed across the intestinal epithelium into the blood stream:

How is the small intestine designed to absorb digested food?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Structural Adaptations of Small Intestine for Absorption:

  1. Enormous Surface Area (Villi and Microvilli): The inner mucosal lining of the ileum is folded into millions of tiny, microscopic finger-like projections called villi. Each epithelial cell of the villi bears countless sub-microscopic folds called microvilli (brush border). This increases the absorptive surface area of the gut by more than $300\text{ m}^2$.
  2. Dense Capillary Network: Each villus is richly supplied with a dense mesh of thin-walled blood capillaries, allowing monosaccharides and amino acids to diffuse rapidly into the venous circulation.
  3. Lacteal for Lipid Absorption: The center of each villus contains a specialized lymphatic capillary called a lacteal, which specifically absorbs digested fats (fatty acids and glycerol packaged as chylomicrons) into the lymphatic system.
  4. Thin Epithelial Barrier: The lining consists of a single layer of columnar epithelial cells, minimizing the diffusion distance for rapid transport.

In-Text Page 91 • Q1
What advantage over an aquatic organism does a terrestrial organism have with regard to obtaining oxygen for respiration?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Respiratory Advantage of Terrestrial Organisms:

  1. Oxygen Concentration: Terrestrial organisms take in oxygen from atmospheric air, which contains an abundant and rich concentration of oxygen (approximately $21\%$ or $210\text{ mL/L}$). In contrast, aquatic organisms obtain oxygen dissolved in water, where oxygen concentration is extremely low (less than $8\text{ mg/L}$ or $\approx 0.7\%$).
  2. Rate of Breathing: Because the amount of dissolved oxygen in aquatic habitats is so low, aquatic organisms (like fish) must pump large volumes of water over their gills, resulting in a very rapid breathing rate.
  3. Terrestrial organisms do not have to expend high metabolic energy on high breathing rates, and their internal respiratory organs (lungs) are well protected against desiccation.

In-Text Page 91 • Q2
What are the different ways in which glucose is oxidised to provide energy in various organisms?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Biochemical Pathways of Glucose Breakdown:

In all living organisms, the initial common metabolic step is Glycolysis occurring in the cytoplasm, where one 6-carbon molecule of glucose is cleaved into two 3-carbon molecules of pyruvate, releasing a small amount of ATP.

Pyruvate is subsequently broken down via three distinct metabolic pathways:

  1. Path 1: Absence of Oxygen (Anaerobic Respiration / Fermentation in Yeast):
    Occurs in cytoplasm of yeast cells; pyruvate is converted into ethanol, carbon dioxide, and 2 ATP: $$\text{Pyruvate} \xrightarrow{\text{Absence of } \text{O}_2} \text{Ethanol (2C)} + \text{CO}_2 + \text{Energy (2 ATP)}$$
  2. Path 2: Lack of Oxygen (Anaerobic pathway in Human Skeletal Muscle Cells during vigorous exercise):
    When oxygen demand outstrips supply, pyruvate is converted into lactic acid and 2 ATP: $$\text{Pyruvate} \xrightarrow{\text{Lack of } \text{O}_2} \text{Lactic acid (3C)} + \text{Energy (Cramps)}$$ Accumulation of lactic acid leads to muscular fatigue and painful cramps.
  3. Path 3: Presence of Oxygen (Aerobic Respiration in Mitochondria):
    Pyruvate undergoes complete oxidative decarboxylation inside the mitochondria into carbon dioxide, water, and 38 ATP: $$\text{Pyruvate} + \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Mitochondria}} 6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{Energy (38 ATP)}$$

In-Text Page 91 • Q3
How is oxygen and carbon dioxide transported in human beings?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Gas Transport Mechanisms in Blood:

  1. 1. Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$) Transport:
    • Oxygen has very low solubility in water/plasma. Therefore, about $97 - 98.5\%$ of oxygen is transported bound to Haemoglobin, a specialized iron-containing respiratory pigment found inside Red Blood Cells (RBCs).
    • Each haemoglobin molecule reversibly binds four oxygen molecules in lung capillaries to form oxyhaemoglobin: $$\text{Hb} + 4\text{O}_2 \rightleftharpoons \text{Hb(O}_2)_4$$
    • In tissues with low partial pressure of oxygen ($p\text{O}_2$), oxyhaemoglobin dissociates, releasing oxygen to the respiring cells. The remaining $1.5\%$ is carried dissolved in plasma.
  2. 2. Carbon Dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$) Transport:
    • Carbon dioxide is about 20 to 25 times more soluble in water than oxygen.
    • About $70\%$ is transported as dissolved Bicarbonate ions ($\text{HCO}_3^-$) in blood plasma.
    • About $20 - 23\%$ binds with the amino groups of haemoglobin to form carbamino-haemoglobin.
    • About $7\%$ travels directly dissolved as physical solute in blood plasma.

How are the lungs designed in human beings to maximise the area for exchange of gases?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Structural Design of Human Lungs:

  1. Extensive Branching Network: Within the lungs, the primary bronchus subdivides repeatedly into smaller and smaller airways (secondary bronchi, tertiary bronchi, and terminal bronchioles) like the branches of an inverted tree.
  2. Balloon-like Alveoli: The terminal bronchioles end in microscopic, cup-shaped balloon-like air sacs called alveoli. There are approximately 300 to 500 million alveoli in both adult human lungs.
  3. Huge Surface Area: If flattened out, the collective surface area provided by these alveoli spans approximately $80\text{ m}^2$ (equivalent to the floor area of a tennis court).
  4. Ultra-thin Membrane & Dense Capillaries: The alveolar walls are just one-cell-thick and wrapped in an ultra-dense network of pulmonary capillaries, enabling instantaneous diffusion of $\text{O}_2$ and $\text{CO}_2$.

What are the components of the transport system in human beings? What are the functions of these components?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Components and Functions of Human Circulatory System:

  1. 1. The Heart (Muscular Pump): A four-chambered myogenic muscular organ that pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs for oxygenation and forcefully pumps oxygenated blood to all systemic body organs.
  2. 2. Blood (Fluid Connective Tissue):
    • Plasma: Fluid matrix transporting dissolved food nutrients, carbon dioxide, nitrogenous wastes (urea), and hormones.
    • RBCs (Erythrocytes): Contain haemoglobin to transport oxygen from lungs to tissues.
    • WBCs (Leukocytes): Produce antibodies and engulf pathogens, providing immune defense.
    • Platelets (Thrombocytes): Initiate blood clotting at injury sites to prevent haemorrhage.
  3. 3. Blood Vessels (Closed Conduit Network):
    • Arteries: Thick, elastic-walled vessels carrying oxygenated blood away from the heart under high pressure.
    • Veins: Thin-walled vessels with internal valves carrying deoxygenated blood back towards the heart.
    • Capillaries: Microscopic one-cell-thick vessels where exchange of gases and nutrients occurs.
  4. 4. Lymphatic System: Drains excess extracellular tissue fluid back into the venous bloodstream and transports absorbed fats.

Why is it necessary to separate oxygenated and deoxygenated blood in mammals and birds?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Biological Significance of Blood Separation:

  1. High Energy Demand (Homeothermy): Mammals and birds are warm-blooded (endothermic/homeothermic) animals. They must constantly maintain a constant, elevated internal body temperature regardless of ambient environmental fluctuations.
  2. Preventing Mixing for Maximum Efficiency: Complete anatomical separation of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood by a four-chambered heart prevents any mixing. This ensures that purely oxygen-saturated blood is delivered to metabolically active tissues.
  3. This enables highly efficient cellular respiration and ATP generation needed to fuel thermal homeostasis and vigorous physical activity.

In-Text Page 96 • Q4
How are water and minerals transported in plants?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Mechanism of Ascent of Sap in Xylem:

  1. Absorption at Roots (Root Pressure): Root hair cells actively absorb mineral ions from soil water using metabolic energy. This creates an osmotic concentration gradient between root cells and soil, causing water to flow continuously into the root xylem, generating a modest hydrostatic root pressure (significant during night).
  2. Transpiration Pull (Primary Driving Force):
    • During daytime, water continuously evaporates as water vapour from the mesophyll cells of leaves through open stomatal pores—a process known as transpiration.
    • This loss creates a massive negative hydrostatic pressure (suction tension) in the leaf xylem.
    • Because of strong cohesion (mutual attraction between water molecules) and adhesion (attraction between water molecules and lignified xylem walls), a continuous, unbroken water column extends from roots to leaf tips.
    • The suction tension pulls the water column upwards through tracheids and vessels against gravity, reaching heights exceeding 100 metres in tall trees.

How is food transported in plants?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Translocation in Phloem:

  1. Active Loading: Soluble products of photosynthesis, primarily sucrose, are actively loaded from leaf mesophyll cells into the sieve tubes of phloem using metabolic energy derived from ATP.
  2. Osmotic Pressure Generation: The accumulation of sucrose raises the osmotic pressure inside the sieve tube, causing water to enter from adjacent xylem vessels by osmosis.
  3. Bulk Flow to Sink: The resulting high hydrostatic pressure drives the phloem sap towards regions of lower osmotic pressure (such as developing roots, fruits, seeds, and buds). There, sucrose is actively unloaded and utilized or stored as starch.

In-Text Page 100 • Q1
Describe the structure and functioning of nephrons.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Nephron: The Structural and Functional Excretory Unit of Kidney

  1. Structure of a Nephron: Each human kidney contains about 1 million nephrons. A nephron consists of:
    • Malpighian Body: Composed of a double-walled cup called Bowman's capsule enclosing a tuft of capillaries called the glomerulus.
    • Renal Tubule: A long coiled tube differentiated into Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT), U-shaped Henle's loop, and Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT), opening into a common collecting duct.
  2. Functioning (Three Step Process of Urine Formation):
    • (a) Glomerular Ultrafiltration: Under high hydrostatic blood pressure in the glomerulus, liquid plasma containing glucose, amino acids, salts, urea, and excess water is filtered across the glomerular membrane into Bowman's capsule as primary filtrate ($\approx 180\text{ litres/day}$). Blood cells and proteins are retained.
    • (b) Selective Tubular Reabsorption: As the filtrate flows along the PCT and Henle's loop, all essential nutrients (100% glucose, amino acids, and major ions) and most water ($\approx 99\%$) are selectively reabsorbed back into the peritubular capillaries.
    • (c) Tubular Secretion: Excess potassium, hydrogen ions, and metabolic wastes are actively secreted from capillaries into the distal tubule to maintain electrolyte balance. The remaining fluid ($1 - 1.5\text{ L/day}$) is concentrated urine.

What are the methods used by plants to get rid of excretory products?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Excretory Mechanisms in Plants:

  1. Gaseous Wastes: Surplus oxygen produced during photosynthesis and excess carbon dioxide produced during respiration are expelled by simple diffusion through stomata in leaves and lenticels in stems.
  2. Excess Water: Removed continuously into the atmosphere as water vapour via transpiration through stomata.
  3. Storage in Shedding Organs: Many metabolic wastes are stored in cellular vacuoles of older leaves, bark, and dead xylem tissues that periodically wither, turn yellow, and fall off.
  4. Resins and Gums: Waste products are stored as insoluble resins and gums, especially in old, non-functional xylem.
  5. Excretion into Soil: Some excretory substances are secreted directly into the soil surrounding their roots.

In-Text Page 100 • Q3
How is the amount of urine produced regulated?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Regulation of Urine Volume:

The total volume of urine produced daily depends on two primary physiological factors:

  1. Volume of Excess Water in the Body: If a person drinks plenty of fluids, the body retains less water and the kidney reabsorbs less water, producing a large volume of dilute urine. If dehydrated (or on a hot summer day with heavy sweating), kidneys reabsorb maximum water, producing a small volume of concentrated urine.
  2. Amount of Dissolved Nitrogenous Wastes: The more dissolved waste products (urea, salts) to be excreted, the more water is needed to dissolve and eliminate them safely without osmotic injury.
  3. Hormonal Control (ADH): Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus stimulate the posterior pituitary to secrete Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH / Vasopressin), which increases water permeability of DCT and collecting ducts for reabsorption when dehydrated.

NCERT Exercise • Q5
How are fats digested in our bodies? Where does this process take place?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Digestion of Fats:

  1. Site of Digestion: Digestion of fats takes place predominantly in the small intestine (duodenum and ileum).
  2. Step 1: Emulsification by Bile Salts: Fats enter the duodenum as large, insoluble lipid globules that cannot be easily accessed by water-soluble enzymes. The liver secretes bile juice containing bile salts (sodium glycocholate and sodium taurocholate). Bile salts break down large fat globules into minute droplets, increasing the surface area for enzyme action. This is called emulsification.
  3. Step 2: Hydrolysis by Pancreatic and Intestinal Lipase: The pancreas secretes lipase in pancreatic juice, and intestinal glands secrete intestinal lipase. Lipase hydrolyses emulsified fat molecules into absorbable fatty acids and glycerol: $$\text{Emulsified Fats} \xrightarrow{\text{Lipase}} \text{Fatty acids} + \text{Glycerol}$$
  4. These products are absorbed into the central lacteals of the intestinal villi.

[2 Marks]

Functions of Saliva in Digestion:

  1. Chemical Digestion of Starch: Saliva contains the digestive enzyme salivary amylase (ptyalin). It breaks down complex insoluble starch molecules into simpler disaccharide sugars (maltose) at an optimum pH of $\approx 6.8$: $$\text{Starch} \xrightarrow{\text{Salivary amylase}} \text{Maltose (Sugar)}$$

  3. Oral Hygiene: Saliva contains lysozyme, an antibacterial enzyme that destroys oral bacteria.

Conditions and By-products of Autotrophic Nutrition:

  1. Necessary Conditions:

NCERT Exercise • Q9
How are the alveoli designed to maximise the exchange of gases?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Structural Adaptations of Alveoli:

  1. Gigantic Surface Area: Hundreds of millions of tiny balloon-like alveoli in both lungs provide a combined surface area of roughly $80\text{ m}^2$, maximizing the interface for gaseous diffusion.
  2. Extremely Thin Diffusion Barrier: The alveolar wall is composed of a single layer of squamous epithelial cells, and the pulmonary capillary is made of a single endothelial layer. The total diffusion thickness is less than $0.5\ \mu\text{m}$.
  3. Extensive Capillary Network: The outer surface of each alveolus is wrapped in an intricate web of fine blood capillaries, ensuring high blood flow and steep partial pressure gradients for rapid $\text{O}_2$ absorption and $\text{CO}_2$ release.
  4. Moist Inner Lining: Alveolar surfaces are coated with a thin moisture film, allowing oxygen gas to dissolve before diffusing across cell membranes.

NCERT Exercise • Q10
What would be the consequences of a deficiency of haemoglobin in our bodies?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Consequences of Haemoglobin Deficiency (Anaemia):

Haemoglobin is the iron-containing pigment in red blood cells that transports oxygen to body tissues. A deficiency of haemoglobin leads to a condition called Anaemia, with the following physiological effects:

  1. Reduced Oxygen Carrying Capacity: The oxygen-carrying capacity of blood drops drastically. Tissue cells receive inadequate oxygen supplies.
  2. Impaired Cellular Respiration: Due to oxygen deprivation, the rate of aerobic cellular oxidation slows down, resulting in diminished ATP production.
  3. Fatigue and Breathlessness: The person experiences chronic exhaustion, physical weakness, pale skin, dizziness, and shortness of breath (hyperventilation as the body tries to compensate for oxygen deficits).

NCERT Exercise • Q11
Describe double circulation of blood in human beings. Why is it necessary?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Double Circulation in Humans:

In human beings, blood travels through the heart twice during each complete circuit of the body. This consists of two separate circulatory circuits:

  1. 1. Pulmonary Circulation:
    • Deoxygenated blood from body tissues enters the Right Atrium → passes into the Right Ventricle.
    • The Right Ventricle pumps it through the pulmonary artery to the lungs.
    • In the lungs, blood discharges $\text{CO}_2$ and picks up $\text{O}_2$.
    • Oxygenated blood returns to the Left Atrium via the pulmonary veins.
  2. 2. Systemic Circulation:
    • Oxygenated blood flows from Left Atrium → into the thick-walled Left Ventricle.
    • The Left Ventricle pumps it under high pressure into the aorta, distributing it to all systemic organs and body tissues.
    • In tissues, oxygen is delivered and metabolic $\text{CO}_2$ is picked up.
    • Deoxygenated blood returns via the vena cava to the Right Atrium.

Why Double Circulation is Necessary:

It completely separates oxygen-rich blood from deoxygenated blood, ensuring that systemic organs receive pure oxygenated blood under high pressure. This sustains the high metabolic rate and continuous heat production required by warm-blooded mammals.

Case Study Question • Haemodialysis (Artificial Kidney)
In cases of chronic renal failure, patients are kept alive using an artificial kidney (haemodialysis machine). (i) What is the function of the cellophane tubes in the dialyser? (ii) What is the composition of the dialysing solution compared to normal blood plasma? (iii) How does haemodialysis differ fundamentally from a healthy living kidney in terms of tubular reabsorption?

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. (i) Function of Cellophane Tubes: Cellophane tubes are made of a selectively semipermeable membrane that allows small solute molecules like urea, uric acid, and excess mineral salts to diffuse through while retaining large blood cells and plasma proteins inside the blood circuit.
  2. (ii) Composition of Dialysing Fluid: The dialysing fluid has the exact same osmotic pressure and concentration of essential electrolytes and glucose as normal blood plasma, but it contains zero nitrogenous wastes (zero urea). This steep concentration gradient ensures that urea and uric acid diffuse passively out of the patient's blood into the dialysing fluid.
  3. (iii) Difference from Natural Kidney: In a natural kidney, approximately $180\text{ litres}$ of primary filtrate is filtered daily and $99\%$ of water and glucose is selectively reabsorbed along the renal tubules. In haemodialysis, there is no tubular reabsorption; only waste diffusion takes place across cellophane tubes into the bath.
