Question 1
What is the importance of DNA copying in reproduction? Why is variation beneficial to the species but not necessarily for the individual?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Biological Significance of DNA Copying in Reproduction:

  1. 1. Transmission of Genetic Information:
    The chromosomes in the nucleus of a cell contain genetic information for the inheritance of features from parents to next generation in the form of DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid). DNA copying ensures that both daughter cells receive an exact set of genetic blueprints necessary for structural body design.
  2. 2. Generation of Organic Cellular Apparatus:
    DNA copying is accompanied by the creation of an additional cellular apparatus (cytoplasm, organelles) so that when the cell divides, each daughter cell has functional machinery to survive independently.
  3. 3. Subtle Inbuilt Variations for Species Survival:
    No biochemical copying mechanism is $100\%$ completely error-free. DNA replication introduces subtle nucleotide variations (mutations). While extreme variations can be lethal, subtle variations provide the raw material for evolution and adaptation, enabling populations of a species to withstand sudden environmental catastrophes (such as global warming, drought, or epidemics).

Feature Binary Fission Multiple Fission

Question 3
Explain the process of sexual reproduction in flowering plants from pollination to seed formation. What are post-fertilisation changes?
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Sexual Reproduction and Seed Formation in Angiosperms (Flowering Plants):

1. Reproductive Parts of a Flower:

  • Stamen (Male reproductive organ): Consists of anther (produces yellow haploid pollen grains containing male gametes) and filament.
  • Carpel / Pistil (Female reproductive organ): Consists of sticky stigma (receives pollen), slender style, and swollen basal ovary (contains ovules, each housing an egg cell).

2. Pollination:

The transfer of pollen grains from anther to stigma is called pollination:

  • Self-pollination: Transfer occurs within the same flower or between flowers of the same plant.
  • Cross-pollination: Transfer occurs between flowers of different plants of the same species, facilitated by abiotic agents (wind, water) or biotic vectors (insects, birds, bats).

3. Fertilisation and Pollen Tube Growth:

  1. Upon landing on a compatible receptive stigma, the pollen grain germinates and develops a long pollen tube.
  2. The pollen tube grows through the tissues of the style and reaches the ovule in the ovary via the micropyle.
  3. Two male gametes are discharged into the embryo sac:
    • One male gamete fuses with the female egg cell to form a diploid Zygote (Syngamy).
    • The second male gamete fuses with the two polar nuclei to form the triploid Endosperm (Triple fusion). This unique event is known as Double Fertilisation.

4. Post-Fertilisation Changes:

  • The zygote divides repeatedly by mitosis to develop into an Embryo.
  • The ovule develops a tough protective integument coat and converts into a Seed.
  • The ovary grows rapidly, ripens, and transforms into the Fruit. Petals, sepals, and stamens shrivel and fall off.

Question 4
What happens when the egg is not fertilised in a human female? Explain the phenomenon of menstruation.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Mechanism of Menstruation in Human Females:

  1. In human females, one of the two ovaries releases one mature ovum (egg cell) every month (ovulation), roughly midway through a 28-day menstrual cycle.
  2. In anticipation of receiving a fertilised egg (zygote), the uterine wall (endometrium) thickens, becomes richly vascularized, and spongy with blood vessels to nourish the prospective embryo.
  3. If Fertilisation does NOT occur:
    The unfertilised egg survives for about 24 to 48 hours and then degenerates. As progesterone hormone levels plunge, the thickened, spongy endometrium is no longer required.
  4. The lining of the uterus slowly breaks down and sheds. Its shredded tissues along with blood and mucus are discharged through the vagina as menstrual flow (Menstruation), which typically lasts for about 3 to 5 days.

Question 5
What are the different methods of contraception? State one advantage of barrier methods over other methods.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Board Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Categories of Contraceptive Methods:

Contraception refers to the deliberate prevention of unwanted pregnancy. Major methods include:

  1. 1. Mechanical Barrier Methods:
    • Examples: Condoms (for males) and diaphragms/cervical caps (for females).
    • Mechanism: Physically prevent sperm from reaching the female genital tract.
    • Dual Advantage: Condoms provide crucial protection against Sexually Transmitted Diseases (STDs) such as HIV-AIDS, Syphilis, and Gonorrhoea.
  2. 2. Chemical Methods (Oral Hormonal Pills):
    • Examples: Oral contraceptive pills (contain synthetic estrogen/progesterone).
    • Mechanism: Alter the female hormonal balance to inhibit ovulation so that no egg is released.
    • Side effects: May cause hormonal imbalance, nausea, or mood fluctuations.
  3. 3. Intrauterine Contraceptive Devices (IUCDs):
    • Example: Copper-T placed inside the uterus by a trained medical professional.
    • Mechanism: Releases copper ions that suppress sperm motility and fertilising capacity, preventing implantation.
  4. 4. Surgical Methods (Permanent Sterilisation):
    • Vasectomy (in males): A small portion of the vas deferens is cut and tied to prevent sperm delivery.
    • Tubectomy (in females): A small portion of the fallopian tubes is cut and ligated to block egg passage.

Case Study Question • Male Reproductive System
The testes in human males are located outside the abdominal cavity within a pouch of skin called the scrotum. Furthermore, secretions of the prostate gland and seminal vesicles merge with sperm. (i) Why are testes located outside the abdominal cavity in the scrotum? (ii) What is the dual function of the male urethra? (iii) What is the role of seminal vesicle and prostate gland secretions?

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. (i) Role of Scrotum: Spermatogenesis (production of viable sperm) requires a temperature that is $2^\circ\text{C} - 2.5^\circ\text{C}$ lower than the internal human body core temperature ($37^\circ\text{C}$). The scrotum hangs outside the warm abdomen to maintain this cooler temperature.
  2. (ii) Dual Function of Urethra: In human males, the urethra serves as a common passage for both the discharge of urine from the urinary bladder and the ejaculation of semen containing sperm during reproduction.
  3. (iii) Role of Accessory Gland Secretions: The seminal vesicles and prostate gland add their alkaline secretions to sperm:
    • Provide a fluid medium for easy sperm motility and transport.
    • Provide nourishment (fructose, enzymes).
    • Neutralize the acidic environment of the urethra and vagina.
