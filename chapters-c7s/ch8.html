Chapter 8: Reproduction in Plants

NCERT Exercise Solutions — Complete Step-by-Step Answers with CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

📌 Key Scientific Concepts & Plant Reproduction Modes
  • Modes of Plant Reproduction:
    • Asexual Reproduction: Multiplication without involving the fusion of gametes or production of seeds. Offspring are genetically identical clones of the single parent. Major types include:
      • Vegetative Propagation: New plants arise from vegetative units like roots (sweet potato, dahlia), stems (runners, rhizomes in ginger/turmeric, tubers with "eyes" in potato, stem cuttings in rose), and leaves (notches of leaves in Bryophyllum).
      • Budding: Formation of a small daughter bulb-like projection (bud) that detaches and matures (e.g., Yeast).
      • Fragmentation: Filamentous organisms break into two or more fragments when mature, each growing into a new individual (e.g., Spirogyra alga).
      • Spore Formation: Production of asexual microscopic spores protected by a tough, thick wall to withstand unfavourable conditions (heat, low humidity) which germinate under moisture (e.g., Bread mould / Rhizopus, ferns, mosses).
    • Sexual Reproduction: Involves fusion of male and female gametes produced by the reproductive organ of angiosperms — the flower.
  • Anatomy of a Flower:
    • Bisexual Flowers: Contain both stamens (male) and pistil (female) — e.g., mustard, rose, petunia.
    • Unisexual Flowers: Possess either male stamens (staminate) or female pistil (pistillate) — e.g., corn, papaya, cucumber.
    • Stamen (Male organ): Comprises a slender filament and a terminal two-lobed anther containing pollen grains that produce male gametes.
    • Pistil / Carpel (Female organ): Comprises the receptive stigma, elongated style, and a basal swollen ovary containing one or more ovules enclosing female gametes (egg cells).
  • Pollination & Fertilisation:
    • Pollination: Transfer of pollen grains from anther to stigma. Can be Self-pollination (same flower or another flower on same plant) or Cross-pollination (flower of another plant of same species via wind, water, insects).
    • Fertilisation: Fusion of male gamete (travelling down a pollen tube) with the female egg cell inside the ovule to produce a diploid zygote. The zygote develops into an embryo; ovule matures into a seed, and the ovary ripens into a fruit.
  • Seed Dispersal Mechanisms: Prevents overcrowding, competition for sunlight, water, and minerals. Dispersed by: Wind (winged seeds of drumstick, maple; hairy seeds of aak, dandelion), Water (spongy, fibrous coats in coconut, lotus), Animals (hooked/spiny seeds of Xanthium, Urena), and Explosion/dehiscence (balsam, castor).
NCERT Intext Questions & Scientific Enquiries
Page 95 — Intext Q1 (Boojho's Query)
Boojho wants to know how a piece of potato tuber with an 'eye' develops into a new potato plant.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Vegetative Propagation via Potato Tuber:
1. A potato tuber is a swollen underground storage stem. The indentations called 'eyes' are vegetative buds located at stem nodes, protected by scar leaves.
2. When a piece of potato containing an eye is buried in moist, warm soil, the dormant bud absorbs water and nutrients from the starch-rich tuber flesh.
3. The eye sprouts into an aerial shoot growing leaves upwards towards sunlight, while adventitious roots develop downwards into the soil, establishing a vigorous, genetically identical new potato plant.

Page 99 — Intext Q2 (Paheli's Query)
Paheli asks: Why do flowers of insect-pollinated plants have bright colorful petals, sweet scent, and nectar, whereas wind-pollinated flowers are small, dull, and lack fragrance?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Ecological Floral Adaptations:
1. Insect-pollinated flowers: Must actively attract animal pollinators (bees, butterflies). Bright colorful petals act as visual beacons; sweet fragrance guides insects from afar; and sugary nectar serves as a food reward. The pollen is sticky so it adheres to the insect's hairy legs.
2. Wind-pollinated flowers: Wind is an inanimate physical carrier, so energy spent on petals, nectar, and scents would be wasted. Instead, these flowers have exposed hanging stamens, large feathery stigmas to trap drifting pollen, and produce huge quantities of dry, ultra-lightweight pollen that easily floats on gentle breezes.

Q2
Describe the different methods of asexual reproduction in plants. Give examples.
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Asexual reproduction is a mode of reproduction in which new individuals are produced from a single parent without the formation and fusion of gametes or seeds. The primary methods are:

1. Vegetative Propagation: New plants arise from vegetative organs (roots, stems, leaves, buds).
  • Stem Tuber: Potato tubers bear "eyes" (buds) covered by scar leaves. Planting an eye piece with a portion of potato gives rise to a new potato plant.
  • Stem Cutting: In rose and money plant, a piece of branch cut at a node and planted in soil develops roots and leaves.
  • Leaf Buds: In Bryophyllum, margin notches produce plantlets with adventitious roots that detach and establish independently.
  • Root Buds: Sweet potato and Dahlia sprout into new plants from their swollen tuberous roots.
2. Budding: A tiny bulb-like cellular outgrowth called a bud gradually grows on the parent cell, receives a replicated nucleus, and pinches off to form a new daughter cell (which may form chains before detaching).
Example: Unicellular fungus Yeast.
3. Fragmentation: When nutrient and water conditions are favourable, filamentous green algae break into two or more small pieces (fragments). Each fragment rapidly grows through cell division into a complete mature filament.
Example: Spirogyra.
4. Spore Formation: Special asexual reproductive bodies called spores are enclosed inside a hard protective coat (sporangium) to endure extreme drying and heat. When conditions become warm and humid, the sporangium bursts, liberating microscopic spores that germinate into new mycelia.
Example: Bread mould (Rhizopus), mosses, and ferns.

Q3
Explain what you understand by sexual reproduction.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Definition: Sexual reproduction is the biological process of creating new offspring by the union of two specialized sex cells (gametes) — a male gamete and a female gamete — typically originating from two parents (or from both male and female organs of a bisexual organism).

Key Features in Flowering Plants:
  • The flower is the reproductive organ of angiosperms.
  • The stamen (male reproductive organ) produces pollen grains containing male gametes.
  • The pistil / carpel (female reproductive organ) bears ovules containing female gametes (egg cells).
  • Pollination transfers pollen to the stigma, leading to fertilisation (syngamy) to form a single diploid cell called a zygote.
  • The zygote develops into an embryo contained within a protective seed, which germinates under favourable moisture and warmth to yield a genetically unique plant.

Q5
Sketch the reproductive parts of a flower.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

The flower contains two essential reproductive whorls: the Stamen (androecium) and the Pistil / Carpel (gynoecium).

1. Stamen (Male Reproductive Organ):
  • Anther: The terminal, bilobed sac that produces and stores microscopic pollen grains (which house the male gametes).
  • Filament: The slender, elongated stalk that supports and positions the anther for optimal pollen dispersal.
2. Pistil / Carpel (Female Reproductive Organ):
  • Stigma: The sticky or feathery top surface designed to capture and anchor landed pollen grains.
  • Style: The narrow, elongated neck connecting the stigma to the ovary, through which the pollen tube grows.
  • Ovary: The basal, swollen chamber containing one or more egg-containing ovules. Upon fertilisation, the ovary ripens into the fruit.
  • Ovule: The female reproductive structure inside the ovary containing the female gamete (egg cell), which transforms into the seed.

Q7
How does the process of fertilisation take place in flowers?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Fertilisation is the process in which the male gamete fuses with the female gamete to form a single diploid cell called a zygote. In flowering plants, it occurs in the following sequential stages:

Step 1: Pollen Germination: After landing on the compatible receptive surface of the stigma, the pollen grain absorbs moisture and sugary nutrient secretions, germinating to produce a narrow, thin pollen tube.
Step 2: Growth down the Style: The pollen tube grows downwards through the tissues of the style towards the ovary, carrying two male gametes in its tip.
Step 3: Entry into the Ovule: The pollen tube enters the ovary and penetrates an ovule through a microscopic aperture called the micropyle.
Step 4: Syngamy / Fusion: The tip of the pollen tube bursts open, releasing the male gamete, which fuses directly with the female egg cell situated within the ovule. This union results in the formation of a zygote.
Post-Fertilisation Changes:
  • The zygote divides repeatedly to develop into an embryo.
  • The ovule develops a tough seed coat and matures into a seed.
  • The ovary enlarges, ripens, and transforms into a succulent or dry fruit.
  • Petals, sepals, and stamens wither and fall off.

Q8
Describe the various ways by which seeds are dispersed.
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Seed dispersal is the natural scattering or transport of seeds away from parent plants. It is ecologically vital because it avoids overcrowding, minimizes intense competition for water, sunlight, and soil minerals, and enables species to invade new geographical habitats. The four major mechanisms are:

1. Dispersal by Wind: Seeds are lightweight, small, and equipped with special aerodynamic structures:
  • Winged seeds: Drumstick (Moringa) and Maple seeds have thin papery wings that catch wind currents and glide over long distances.
  • Hairy seeds and tufts: Aak (Calotropis) and Dandelion have parachutes or tufts of fine silk-like hairs; sunflower fruits possess a hairy pappus that floats on breezes.
  • Microscopic spores: Extremely light spores of ferns and mosses blow away effortlessly.
2. Dispersal by Water: Seeds or fruits develop light, fibrous, or spongy outer coats that trap air, enabling them to float on surface water streams and ocean currents.
Examples: Coconut has a buoyant fibrous mesocarp; Lotus has a spongy fruit that floats down rivers.
3. Dispersal by Animals: Seeds possess physical clinging adaptations or sweet fleshy pulp:
  • Hooked / Spiny seeds: Xanthium and Urena have hooked spines that latch tenaciously onto the fur of grazing animals or human clothing, falling off miles away.
  • Edible fruits: Birds and mammals consume juicy fruits (figs, guava, berries); the indigestible hard seeds pass unharmed through the digestive tract and are deposited far away in nutrient-rich droppings.
4. Dispersal by Explosive Mechanism (Dehiscence): Upon ripening and drying under solar heat, extreme mechanical tension builds up inside the fruit wall, causing the pericarp to suddenly burst open with explosive force, catapulting seeds several metres away from the parent plant.
Examples: Balsam, Castor, and Pea pods.

🎯 Competency-Based Questions (CBQ) & HOTS CBSE Board Exam Pattern
Case Study: Commercial Horticulture & Vegetative Propagation
Horticultural Application: An agricultural scientist advises a farmer growing seedless Thompson grapes and high-yielding Alphonso mangoes to exclusively practice vegetative propagation (cuttings and grafting) rather than growing plants from seeds.

(i) Why cannot seedless grapes be propagated through sexual reproduction?
(ii) State two major commercial advantages vegetative propagation offers over growing from seeds.
(iii) What is the fundamental disadvantage of monoculture clones produced through vegetative propagation?

(i) Reason for Seedless Grapes: Seedless grape varieties do not produce viable seeds containing embryos; hence sexual reproduction through seed germination is biologically impossible. They can only be multiplied through stem cuttings or grafting.

(ii) Commercial Advantages:
1. Speed and Earlier Maturity: Vegetatively propagated plants bear flowers and fruits much earlier than seedlings grown from seeds.
2. Exact Genetic Quality: Offspring are exact clones, ensuring 100% preservation of desirable traits (taste, fruit size, disease resistance, sweetness) without genetic variation.

(iii) Biological Disadvantage: Because all plants in the orchard share identical genetic makeup, there is zero genetic diversity. If a new disease or pest strikes, every single plant will be equally susceptible, risking total crop wipeout.

⚡ Assertion & Reason (HOTS)
Assertion (A): Nature has evolved intricate mechanisms to favor cross-pollination over self-pollination in most flowering plants.
Reason (R): Cross-pollination brings about new combinations of genes from two distinct parent plants, creating healthier offspring with higher adaptability to changing environments.

Choose the correct option:
(a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
(b) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
(c) A is true, but R is false.
(d) A is false, but R is true.

Answer: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Scientific Explanation: Continuous self-pollination leads to inbreeding depression, accumulation of harmful recessive alleles, and reduced vigour. Cross-pollination introduces novel genetic variations, enhances environmental fitness, and produces vigorous, disease-resistant seeds.
