📌 Key Scientific Concepts & Digestive System Summary
  • Five Major Steps of Holozoic Nutrition:
    1. Ingestion: Taking food into the body via the mouth (buccal cavity).
    2. Digestion: Breakdown of complex, insoluble food molecules into simple, soluble forms by digestive enzymes.
    3. Absorption: Movement of digested nutrients through intestinal walls (villi) into the bloodstream.
    4. Assimilation: Utilization of absorbed nutrients by body cells for energy, growth, and tissue repair.
    5. Egestion: Removal of undigested, unabsorbed waste residue from the body through the anus.
  • Human Digestive Canal & Associated Glands:
    • Mouth & Teeth: Incisors (cutting/biting), Canines (tearing), Premolars & Molars (chewing/grinding). Salivary amylase breaks starch into maltose.
    • Stomach: Secretes mucous (protects inner wall), hydrochloric acid (kills bacteria, creates acidic medium pH ~1.5–2), and pepsin (digests proteins into peptides).
    • Liver: Largest gland in human body; secretes bile stored in gall bladder; emulsifies fats (breaks large fat globules into tiny droplets).
    • Pancreas: Secretes pancreatic juice containing amylase, trypsin, and lipase.
    • Small Intestine: Longest part (~7.5 m); site of complete digestion and nutrient absorption through finger-like villi.
    • Large Intestine: ~1.5 m; absorbs water and essential salts from undigested residue.
  • Nutrition in Ruminants: Four-chambered stomach (Rumen, Reticulum, Omasum, Abomasum). Swallowed food is stored in rumen as cud, brought back to mouth and chewed (rumination). Symbiotic bacteria in caecum digest complex cellulose.
  • Nutrition in Amoeba: Unicellular organism that pushes out finger-like projections called pseudopodia (false feet) to engulf food into a food vacuole where digestive enzymes break it down.

Q5
What are villi? What is their location and function?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Definition: Villi (singular: villus) are millions of microscopic, finger-like projections protruding from the mucous membrane of the digestive tract.
Location: Villi are located on the inner mucosal lining of the small intestine (especially in the jejunum and ileum).
Functions:
  • Increasing Surface Area: The millions of villi vastly increase the effective absorptive surface area of the small intestine by hundreds of times.
  • Rapid Nutrient Absorption: Each villus contains a dense network of thin, fine blood capillaries and a central lymph vessel (lacteal). Simple digested nutrients (glucose, amino acids, minerals, vitamins) readily diffuse through the thin single-cell epithelium into the blood stream for transportation to all body cells.

Q6
Where is bile produced? Which component of the food does it help to digest?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Site of Production & Storage: Bile juice is produced continuously by the Liver. It is temporarily stored and concentrated in a sac-like organ called the Gall Bladder.
Component of Food Digested: Bile plays a vital role in the digestion of Fats (Lipids).
Mechanism (Emulsification): Bile does not contain enzymes, but its bile salts break down large, hydrophobic fat globules into microscopic droplets (a process called emulsification). This enormously increases the surface area for pancreatic lipase enzymes to chemically break fats into fatty acids and glycerol. Furthermore, bile provides the necessary alkaline medium for pancreatic enzymes to function.

Q7
Name the type of carbohydrate that can be digested by ruminants but not by humans. Give the reason also.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Carbohydrate Name: Cellulose (the tough structural polysaccharide constituting plant cell walls and grass).
Scientific Reason:
  • In Ruminants (Cattle, Deer, Buffaloes): Ruminants possess a specialised large sac-like chamber called the caecum / rumen between the small and large intestine. Inside this chamber live specialised symbiotic anaerobic bacteria and protozoa that secrete the enzyme cellulase, which hydrolyses the tough β-glycosidic bonds of cellulose.
  • In Humans: The human digestive system completely lacks the enzyme cellulase as well as the symbiotic bacteria required to break down cellulose. Therefore, cellulose passes through the human alimentary canal undigested, functioning only as dietary fibre (roughage) to aid bowel movement.

Q8
Why do we get instant energy from glucose?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
1. No Digestion Required: Glucose is a simple monosaccharide (the simplest form of carbohydrate). Unlike complex carbohydrates (starch, sucrose) which require hours of enzymatic digestion in the mouth, stomach, and intestine to be broken down, glucose requires zero digestion.
2. Direct & Immediate Absorption: When consumed, glucose is instantly absorbed directly across the intestinal wall into the bloodstream and delivered straight to body cells.
3. Rapid Cellular Respiration: Inside cells, glucose is readily oxidised by oxygen during cellular respiration to immediately release energy in the form of ATP:
Glucose + Oxygen ⎯⎯→ Carbon dioxide + Water + Energy (ATP)

Q10
State one similarity and one difference between the nutrition in amoeba and human beings.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Similarity: Both Amoeba and humans exhibit holozoic nutrition consisting of the same fundamental sequence of stages: ingestion, digestion (where complex substances are broken down into simpler soluble forms by digestive enzymes), absorption, assimilation for energy, and egestion of undigested waste.
Difference:
  • Amoeba: Being a single-celled microscopic organism, it has no mouth or specialised digestive canal; food is captured by pseudopodia and digested inside a temporary food vacuole.
  • Humans: Being complex multicellular organisms, they possess a specialised, highly developed alimentary canal with distinct organs (mouth, stomach, intestine) and specialised digestive glands (salivary glands, liver, pancreas).

Q12
Label the following parts of the human digestive system in sequence: Stomach, Gall bladder, Liver, Pancreas, Small intestine, Large intestine, Rectum, Anus.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

The anatomical sequence of organs forming the human digestive system from mouth to anus is:

1. Mouth / Buccal Cavity: Teeth masticate food; salivary glands add saliva.
2. Oesophagus (Foodpipe): Connects pharynx to stomach through peristaltic waves.
3. Stomach: J-shaped muscular sac on the upper left abdomen that releases HCl, mucous, and pepsin.
4. Liver: Large reddish-brown gland on upper right side; produces bile.
5. Gall Bladder: Pear-shaped sac nestled beneath liver; stores and concentrates bile.
6. Pancreas: Cream-coloured leaf-shaped gland located just below stomach; secretes pancreatic enzymes.
7. Small Intestine: Highly coiled 7.5-metre central organ; completes chemical digestion and absorbs nutrients via villi.
8. Large Intestine: Wider 1.5-metre frame surrounding the small intestine; absorbs water and salts.
9. Rectum: Terminal pouch storing compacted faeces.
10. Anus: Muscular sphincter opening for egestion.

Q13
Can we survive only on raw, leafy vegetables/grass? Discuss.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

No, human beings cannot survive solely on raw, leafy vegetables and grass.

1. Inability to Digest Cellulose: Grass and raw leafy plants primarily contain cellulose, a tough structural carbohydrate. The human digestive tract completely lacks the enzyme cellulase and does not house the symbiotic cellulose-fermenting micro-organisms found in ruminants (cows, goats). Consequently, cellulose passes out undigested, providing virtually zero energy to humans.
2. Severe Energy & Nutrient Deficit: Raw vegetables contain very high proportions of water and fibre, but extremely low concentrations of accessible proteins, essential fats, and complex carbohydrates needed to sustain human metabolic rates and body organ functions.
3. Nutritional Role as Roughage: While leafy vegetables are vital in a balanced diet to provide essential vitamins, minerals, and roughage (fibre) to maintain healthy bowel movement and prevent constipation, they must be supplemented with energy-dense foods (grains, pulses, dairy, oils) to sustain life.

🎯 Competency-Based Questions (CBQ) & HOTS CBSE Board Exam Pattern
Case Study: Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT)
Clinical Case: 10-year-old Aarav contracted an intestinal infection after eating unhygienic street food. He experienced frequent watery stools and vomiting, feeling extremely weak, dizzy, and parched. The doctor diagnosed him with acute diarrhoea, warned against severe dehydration, and immediately prescribed frequent sips of ORS (Oral Rehydration Solution).

(i) What is dehydration, and why is it dangerous?
(ii) How can an emergency ORS solution be prepared at home?
(iii) Why is simple plain water not as effective as ORS during severe diarrhoea?

(i) Dehydration: Dehydration is the excessive loss of water and vital dissolved mineral salts (electrolytes such as sodium, potassium, and chloride) from body tissues. It is life-threatening because blood volume drops, leading to hypovolemic shock, organ failure, and collapse.

(ii) Home Preparation of ORS: Boil 1 litre of clean drinking water and let it cool. Dissolve 6 level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt thoroughly in the water.

(iii) Mechanism: The intestinal lining absorbs sodium and water through co-transport coupled with glucose molecules. Plain water cannot be efficiently absorbed by an inflamed, rapidly purging bowel and gets excreted. The presence of glucose in ORS triggers rapid intestinal sodium and water reabsorption back into the circulatory system.

⚡ Assertion & Reason (HOTS)
Assertion (A): A patient whose gall bladder has been surgically removed is advised to avoid heavy, fatty meals.
Reason (R): The gall bladder produces bile juice, and without it, no bile is synthesised in the body.

Choose the correct option:
(a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
(b) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
(c) A is true, but R is false.
(d) A is false, but R is true.

Answer: (c) A is true, but R is false.

Scientific Explanation: Assertion (A) is true because without the gall bladder, there is no reservoir to store and release concentrated pulses of bile when a fatty meal enters the duodenum. However, Reason (R) is completely false: bile is synthesised by the liver, not the gall bladder. Following a cholecystectomy, the liver continues to produce bile, which drips continuously in dilute amounts directly into the intestine, making large boluses of fat difficult to digest.
