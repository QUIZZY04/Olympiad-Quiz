Chapter 1: Water — The Essence of Life

Exploring water states, the global water cycle, groundwater aquifers, traditional stepwells, and conservation

Chapter Highlights, Learning Objectives & Core Concepts
  • States of Water: Exists naturally in three interconnected physical states—solid (ice/snow), liquid (water), and gas (water vapour).
  • The Water Cycle: Solar heat powers continuous evaporation from water bodies and transpiration from plants, followed by atmospheric condensation into clouds and precipitation as rain or snow.
  • Groundwater & Surface Water: Surface water fills lakes, ponds, and rivers; rainwater seeps through permeable soil layers into underground aquifers (water table) accessed via wells and borewells.
  • Traditional Indian Water Architecture: Ancient engineering structures like Bawris (stepwells in Rajasthan/Gujarat), Johads (earthen check dams), Kunds, and Tankas preserved precious desert rainwater for centuries.
  • Water Scarcity & Pollution: Untreated sewage, industrial discharge, and unchecked chemical runoff severely deplete freshwater sources, causing seasonal droughts.
  • Rainwater Harvesting (RWH): Capturing rooftop rainwater and filtering it directly into recharging pits or underground cisterns ensures sustainable water security.

Q1 What are the three physical states of water found on Earth, and how does water transition between them in nature? Conceptual Understanding 3 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
Water exists in three distinct states:
  1. Liquid State: Common water found in rivers, oceans, lakes, and rain.
  2. Solid State: Ice, snow, and glaciers formed when water cools below 0°C.
  3. Gaseous State: Invisible water vapour produced when water evaporates due to heat.
Natural Transition: The Sun heats liquid surface water, causing it to evaporate into water vapour (gas). As water vapour rises into the cold upper atmosphere, it cools and condenses into liquid droplets to form clouds. When these droplets grow heavy, they fall back to Earth as precipitation (rain or snow), completing the natural hydrological cycle.

Q2 How does groundwater differ from surface water? Explain how rainwater seeps down to form underground aquifers. Scientific Explanation 3 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
Difference:
  • Surface Water: Water visible on Earth's surface in ponds, streams, rivers, and reservoirs, readily exposed to sunlight and direct atmospheric pollution.
  • Groundwater: Water located beneath Earth's surface inside pores of soil, gravel, and rock formations known as aquifers.
Process of Seepage (Infiltration & Percolation): When rain falls on open ground, soil, and forest floors, gravity pulls the water downward through porous layers of sand and pebbles. As it travels down, the soil layers naturally filter out debris until the water meets an impermeable rock bed, accumulating as clean groundwater that feeds wells and natural springs.

Q3 What are 'Bawris' (stepwells)? Explain their historical, social, and architectural importance in arid regions like Rajasthan and Gujarat. Cultural & Environmental Heritage 4 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
A Bawri is a traditional Indian stepwell—a multi-storeyed, intricately carved underground reservoir with flight of stone steps descending deep into the water level.

Key Importance:
  1. Rainwater Harvesting in Arid Climates: In desert regions receiving sparse, erratic rainfall, Bawris collected monsoon runoff, ensuring year-round drinking and cooking water.
  2. Cool Community Gathering Spaces: Deep subterranean stone pavilions provided a naturally cool refuge from searing desert heat where travelers, merchants, and villagers rested and socialized.
  3. Architectural Masterpieces: Examples like Rani ki Vav in Gujarat and Chand Baori in Abhaneri (Rajasthan) feature stunning geometry, stone pillars, and cooling subterranean galleries.

Q4 Hands-on Activity: Describe an experiment using a glass tumbler, warm water, and ice cubes to demonstrate condensation. Hands-on Experiment & Observation 4 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
Experiment: Simulating Cloud Formation and Condensation
  • Materials Needed: A transparent glass tumbler, warm water (about half-full), a small metal lid or saucer, and 3–4 ice cubes.
  • Procedure:
    1. Pour warm water into the transparent glass tumbler until it is half full.
    2. Cover the mouth of the tumbler immediately with the metal saucer.
    3. Place several ice cubes on top of the metal saucer.
    4. Observe the underside of the saucer and the upper walls of the glass tumbler for 2–3 minutes.
  • Observation: Warm water evaporates, producing invisible water vapour that rises inside the tumbler. When this warm vapour touches the cold metal plate chilled by ice cubes, it loses thermal energy, and tiny drops of liquid water condense on the underside of the saucer, eventually dripping down like raindrops.
  • Scientific Conclusion: Condensation occurs whenever warm water vapour cools down and transforms back into liquid droplets.

🌟 NEP 2020 Competency-Based Case Study (CBQ)

Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting for Climate-Resilient Schools

Real-World Case Scenario: A government primary school in a rural district experiences severe groundwater depletion by March each year, leaving the school handpump dry and forcing students to carry water from distant wells.

Analytical Task: Design a simple, cost-effective Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting (RRWH) model that the school Eco-Club can implement. Outline the primary components and explain how this will secure drinking water and recharge local aquifers.

Proposed School Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting Framework:
  1. Catchment Area (Rooftop): The clean sloping roof of the school building serves as the catchment surface where fresh monsoon rain falls. Regular cleaning of the roof before monsoons prevents leaf and dust contamination.
  2. Conveyance Pipes (Gutters & Downspouts): PVC gutters fitted along the roof edges channel collected rainwater into downpipes.
  3. First-Flush Device: A simple valve drains away the first 15–20 minutes of monsoon rain, washing away atmospheric dust and bird droppings.
  4. Multi-layered Filtration Pit: Water then passes through a natural filter chamber containing layers of coarse gravel, fine sand, and charcoal, which remove suspended particles and impurities.
  5. Storage Tank & Percolation Well: The filtered water is stored in an underground masonry tank for washing and toilets, while the overflow is directed into a soak-pit to recharge the school's dried groundwater aquifer.
Impact: This ensures 4–5 months of water independence for the school and raises the surrounding water table.
CBSE Assessment Rubric: 2 Marks for identifying the 5 key structural components (catchment, gutters, first-flush, filter, storage) + 3 Marks for operational explanation and ecological aquifer recharge impact = 5 Marks

Chapter 2: Journey of a River

Following river courses from snowy glacial sources down fertile plains to ocean deltas, studying ecosystems and river conservation

Chapter Highlights, Learning Objectives & Core Concepts
  • River Course Stages: A river journeys across three distinct stages—Upper Course (steep mountain origin, rapid flow, V-shaped valleys, waterfalls), Middle Course (gentle gradient, meanders, wide valleys, human settlements), and Lower Course (slow flow, sediment deposition, delta formation, meeting the sea).
  • Perennial vs Seasonal Rivers: Perennial rivers (Ganga, Yamuna, Brahmaputra) are fed year-round by melting Himalayan glaciers and monsoons; seasonal rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Narmada) rely primarily on monsoon rainfall.
  • River Ecosystems: Rivers provide freshwater habitats for fish, freshwater turtles, aquatic plants, and endangered species like the Gangetic Dolphin (*Platanista gangetica*).
  • Historical & Agricultural Importance: River floodplains deposit nutrient-rich alluvial silt, creating India's most fertile agricultural belts that supported ancient civilizations.
  • River Pollution Drivers: Municipal untreated sewage, industrial chemical effluents, washing clothes with synthetic detergents, and plastic trash threaten river health.
  • Conservation Programs: Initiatives like the Namami Gange Project, constructing Sewage Treatment Plants (STPs), and community riverbank tree planting (riparian buffers) help restore river cleanliness.

Q1 Trace the three distinct stages of a river from its mountain source to the sea. How does the river's speed and shape change? Physical Geography 4 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
A river journeys through three distinct geographical stages:
  1. The Upper Course (Mountain Stage): The river originates high in mountains from melting glaciers or rain-fed springs. The terrain is steep, causing the river to flow rapidly with high erosive power, cutting steep V-shaped valleys and cascading down waterfalls.
  2. The Middle Course (Valley & Plains Stage): As the river enters flat plains, the land gradient decreases. The river widens, slows down, and swings from side to side in wide loops called meanders. Tributaries join it, increasing water volume.
  3. The Lower Course (Delta & Mouth Stage): Near its journey's end, the river flows very sluggishly. It deposits heavy sediments (silt and sand), splitting into multiple smaller channels (distributaries) to form a triangular delta before merging into the sea or ocean.

Q2 What is the difference between a tributary and a distributary? Give one Indian example of each. Geographical Terminology 3 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
Definitions and Differences:
  • Tributary: A smaller stream or river that flows into and joins a larger parent river, increasing its total volume.
    Example: River Yamuna is the largest tributary of River Ganga (joining at Prayagraj).
  • Distributary: A channel that branches off from the main river near its mouth in a delta and carries water away toward the sea without rejoining the main stream.
    Example: River Hooghly in West Bengal is a major distributary branching off from River Ganga.

Q6 What is the Gangetic River Dolphin, and why is its presence considered an indicator of a healthy river ecosystem? Biodiversity & Ecology 3 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
The Gangetic River Dolphin (Platanista gangetica) is India's National Aquatic Animal, living exclusively in freshwater river stretches of the Ganga and Brahmaputra basins.

Significance as an Ecosystem Indicator:
  1. These dolphins are almost completely blind and navigate and hunt fish using echolocation (sonar clicks).
  2. They can survive only in deep, clean, unpolluted freshwater with abundant fish and high dissolved oxygen.
  3. Therefore, their presence in a river stretch proves that the aquatic food chain is healthy and chemical pollution is low. When river water becomes polluted or silty, dolphins migrate away or die out.

Community Action Plan for Rejuvenating Polluted Urban Riverfronts

Chapter 3: The Mystery of Food

Understanding food spoilage, microbial growth, traditional and modern preservation methods, and nutritional balance

Chapter Highlights, Learning Objectives & Core Concepts
  • Food Spoilage vs Food Wastage: Food spoilage occurs when chemical and biological changes render food unsafe for consumption; food wastage occurs when wholesome, edible food is discarded unconsumed.
  • Causes of Food Spoilage: Growth of microorganisms (bacteria, yeast, moulds/fungi), presence of enzymes, excessive moisture, and warm ambient temperature.
  • Recognizing Spoiled Food: Visible cottony or green mould growth, sour or bitter taste, foul putrid smell, slimy surface texture, and gas bubbles in liquids.
  • Traditional Preservation Methods: Drying/dehydration (removing moisture), salting and pickling (creating hypertonic conditions inhibiting bacterial growth), sweetening (jams and murabba), and boiling/cooking.
  • Modern Food Preservation: Pasteurization (rapid heating followed by quick chilling in milk), refrigeration (slowing microbial activity at low temperatures), deep freezing, and vacuum packaging.
  • Reading Food Labels: Checking Manufacturing Date, Best Before / Expiry Date, Ingredients, FSSAI quality seal, and Vegetarian/Non-vegetarian indicators (green/brown dots).

Q1 What causes food to spoil, and what are four visible or sensory signs indicating that cooked food is no longer safe to eat? Food Science & Health 4 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
Primary Causes of Food Spoilage: Food spoils due to the rapid growth of microorganisms like bacteria, yeasts, and moulds (fungi). These microbes thrive in the presence of moisture, warm ambient temperature, and oxygen, breaking down food proteins and carbohydrates while producing toxins.

Four Sensory Signs of Spoiled Food:
  1. Foul Odor: A sour, rancid, or putrid smell indicating bacterial decomposition.
  2. Surface Appearance: Visible white, green, or black furry patches (mould spores) developing on breads, fruits, or rotis.
  3. Altered Texture: Slimy or sticky film covering cooked curries, dals, or boiled vegetables.
  4. Taste Change: A distinctly bitter, fermented, or acidic sour taste (even before visible mould appears).

Q2 Explain four scientific methods commonly used in Indian households to preserve food for several months without refrigeration. Traditional Preservation Techniques 4 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
Four traditional Indian food preservation techniques include:
  1. Sun-Drying (Dehydration): Removing moisture content so microorganisms cannot survive or reproduce.
    Examples: Papad, dried red chillies, raw mango slices (amchur), and methi leaves.
  2. Salting & Pickling (Addition of Oil and Salt): High salt concentration draws out moisture from bacterial cells through osmosis, killing them. Mustard oil forms an airtight seal preventing fungal spores from landing on food.
    Examples: Mango pickle (achar), lime pickle, and salted fish.
  3. Sweetening (High Sugar Concentration): Thick sugar syrups bind water molecules, preventing microbes from absorbing moisture needed to grow.
    Examples: Amla murabba, apple jam, and gulkand.
  4. Boiling (Heat Inactivation): High temperatures kill vegetative bacterial cells and inactivate enzymes.
    Example: Boiling fresh milk twice a day to prevent curdling.

Q3 Hands-on Activity: Describe the bread mould experiment. What changes are observed over a 5-day observation period? Scientific Investigation 4 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
Bread Mould Growth Investigation:
  • Procedure: Take a fresh slice of bread, sprinkle a few drops of clean water on it (do not make it soggy), place it inside an airtight transparent plastic box or zip-lock pouch, and leave it undisturbed in a warm, dark corner of the kitchen for 5 days.
  • Daily Observations:
    • Day 1–2: No visible changes; bread feels slightly moist.
    • Day 3: Faint whitish, cotton-like fuzzy patches appear on the bread surface.
    • Day 4: The patches turn pale green or greyish-black with a distinct stale, musty smell.
    • Day 5: Dense dark greenish-black velvety colonies (sporangia of *Rhizopus* mould) spread across the slice. The bread softens and rots.
  • Inference: Fungal spores present in the air landed on the bread and germinated rapidly because of the moisture, warmth, and nutrient-rich starch.

Q5 What is 'Pasteurization', and how was this method discovered by Louis Pasteur to keep milk fresh? Scientific Discovery & Technology 3 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
Pasteurization is a food preservation process where liquid food (especially milk) is heated to a specific temperature for a set time and then chilled rapidly to destroy harmful disease-causing microorganisms without altering the food's taste or nutritional value.

How it Works:
  1. Fresh milk is heated to approximately 70°C for 15 to 30 seconds.
  2. It is then suddenly chilled to below 10°C and packaged immediately into sterile pouches.
  3. The sudden temperature shock destroys pathogenic bacteria like *Salmonella* and *Mycobacterium*, allowing packaged pasteurized milk to be consumed safely even without immediate boiling.
📋 CBSE Official Step Marking Rubric (3 Marks):

Q6 Differentiate between 'food spoilage' and 'food wastage'. Suggest three practical ways families can eliminate food wastage. Ethics & Environmental Awareness 3 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
Difference:
  • Food Spoilage: Natural decay caused by bacteria and fungi that makes food unsafe or hazardous to consume.
  • Food Wastage: Edible, perfectly good food that is discarded due to over-purchasing, excess cooking, or careless disposal.
Three Ways to Eliminate Food Wastage:
  1. Smart Serving Sizes: Take only as much food on your plate as you can finish; take second helpings only if hungry.
  2. Plan Meals & Grocery Lists: Check refrigerator contents before buying fresh groceries to avoid buying duplicate perishables.
  3. Repurpose Leftovers: Store leftover rotis, rice, and vegetables safely in the refrigerator and convert them into nutritious breakfasts (e.g. vegetable fried rice or stuffed parathas).

🌟 NEP 2020 Competency-Based Case Study (CBQ)

Community Food Bank & Wedding Leftover Management

Real-World Case Scenario: In a neighborhood banquet hall, large quantities of cooked rice, paneer curries, and rotis were routinely thrown into municipal garbage dumpsters after wedding banquets, while unhoused families in nearby streets lacked nutritious meals.

Analytical Task: Develop an ethical, health-compliant protocol for partnering with community food rescue organizations to collect, test, and distribute surplus banquet food safely within 3 hours.

Community Food Rescue & Quality Protocol:
  1. Immediate Quality & Temperature Verification: Trained student volunteers and NGO workers check that surplus food has been kept in hot holding vessels (above 60°C) and shows zero signs of souring or odor.
  2. Hygienic Packaging: Food is transferred into clean, food-grade stainless steel containers or compostable boxes labeled with the preparation time and date.
  3. Rapid Cold-Chain or Thermal Transport: Utilizing insulated transport bags, the surplus food is dispatched within a strict 2-hour transit window to nearby shelters, night shelters, and orphanages.
  4. Safe Consumption Window: Food recipients are advised to consume the meals within 60 minutes of delivery, preventing bacterial multiplication.
Long-term Benefit: Prevents thousands of kilograms of greenhouse methane from decomposing in landfills while addressing urban nutritional hunger.

Chapter 4: Our School — A Happy Place

Fostering inclusive school communities, Green School practices, waste segregation, water harvesting, and student safety

Chapter Highlights, Learning Objectives & Core Concepts
  • Inclusive Learning Spaces: A happy school welcomes every student regardless of physical ability, gender, mother tongue, or socioeconomic background; features barrier-free architecture (ramps, wide doors, tactile tactile tiles).
  • Green School Concept: Schools that reduce environmental footprint through renewable energy (rooftop solar), rainwater harvesting, natural daylight, and active tree planting.

  • Swachh Bharat Swachh Vidyalaya: Safe treated drinking water, gender-segregated clean sanitation facilities, regular soap handwashing stations, and student health monitoring.
  • Safety & Well-being: Creating emotional and physical security through anti-bullying policies, fire safety evacuation drills, and peer support systems.

Q2 Explain how the Khasi and Jaintia tribes of Meghalaya create 'Living Root Bridges'. Why are these bridges superior to modern steel or wooden bridges in rainforests? Indigenous Bio-Engineering 4 Marks

  1. The Khasi and Jaintia tribes guide the flexible, aerial secondary roots of the Ficus elastica (Indian rubber fig tree) across mountain river chasms using hollowed betel nut tree trunks.

Chapter 7: Energy — How Things Work

Understanding energy forms, renewable vs fossil sources, everyday mechanical transformations, and sustainable conservation

Chapter Highlights, Learning Objectives & Core Concepts
  • Definition of Energy: Energy is the capacity or ability to do work; it exists in many forms including solar, thermal, mechanical, chemical, electrical, and kinetic energy.
  • The Sun as Primary Energy Source: Sunlight powers plant photosynthesis, drives wind circulation, and evaporates water in the hydrological cycle, making the Sun the ultimate driver of Earth's energy.
  • Renewable Energy: Inexhaustible natural sources that regenerate quickly and do not emit greenhouse gases—Solar (photovoltaic panels), Wind (turbines), Hydroelectric (flowing water), and Biogas.
  • Non-Renewable Fossil Fuels: Coal, petroleum (petrol, diesel), and natural gas formed over millions of years from buried prehistoric organisms; exhaustible and causes air pollution and global warming.
  • Energy Transformations in Everyday Machines: Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed (e.g. Electric iron: electrical to thermal; Fan: electrical to kinetic; Battery: chemical to electrical).
  • Simple Machines: Force multipliers and directional tools that make mechanical work easier—inclined plane, lever, pulley, screw, wheel and axle, and wedge.

Q5 Map Skill Task: On a world map or globe, identify and define: (a) The Equator, (b) The Northern and Southern Hemispheres, and (c) The Prime Meridian. Map & Globe Interpretation 3 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
Key Planetary Lines of Reference:
  1. The Equator (0° Latitude): An imaginary horizontal circle drawn around the exact middle of the Earth, equidistant from the North and South Poles. It divides the Earth into the Northern Hemisphere and Southern Hemisphere.
  2. Northern & Southern Hemispheres:
    • Northern Hemisphere: The half of Earth lying north of the Equator (contains India, Asia, Europe, and North America).
    • Southern Hemisphere: The half of Earth lying south of the Equator (contains Australia, Antarctica, and major parts of South America and Africa).
  3. The Prime Meridian (0° Longitude): An imaginary vertical line running from the North Pole to the South Pole passing through Greenwich, England. It serves as the baseline for global standard time zones.

Q6 What does the ancient Indian philosophical concept 'Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam' teach humanity about environmental stewardship? Ethics & Global Citizenship 3 Marks
Step-by-Step Model Answer & Explanation:
Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam (वसुधैव कुटुम्बकम्): Sourced from the ancient *Maha Upanishad*, this Sanskrit verse translates to 'The World is One Family'.

Lessons for Environmental Stewardship:
  1. Universal Kinship Beyond Borders: It teaches that all human beings, animals, birds, forests, and oceans are inextricably linked members of a single planetary family sharing one common home.
  2. Living in Harmony with Nature: Instead of exploiting nature as commodities, humanity must act as humble caretakers (stewards), ensuring that development does not poison the air, water, or soil that other species rely on.
  3. Collective Global Responsibility: Global challenges like climate change, ocean acidification, and pandemics cannot be solved by one country alone; all nations must collaborate as one family to protect planet Earth.
