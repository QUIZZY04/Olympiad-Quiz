Poem 2: Not Marble nor the Gilded Monuments (Sonnet 55)

William Shakespeare — Immortality of Art, Poetry vs The Ravages of Time — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

Not marble, nor the gilded monuments Of princes, shall outlive this powerful rhyme; But you shall shine more bright in these contents Than unswept stone, besmear’d with sluttish time. When wasteful war shall statues overturn, And broils root out the work of masonry, Nor Mars his sword nor war’s quick fire shall burn The living record of your memory. ‘Gainst death and all-oblivious enmity Shall you pace forth; your praise shall still find room Even in the eyes of all posterity That wear this world out to the ending doom.
— William Shakespeare
I. Literature Reader Textbook Solutions & Analysis — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27
Q1
What will outlast marble statues and gilded monuments of princes according to Shakespeare?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Shakespeare asserts that neither polished marble statues nor the gold-gilded monuments erected by royalty to perpetuate their glory will outlive his 'powerful rhyme' (his poetry). While stone and gold decay, gather dust, and crumble under the ravages of time, his verse will shine with timeless, eternal brilliance.

Q2
Why does the poet describe time as 'sluttish'? What happens to physical masonry during wars?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. 'Sluttish Time': The poet personifies time as 'sluttish' (untidy, careless, dirty, and disrespectful) because time indiscriminately soils, weathers, tarnishes, and covers magnificent stone monuments with filth and dust, treating regal memorials with total disrespect.

2. Devastation of War: Destructive wars overturn royal statues, and violent civil broils demolish masonry works, reducing majestic stone buildings to rubble.

Q3
Why can neither Mars' sword nor war's fire burn the living record of the poet's friend?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Mars is the Roman god of war. While sword and fire can annihilate solid physical structures made of stone and iron, they are completely powerless against poetry and memory. Because verse resides in the realm of human intellect, soul, and shared language, it is indestructible and immune to physical weapons.

Q4
How does Shakespeare immortalize his beloved friend in Sonnet 55? Discuss the theme of art triumphing over death and time.
6 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Futility of Material Memorials:
Monarchs and tyrants invest fortunes in erecting massive pyramids, marble tombs, and gilded effigies to keep their names alive. Shakespeare reveals that all physical monuments are doomed to oblivion by 'sluttish time' and devastating wars.

2. Immortality of Poetic Verse:
In contrast to perishable stone, the written word possesses immortal life. Shakespeare's sonnet acts as a living temple for his friend. Death and all-oblivious enmity cannot erase him; the friend will march forward through future generations ('all posterity') until the final Day of Judgment.

3. The Ultimate Victory of Art:
The concluding couplet summarizes this Renaissance humanist triumph: 'So, till the judgment that yourself arise, / You live in this, and dwell in lovers' eyes.' Art conquers mortality by continuously resurrecting beauty in every reader's mind.

Poetic Structure & Classical Allusion
Analysis: Explain the rhyme scheme and structure of Sonnet 55. What is the significance of referencing 'Mars' and 'ending doom'?

Sonnet 55 follows the traditional Shakespearean sonnet form: 14 lines in iambic pentameter with the rhyme scheme **abab cdcd efef gg** (three quatrains and a rhyming couplet). Referencing **Mars** (Roman war god) highlights the ferocity of military conflict, while **'ending doom'** (biblical Day of Judgment) establishes that the friend's fame will outlive the entire span of human history itself.
