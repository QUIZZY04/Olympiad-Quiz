Poem 5: Snake

D.H. Lawrence — Instinctive Wonder, Conditioned Fear & The Guilt of Human Cruelty — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

A snake came to my water-trough On a hot, hot day, and I in pyjamas for the heat, To drink there. In the deep, strange-scented shade of the great dark carob-tree I came down the steps with my pitcher And must wait, must stand and wait, for there he was at the trough before me. He reached down from a fissure in the earth-wall in the gloom And trailed his yellow-brown slackness soft-bellied down, over the edge of the stone trough And rested his throat upon the stone bottom, And where the water had dripped from the tap, in a small clearness, He sipped with his straight mouth, Softly drank through his straight gums, into his slack long body, Silently.
— D.H. Lawrence

Q1
Why did the poet go to the water trough? Describe the atmosphere and his attire.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The poet went to his garden water-trough with a pitcher to fetch drinking water. It was a fiercely hot July day in Taormina, Sicily, with Mount Etna smoking in the distance. The heat was so oppressive that the poet was dressed only in his night pyjamas.

Q3
What did the 'voice of education' command the poet to do? What inner conflict ensued?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The 'voice of human education' whispered that in Sicily, black snakes are harmless while gold snakes are venomous, urging: 'He must be killed, take a stick and finish him off.' This sparked a fierce inner struggle: the poet's natural instinct admired the creature as a royal, honored guest, while social training branded his hesitation as cowardice and demanded violence.

Q4
Why did the poet throw a clumsy log at the snake? How did he feel immediately afterwards?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

As the snake turned and put its head back into the dark fissure to depart, a sudden wave of petty human revulsion seized the poet. Picking up a clumsy log, he hurled it at the water trough. Though the log missed the snake's body, the snake convulsed with haste and vanished into the wall. Immediately, the poet was overcome with bitter shame, despising himself, his vulgar action, and the pettiness of human education.

Q5
How does D.H. Lawrence's 'Snake' explore the conflict between primal ecological wonder and civilized human prejudice? Why does the poet compare the snake to an 'uncrowned king'?
8 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Primal Wonder vs Social Conditioning:
Lawrence's poem is an indictment of modern human civilization. Born with an innate capacity to love nature, humans are systematically corrupted by an 'education' of fear, conquest, and violence. The poet instinctively regards the snake as an innocent fellow creature who arrived at the trough first, deserving courtesy as a guest.

2. The Guilt of Petty Violence:
The poet's aggressive act of throwing a log was not self-defense, but an insecure exhibition of false bravado demanded by societal conditioning. The moment the violence was done, he felt tainted by spiritual vulgarity.

3. The Snake as an 'Uncrowned King':
The poet compares the creature to a lord of the underworld—an exiled, magnificent king uncrowned in the dark subterranean depths. Comparing his petty act to the Ancient Mariner's shooting of the Albatross, the poet acknowledges that he missed a sacred chance to commune with a god of the earth, leaving him with an enduring spiritual pettiness to expiate.

Philosophical & Psychological Reflection
Analysis: Why does the poet refer to his act of throwing the log as a 'petty' crime? What does this word signify?

The word 'petty' signifies smallness of mind, cowardice, and vulgarity. Throwing a log at a peaceful, non-threatening creature whose back was turned did not require bravery; it was an act of cheap, cowardly spite. The poet recognized that human beings often commit unprovoked cruelty simply because their fragile egos cannot tolerate peaceful coexistence with the wild.
