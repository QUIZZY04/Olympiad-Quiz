📌 Key Biological Concepts & Plant Morphology
  • Plant Classification by Stature:
    • Herbs: Small plants with green, soft, and tender stems; usually have few branches and a short lifespan (e.g., Tomato, Mint, Coriander, Wheat).
    • Shrubs: Medium-sized plants with a hard, woody stem branching out near the ground level; neither very tall nor tender (e.g., Rose, Lemon, China rose, Jasmine).
    • Trees: Tall plants with thick, hard, woody main trunks (bark); branches arise higher up from the trunk (e.g., Mango, Banyan, Neem, Peepal).
    • Creepers: Plants with thin, fragile stems that cannot stand upright and spread along the ground (e.g., Pumpkin, Watermelon, Sweet potato).
    • Climbers: Plants with weak stems that take the support of neighbouring structures and climb upward using tendrils (e.g., Money plant, Pea plant, Grapevine).
  • Leaf Venation & Correlation with Roots:
    • Reticulate Venation: Veins form a net-like or webbed design across the leaf lamina. Plants with reticulate venation always possess a Taproot system (e.g., Gram, Mustard, Mango, Rose).
    • Parallel Venation: Veins run parallel to each other from base to apex. Plants with parallel venation always possess a Fibrous Root system (e.g., Grass, Maize, Wheat, Banana).
  • Parts of a Typical Flower:
    • Sepals: Outermost green, leaf-like protective layer shielding the flower in bud stage.
    • Petals: Brightly coloured, fragrant parts that attract pollinators like bees and butterflies.
    • Stamens (Male Reproductive Organ): Consists of the slender stalk (filament) and pollen-producing sac (anther).
    • Pistil / Carpel (Female Reproductive Organ): Innermost whorl consisting of the sticky stigma, elongated style, and swollen basal ovary containing ovules.

NCERT Exercise 2.2: Leaf Venation, Roots & Floral Morphology
Q2
Can you determine what kind of root a plant has without digging it out of the soil? Explain the scientific rationale with two examples.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Yes, Absolutely:
A botanist or student does not need to dig out or injure a plant to discover its root architecture. One can simply inspect the venation pattern on its leaves, because nature exhibits a direct, universal structural correlation:
The Scientific Correlation Rule:
• If the leaves exhibit Reticulate Venation (net-like arrangement of veins), the plant possesses a Taproot System with a dominant central root and secondary lateral branches (e.g., Pea, Mango, Rose, Gram).
• If the leaves exhibit Parallel Venation (veins running side-by-side like railway tracks), the plant possesses a Fibrous Root System with a cluster of equal-sized roots originating from the stem base (e.g., Grass, Maize, Wheat, Paddy).

Q3
Describe the main reproductive organs of a flower. Differentiate clearly between anther and ovary.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
1. Stamen (Male Reproductive Organ):
Consists of a slender stalk called the filament and a terminal bilobed sac called the anther. The anther produces tiny, dust-like yellow pollen grains containing the male gametes.
2. Pistil / Carpel (Female Reproductive Organ):
Situated in the center of the flower. It consists of three regions:
• Stigma: Sticky terminal surface that receives pollen grains.
• Style: Long slender tube connecting stigma to ovary.
• Ovary: Swollen basal chamber that encloses ovules (female reproductive cells). Post-fertilization, the ovary develops into a fruit and ovules mature into seeds.
Direct Comparison (Anther vs Ovary):
• Anther: Part of stamen (male organ); produces pollen grains.
• Ovary: Part of pistil (female organ); produces ovules and later transforms into the fruit.

CBSE Competency-Based Question (Ecological Adaptation Case Study)
Case Study: Desert vs Aquatic Plant Structural Adaptations:
During a field trip, students observed a prickly Cactus plant growing in dry desert sand and a Water Lily floating effortlessly on a village pond.
(a) How has the Cactus modified its leaves, stem, and root system to survive extreme desert drought?
(b) How are the leaves and stems of a Water Lily specially adapted for an aquatic habitat?
(c) Why do underwater plants have narrow, ribbon-like leaves?
Detailed Analytical Solution & Marking Scheme

(a) Xerophytic (Desert) Adaptations of Cactus:
• Leaves: Modified into sharp spines to drastically minimize water loss through transpiration and defend against herbivores.
• Stem: Thick, green, succulent, and fleshy; coated with a waxy cuticle layer to store water and perform photosynthesis in place of leaves.
• Roots: Deep-penetrating taproot system spreading widely beneath the topsoil to absorb every drop of occasional rainwater.

(b) Hydrophytic (Aquatic) Adaptations of Water Lily:
• Leaves: Broad, circular, disc-shaped floating leaves covered with a waxy water-repellent coating. Stomata are situated only on the upper surface exposed to air.
• Stem: Long, hollow, flexible, light stalks with large air spaces (aerenchyma) that provide buoyancy and withstand water currents without snapping.

(c) Reason for Narrow Ribbon-like Leaves in Submerged Plants:
Submerged plants (such as Tape grass / Vallisneria and Hydrilla) have thin, ribbon-like leaves so that fast-moving water currents can flow past them freely without tearing or uprooting the plant.
