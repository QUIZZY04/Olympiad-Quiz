📌 Key Magnetic Principles & Field Concepts
  • Discovery & Types of Magnets: Naturally occurring magnetic rock is called Magnetite (an iron ore, Fe₃O₄), discovered in Magnesia. Artificial magnets are fashioned into shapes: Bar magnet, Horse-shoe magnet, Cylindrical magnet, and Ball-ended magnet.
  • Magnetic vs Non-Magnetic Materials:
    • Magnetic: Materials strongly attracted towards a magnet (e.g., Iron, Steel, Nickel, Cobalt).
    • Non-Magnetic: Materials that experience no attraction towards a magnet (e.g., Wood, Plastic, Glass, Copper, Aluminium, Paper).
  • Poles of a Magnet: Every magnet has two distinct poles where magnetic force is most concentrated: North Pole (N) and South Pole (S). Magnetic poles always exist in inseparable pairs (monopoles do not exist).
  • Fundamental Law of Magnetism:
    • Unlike (opposite) poles ATTRACT: North pole attracts South pole (N ↔ S).
    • Like (similar) poles REPEL: North repels North (N ⇄ N); South repels South (S ⇄ S). Repulsion is the only sure test of magnetism!
  • Directive Property & Compass: A freely suspended magnet always aligns itself along the geographic North-South direction. This directive property is utilized in the magnetic compass for navigation.
  • Preservation & Care: Magnets lose their magnetism if heated strongly, hammered, dropped from heights, or stored without magnetic keepers (soft iron bars).

NCERT Exercise 4.1: Magnetic Properties & Material Classification
Q1
A bar magnet has no markings to indicate its poles. How can you find out which end is the North Pole using only a piece of thread?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Method of Free Suspension (Directive Property):
1. Tie the thread tightly around the exact midpoint of the unmarked bar magnet so that it balances horizontally.
2. Suspend the magnet freely from a wooden stand in a room free from air draughts and away from magnetic objects.
3. Gently rotate the magnet and allow it to come to rest naturally.
4. Repeat this multiple times and note the alignment of the ends.
Inference & Identification:
• A freely suspended magnet always aligns itself along the geographic North-South direction.
• By observing the direction of sunrise (East) and sunset (West), identify the geographic North.
• The end of the magnet that points towards the Geographic North is the North-Seeking Pole (North Pole).
• The other end pointing towards the Geographic South is the South Pole.

NCERT Exercise 4.2: Magnetic Compass & Preservation
Q2
Why is repulsion considered the only sure test of magnetism, while attraction is not? Explain with a clear illustration.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Why Attraction Fails as a Sure Test:
A magnet attracts opposite poles of another magnet, but it also attracts any unmagnetized magnetic material (like an ordinary iron nail or steel bar). Therefore, if two objects attract each other, one cannot be sure whether both are magnets or if one is merely an unmagnetized piece of iron.
Why Repulsion is the Sure Test:
Repulsion happens exclusively between two similar magnetic poles (North repelling North, or South repelling South). An unmagnetized iron bar can never repel a magnet. Thus, only when an object repels a known magnet can it be conclusively proved to be a magnet itself.

Q3
Explain how you can turn an ordinary iron sewing needle into a temporary magnet using the touch stroke method. How would you test if it is magnetized?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Single-Touch Magnetization Method:
1. Place the clean iron needle horizontally on a flat wooden table.
2. Take a bar magnet and place one of its poles (say North pole) at one end of the needle.
3. Move the magnet along the entire length of the needle until you reach the other end, without lifting it.
4. Lift the magnet into the air, return it to the starting point, and repeat the stroke in the same direction about 30 to 40 times.
Crucial Precaution: Do not rub the magnet back and forth; the stroke must be strictly unidirectional using the same pole.
Testing for Magnetization:
Bring a few small iron all-pins or paper clips near the needle. If the needle attracts the pins to its tips, it has successfully transformed into a temporary magnet.

CBSE Competency-Based Question (Modern Engineering Case Study)
Case Study: Maglev Trains & Demagnetization Hazards:
High-speed Magnetic Levitation (Maglev) trains in countries like Japan and China travel at over 500 km/h without touching railway tracks. Electromagnets on the train undercarriage and the track have identical magnetic poles facing each other.
(a) Which fundamental principle of magnetism allows the Maglev train to float above the track?
(b) What are two major advantages of a floating train over conventional wheeled trains?
(c) Why are laboratory magnets kept with soft iron keepers across their poles when stored for long periods?
Detailed Analytical Solution & Marking Scheme

(a) Working Principle:
Maglev trains operate on the Principle of Magnetic Repulsion (Like poles repel each other). Because similar poles on the track and train face each other, the powerful upward repulsive force lifts the entire weight of the train 10 to 15 mm into the air.

(b) Advantages over Wheeled Trains:
1. Zero Mechanical Friction: Without wheel-rail contact, there is no physical friction, allowing ultra-high speeds with significantly less energy consumption.
2. Whisper-Quiet Operation & Low Maintenance: There is almost no noise pollution or mechanical wear and tear of tracks.

(c) Function of Magnetic Keepers:
When stored freely, isolated magnetic poles demagnetize themselves over time due to interaction with Earth's magnetic field and thermal vibration. Soft iron keepers provide a closed magnetic loop (path for magnetic field lines), preserving magnetic strength indefinitely.
