📌 Key Chemical Separation Techniques & Principles
  • Need for Separation: To remove harmful or undesirable substances (e.g., stones from rice), to separate two useful components (e.g., butter from milk), and to obtain pure substances for scientific use.
  • Separation of Insoluble Solid Mixtures:
    • Handpicking: Manually removing visibly different, large-sized impurities present in small quantities (e.g., picking small stones and husk from wheat).
    • Threshing: Beating harvested stalks against a hard surface or using machines to free edible grains from stalks.
    • Winnowing: Separating lighter components (husk/chaff) from heavier grains with the help of blowing wind or fan.
    • Sieving: Passing a mixture through a mesh sieve where smaller particles pass through holes while larger impurities are retained (e.g., sieving fine flour, separating pebbles from construction sand).
  • Separation of Insoluble Solids from Liquids:
    • Sedimentation: Allowing a suspension of heavier, insoluble solid particles in liquid to settle down undisturbed at the bottom under gravity (the settled solid is called sediment).
    • Decantation: The careful pouring off of the clear top liquid (supernatant) into another container without disturbing the settled sediment.
    • Filtration: Passing an insoluble solid-liquid mixture through a porous filter paper cone. The clear liquid that passes through is the filtrate; solid particles retained on the filter paper are the residue.
  • Separation of Soluble Solids from Liquids:
    • Evaporation: Heating a solution until the liquid solvent completely vaporizes, leaving behind the solid solute (e.g., harvesting common salt from sea water).
    • Saturated Solution: A solution in which no more solute can be dissolved at that given temperature. Solubility increases when the solution is heated.

NCERT Exercise 9.1: Solid & Liquid Mixture Separation
Q1
How will you separate a mixture of sand and water? Explain the steps of sedimentation, decantation, and filtration, highlighting which method gives the purest liquid.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Step 1: Sedimentation:
Leave the sand-water mixture undisturbed in a beaker for 15 to 20 minutes. Due to gravity, the heavy, insoluble sand particles settle at the bottom of the beaker as sediment, leaving water relatively clearer at the top.
Step 2: Decantation:
Slowly tilt the beaker without shaking it, and pour the upper clear water into a second clean beaker. While this removes coarse sand, tiny suspended mud particles still remain in the decanted water.
Step 3: Filtration (Purest Liquid):
Fold a circular circular filter paper into a cone, fit it inside a glass funnel, and moisten it with water. Pour the decanted water through the funnel. Filter paper pores are microscopic; they retain all fine clay particles as residue on the paper, while crystal-clear water collects in the flask below as filtrate.
Conclusion: Filtration gives the purest clear water.

NCERT Exercise 9.2: Solutions, Solubility & Separation of Salt
Q2
What is a saturated solution? How can you dissolve more salt in an already saturated salt solution without adding more water?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Definition of Saturated Solution:
A solution in which no more quantity of a given solute (such as common salt) can dissolve at a specific, constant temperature is called a saturated solution. Any extra salt added settles undissolved at the bottom.
Dissolving More Solute by Heating:
To dissolve additional salt without adding water, simply heat the saturated solution. When temperature rises, the kinetic energy of water molecules increases, enlarging intermolecular spaces. The solubility of the solid solute rises, allowing the extra settled salt to dissolve completely.

CBSE Competency-Based Question (Industrial & Environmental Case Study)
Case Study: Sea Salt Harvesting & Municipal Drinking Water Purification:
In coastal Gujarat, sea water is captured in shallow lagoons called salt pans and allowed to stand under the blazing sun for days, leaving behind crusts of white common salt.
Meanwhile, in a municipal city waterworks, river water undergoes multiple sequential stages: Sedimentation tank → Coagulation with alum → Sand filtration beds → Chlorination.

(a) Which separation method is employed in coastal salt pans to harvest common salt?
(b) What role does adding alum (fitkari) play in municipal sedimentation tanks?
(c) Why is chlorination carried out as the final step before water is pumped to city taps?
Detailed Analytical Solution & Marking Scheme

(a) Harvesting Sea Salt:
The separation technique is Evaporation. The sun's heat slowly converts water in the shallow pans into water vapour. Once all water evaporates into the atmosphere, raw solid common salt is left behind as a crystal crust, which is collected and refined.

(b) Role of Alum (Coagulation / Loading):
Extremely fine suspended clay particles in river water are too light to settle quickly on their own. Adding alum coats these fine particles, making them heavier and clump together into larger flocs (loading). These heavy flocs rapidly settle to the bottom of the sedimentation tank.

(c) Purpose of Chlorination:
Physical processes like sedimentation and filtration remove suspended dirt, mud, and debris, but they cannot kill microscopic pathogenic microbes (bacteria and viruses). Chlorination is a chemical disinfection process that kills disease-causing germs, ensuring the municipal supply is safe and potable.
