Exercise Set 9.1 — If–Then Statements and Logic
Q1
Write the converse of each statement: (i) If it rains, then the ground is wet.  (ii) If a triangle is equilateral, then all its angles are 60°.  (iii) If a number is divisible by 6, then it is divisible by 2.
3 Marks
✓ Suggested Step-by-Step Solution

The converse of "If P, then Q" is "If Q, then P".

(i) If the ground is wet, then it rained.
(ii) If all angles of a triangle are 60°, then the triangle is equilateral.
(iii) If a number is divisible by 2, then it is divisible by 6. (Note: The converse is NOT always true — e.g., 4 is divisible by 2 but not 6.)

Q2
For the statement "If a quadrilateral is a square, then it is a rectangle": (i) Write the converse.  (ii) Is the original statement true?  (iii) Is the converse true? Justify.
4 Marks
✓ Suggested Step-by-Step Solution
(i) Converse: If a quadrilateral is a rectangle, then it is a square.
(ii) Original is TRUE: A square has all right angles and equal sides → it is a rectangle.
(iii) Converse is FALSE: A rectangle has all right angles but sides need not be equal. Example: A 4 cm × 6 cm rectangle is NOT a square. Counterexample disproves the converse.

Exercise Set 9.2 — Proofs in Geometry
Q1
Prove: The base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal.
4 Marks
✓ Suggested Step-by-Step Solution

Given: Triangle ABC with AB = AC. To prove: ∠B = ∠C.

Construction: Draw AD, the bisector of ∠A, meeting BC at D.
In △ABD and △ACD:
AB = AC (given)
AD = AD (common)
∠BAD = ∠CAD (AD bisects ∠A)
By SAS congruence: △ABD ≅ △ACD
Therefore: ∠ABD = ∠ACD (CPCT), i.e., ∠B = ∠C □

Exercise Set 9.3 — Counterexamples
Q1
Disprove the following using a counterexample: "If n is a prime number, then n is odd."
2 Marks
✓ Suggested Step-by-Step Solution

Counterexample: n = 2.

2 is a prime number (divisible only by 1 and itself).
But 2 is even, not odd.

Conclusion: The statement is FALSE. n = 2 is a prime that is not odd. One counterexample is sufficient to disprove a universal statement.

📝 Case Study & Real-Life Modeling
Logical Deductions & Geometry Theorems: Consider the geometric proposition: 'If two lines intersect, then the vertically opposite angles are equal.'
(a) Identify the hypothesis (P) and the conclusion (Q) in the proposition.
(b) Write the converse of the statement.
(c) Is the converse always true? Explain with a counterexample or diagrammatic explanation.
(a) Hypothesis (P): 'Two straight lines intersect.' Conclusion (Q): 'The vertically opposite angles are equal.'
(b) Converse: 'If two angles formed by intersecting lines are equal, then they are vertically opposite angles.'
(c) The converse is not necessarily true in general without specifying geometric orientation: two non-vertically opposite angles can also be equal (for example, alternate interior angles or adjacent supplementary angles each equal to 90°).
🧠 HOTS & Assertion-Reasoning
Assertion (A): A single counterexample is sufficient to disprove a universally quantified mathematical conjecture.
Reason (R): For a universal statement 'For all x, P(x)' to be true, P(x) must hold for every member of the domain without exception.
Choose: (a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation. (b) Both A and R are true, but R is not correct explanation. (c) A is true, R is false. (d) A is false, R is true.
Correct Choice: (a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation.
Explanation: The negation of '∀x, P(x)' is '∃x such that ¬P(x)'. Finding even one instance where the conjecture fails proves the negation and invalidates the claim.
