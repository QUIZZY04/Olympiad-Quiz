Question 1
What are the controls affecting the climate of India?
3 Marks (50-60 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

There are six major climatic controls that determine the climate of any region, including India:

  1. Latitude: The Tropic of Cancer passes through the middle of India, dividing it into tropical (southern half) and sub-tropical (northern half) zones.
  2. Altitude: Higher elevations (Himalayas) experience much cooler temperatures compared to coastal plains.
  3. Pressure and Wind System: Governed by surface winds, upper-air jet streams, and tropical cyclones.
  4. Distance from the Sea (Continentality): Coastal areas enjoy an equable maritime climate, while interior northern plains experience extreme seasonal variations.
  5. Ocean Currents: Warm and cold offshore currents influence coastal temperatures.
  6. Relief Features: High mountains act as climatic barriers, intercepting rain-bearing winds (Western Ghats windward slopes) or creating rain-shadow deserts (Leeward Deccan/Thar).

Question 2
Explain the mechanism of the Indian Monsoon.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The mechanism of the Indian Monsoon is driven by five interconnected meteorological phenomena:

  1. Differential Heating of Land and Water: In summer, the vast landmass of northern India heats up rapidly, creating an intense low-pressure trough over north-western India, while surrounding seas remain comparatively cool under high pressure.
  2. Shift of the ITCZ (Inter Tropical Convergence Zone): In summer, the equatorial trough of low pressure shifts northward over the Ganga plain (monsoon trough), attracting southern hemisphere trade winds.
  3. High-Pressure Cell East of Madagascar: The presence of a persistent high-pressure area east of Madagascar (around 20°S in the Indian Ocean) influences the intensity of the south-west monsoon.
  4. Intense Heating of the Tibetan Plateau: In summer, the Tibetan plateau gets strongly heated, resulting in vertical air currents and the formation of a low-pressure area about 9 km above sea level.
  5. Movement of Jet Streams: The withdrawal of the Sub-Tropical Westerly Jet Stream from south of the Himalayas to the north of the Tibetan plateau, and the simultaneous introduction of the Tropical Easterly Jet Stream over the Indian peninsula (around 14°N), triggers the sudden 'burst' of the monsoon.

Question 3
Give reasons for the following:
(i) The Tamil Nadu coast receives winter rainfall.
(ii) The delta region of the eastern coast is frequently struck by cyclones.
(iii) Parts of Rajasthan, Gujarat and the leeward side of the Western Ghats are drought-prone.
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(i) Tamil Nadu Winter Rainfall: During the winter months, the North-East Retreating Monsoon winds blow from land to sea. While crossing the Bay of Bengal, they pick up copious moisture and strike the Coromandel coast (Tamil Nadu) as on-shore winds, causing torrential winter rains.

(ii) Cyclones on Eastern Deltas: By early November, the low-pressure conditions over north-western India shift to the Bay of Bengal. This shift generates severe tropical cyclonic depressions over the Andaman Sea. Driven westward by easterly winds, these destructive cyclones strike the densely populated deltas of the Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri, causing catastrophic loss of life and property.

(iii) Drought-Prone Western Regions:

  • Western Ghats Leeward Side: Moisture-laden South-West Monsoon winds are forced to rise on the windward side, dropping heavy rainfall; as they descend on the leeward (eastern) side, the air warms up, relative humidity drops, creating a dry rain-shadow zone.
  • Rajasthan & Gujarat: The Arabian Sea branch of the monsoon blows parallel to the Aravali Range without being intercepted by any high relief barrier. The high temperatures of the desert further lower relative humidity, preventing condensation.

Question 4
Why is the monsoon considered a unifying bond in India?
3 Marks (50-60 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Despite wide regional variations in temperature and precipitation, the monsoon acts as a powerful national unifying force across India:

  • Rhythm of Agricultural Cycle: The entire Indian agricultural calendar, sowing of Kharif crops, and economic prosperity revolve around the arrival and distribution of monsoon rains.
  • Cultural and Festive Unity: Celebrations, folk songs, music, and major festivals throughout India (Teej, Baisakhi, Onam, Pongal) celebrate the monsoon harvest and the retreat of summer heat.
  • Water Supply Binding the Nation: The monsoon feeds all major river systems, replenishes reservoirs and groundwater tables, binding the multi-ethnic nation in a single hydrological destiny.

Climate Science & El Niño Competency
What is ENSO (El Niño Southern Oscillation), and how does it affect the performance of the Indian monsoon?

Normally, the tropical eastern South Pacific Ocean experiences high pressure, while the tropical eastern Indian Ocean experiences low pressure. In certain years, there is a periodic reversal in pressure conditions called the Southern Oscillation (SO). Connected with this is El Niño, a warm ocean current that temporarily replaces the cold Peruvian (Humboldt) current along the coast of Peru every 2 to 5 years.

This complex ocean-atmosphere coupled system is termed ENSO. When an El Niño event occurs, it weakens the pressure gradient over the Indian Ocean, frequently leading to delayed onset of the monsoon, prolonged dry breaks, and severe droughts across the Indian subcontinent.

Regional Variations & Geographic Thinking
Explain with examples the extreme variations in temperature and precipitation across different regions of India.

India exhibits dramatic climatic contrasts: (1) Temperature: In summer, mercury touches 50°C in parts of Rajasthan desert, while it may be around 20°C in Pahalgam (Kashmir). On a winter night, temperature at Drass in Ladakh drops to –45°C, while Thiruvananthapuram records a balmy 22°C; (2) Precipitation: While Mawsynram in Meghalaya receives over 1,141 cm (highest in the world), Jaisalmer in Rajasthan receives less than 10 cm annually. Precipitation falls as snow in upper Himalayas, but as rain over the rest of the country.
