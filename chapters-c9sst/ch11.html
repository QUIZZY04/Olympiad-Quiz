Question 1
Answer the following questions briefly:
(i) Why is the rate of population growth in India declining since 1981?
(ii) Discuss the major components of population growth.
(iii) Define age structure, death rate and birth rate.
(iv) How is migration a determinant factor of population change?
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(i) Why Growth Rate is Declining since 1981: Since 1981, the annual growth rate started declining steadily because birth rates declined rapidly due to increasing literacy, greater awareness of family planning, female education, and urbanization.

(ii) Major Components of Population Growth:

  1. Birth Rate: Number of live births per thousand persons in a year.
  2. Death Rate: Number of deaths per thousand persons in a year.
  3. Migration: Movement of people across regions and territories (internal or international).

(iii) Age Structure: The proportion of people in different age groups in a country (Children: 0–14 years, Working Age: 15–59 years, Aged: 60+ years).

(iv) Migration as a Determinant: Internal migration does not alter the absolute population size of the country, but it significantly changes the demographic distribution, density, age composition, and sex ratio between rural and urban areas (e.g., massive rural-to-urban migration driven by poverty 'push' factors and city employment 'pull' factors).

Question 2
What is the relation between occupational structure and economic development of a country?
3 Marks (50-60 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The distribution of the population according to different occupations shows a clear, direct correlation with the level of economic development of a country:

  • Developing Nations: A high proportion of the workforce is engaged in the Primary Sector (agriculture, forestry, fishing). In India, approximately 50-60% of the population historically depended on agriculture, indicating a developing economy.
  • Developed Nations: A major proportion of the workforce (up to 70–80%) is engaged in the Secondary Sector (manufacturing, construction) and Tertiary Sector (services, banking, IT, trade), leading to higher national productivity, per capita income, and living standards.

Question 3
What are the significant features of the National Population Policy (NPP) 2000?
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The National Population Policy (NPP) 2000 was formulated by the Government of India to provide a holistic framework for advancing health, education, and family welfare:

  1. Free and Compulsory Education: Imparting free and compulsory school education up to 14 years of age.
  2. Reducing Infant Mortality: Reducing the Infant Mortality Rate (IMR) to below 30 per 1,000 live births.
  3. Universal Immunisation: Achieving universal immunization of children against all vaccine-preventable diseases.
  4. Delayed Marriage for Girls: Promoting delayed marriage for girls (strictly enforcing the legal age of 18 years and encouraging marriage after 20).
  5. Focus on Adolescents: Emphasizes addressing the nutritional, reproductive, and psychological needs of adolescents, protecting them from unwanted pregnancies, STDs, and promoting adolescent awareness.

Demographic Dividend & Youth Opportunity
What is meant by 'Demographic Dividend'? How can India leverage its large adolescent and working-age population?

Demographic Dividend refers to the economic growth potential that results from shifts in a population’s age structure, specifically when the share of the working-age population (15 to 59 years) is larger than the non-working dependent share (children and elderly). India currently possesses one of the youngest populations in the world, with over 50% under 25 years.

To leverage this window of opportunity, the nation must invest heavily in high-quality vocational education, digital skill development, health infrastructure, and foster job creation in manufacturing and services. Without adequate employment and skill training, this dividend risks becoming a demographic liability.

Population Density & Geographic Determinants
Explain the factors responsible for the uneven distribution of population density across India.

Population density in India varies drastically, from over 1,102 persons per sq. km in Bihar to barely 17 persons per sq. km in Arunachal Pradesh. This unevenness is governed by: (1) Terrain & Relief: Rugged mountains, steep slopes, and harsh rocky plateaus (Himalayas, Ladakh) discourage human settlement; flat plains (Northern Plains) encourage high density; (2) Climate: Moderate rainfall and pleasant climates attract settlements, while arid deserts (Thar) have low density; (3) Soil Fertility & Water: Deep, rich alluvial soils with abundant perennial water support intensive agriculture and colossal populations (UP, West Bengal).
