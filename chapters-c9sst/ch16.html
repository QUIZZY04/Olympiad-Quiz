Question 1
Describe the six Fundamental Rights guaranteed by the Constitution of India.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Part III of the Constitution of India guarantees six fundamental freedoms essential for human dignity and democratic citizenship:

  1. Right to Equality (Articles 14–18): Equality before the law, prohibition of discrimination based on religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth, equal opportunity in public employment, and abolition of untouchability and titles.
  2. Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22): Freedom of speech and expression, peaceful assembly, forming associations/unions, free movement throughout India, residing in any part of the country, and practicing any trade/profession. Protects personal liberty against arbitrary arrest and detention.
  3. Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24): Prohibits human trafficking, forced bonded labour (begar), and the employment of children below 14 years in factories, mines, and hazardous occupations.
  4. Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28): Guarantees freedom of conscience and the right to freely profess, practice, and propagate any religion. India has no official state religion.
  5. Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30): Guarantees religious and linguistic minorities the right to preserve their distinct language, script, and culture, and to establish and administer their own educational institutions.
  6. Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32): Empowers citizens to directly move the High Courts or Supreme Court to enforce their fundamental rights. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar termed this right the 'Heart and Soul of the Constitution'.

Question 2
Explain what life looks like without rights by giving examples from: (a) Guantanamo Bay, (b) Saudi Arabia, and (c) Kosovo.
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Historical and international instances illustrate the horrific reality of human existence in the absence of institutionalized rights:

  • (a) Prison in Guantanamo Bay: Around 600 people were secretly abducted by US forces across the globe following 9/11 and detained in a military naval base in Cuba without charge, trial, or access to lawyers. Even the UN and Amnesty International were denied access; basic rights of habeas corpus were crushed by executive fiat.
  • (b) Saudi Arabia: Ruled by an absolute hereditary monarch who selects judges and government officials. No political parties or elections are permitted. Citizens cannot publicly practice any religion other than Islam, and women face severe legal guardianship restrictions.
  • (c) Kosovo: In the late 1990s, the Serb-dominated military government of Slobodan Milosevic unleashed ethnic cleansing against the minority ethnic Albanian population, burning villages, committing mass rapes, and executing thousands of civilians simply because of their ethnic identity.

Question 3
How has the scope of rights expanded in India over the decades?
3 Marks (50-60 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The scope of rights has progressively broadened through judicial interpretations and legislative enactments:

  1. Expansion of Article 21 (Right to Life): The Supreme Court expanded the Right to Life to encompass the Right to Food, Right to Clean Drinking Water and Clean Environment, and the Right to Privacy (declared a fundamental right in 2017).
  2. Right to Education (RTE): Article 21A made free and compulsory elementary education a fundamental right for children aged 6–14.
  3. Right to Information (RTI): Enacted in 2005 under freedom of expression to empower citizens to demand transparency and examine public records.

Constitutional Remedies & Legal Activism
Why did Dr. B.R. Ambedkar describe the Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32) as the 'Heart and Soul of the Constitution'?

A declaration of fundamental rights is meaningless unless there is an effective mechanism to enforce them when violated by the state or powerful citizens. Article 32 gives citizens the direct right to approach the Supreme Court, which can issue extraordinary writs (Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Quo Warranto, and Certiorari) to strike down illegal executive orders or release unlawfully detained citizens.

Without Article 32, all other fundamental rights would be reduced to dead letters on paper. It provides the legal teeth that make all other rights real, enforceable, and supreme.

Human Rights & Civil Liberties
Are fundamental rights absolute in nature, or are they subject to reasonable restrictions? Explain with examples.

Fundamental rights in India are not absolute; they are subject to reasonable restrictions to balance individual liberty with national security, public order, morality, and the rights of others. For example: (1) Freedom of Speech (Article 19(1)(a)): Does not permit hate speech, defamation, inciting violence, or threatening the sovereignty of India; (2) Freedom of Assembly: Must be peaceful and without arms; (3) Freedom of Movement: Can be curbed in strategic border zones or during pandemics (lockdowns) for public health safety. Liberty without limits would result in anarchy.
