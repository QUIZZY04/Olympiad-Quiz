Economics: Understanding Economic Development – I

Poverty as a Challenge

Economics | Chapter 3 — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

NCERT Textbook Exercises — 100% Question Coverage
Question 1
Describe how the poverty line is estimated in India. Do you think that the present methodology of poverty estimation is appropriate?
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Estimation of Poverty Line in India:
A person is considered poor if his or her income or consumption level falls below a given minimum level necessary to fulfill basic human needs:

  • Minimum Nutritional Calorie Criteria: The accepted average daily calorie requirement in India is 2,400 calories per person per day in rural areas (higher due to strenuous physical manual work) and 2,100 calories per person per day in urban areas.
  • Monetary Per Capita Expenditure: Based on periodic consumer expenditure surveys by the NSO, these calorie requirements, along with minimum clothing, footwear, fuel, and education, are converted into a monetary poverty line (e.g., in 2011-12: ₹816 per capita per month in rural areas and ₹1,000 per month in urban areas).

Appropriateness of Methodology:
The present methodology is widely criticized as insufficient because it measures only a 'minimum subsistence level of living' rather than a 'reasonable level of living'. It overlooks vital dimensions of Human Poverty—such as access to quality education, healthcare, shelter, job security, gender empowerment, and human dignity.

Calorie norms: 2400 rural vs 2100 urban calories per person per day1.5 Marks
Monetary conversion into monthly per capita consumption expenditure1.0 Mark
Critique: Subsistence vs reasonable living, and neglect of Human Poverty dimensions1.5 Marks
Question 2
Identify the social and economic groups which are most vulnerable to poverty in India. Discuss the major reasons for poverty in India.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Most Vulnerable Groups:

  • Social Groups: Scheduled Castes (SC) and Scheduled Tribes (ST) households face the highest poverty ratios due to historical caste discrimination, landlessness, and low educational attainment.
  • Economic Groups: Rural Agricultural Labourer households and Urban Casual Labour households who depend on erratic, low-wage daily manual work.
  • Within Families: Women, elderly persons, and female infants suffer the worst deprivations within poor households.

Major Causes of Poverty in India:

  1. Historical Colonial Exploitation: British colonial policies ruined traditional Indian handicrafts, textile industries, and discouraged domestic industrial development, leaving India with a ruined agrarian base.
  2. High Population Growth: Rapid population growth combined with sluggish GDP growth in the initial post-independence decades resulted in very low growth rates of per capita income.
  3. Unequal Distribution of Land and Assets: Failure of land reform implementation in most states left millions of peasants landless.
  4. Lack of Formal Jobs: Industries could not generate adequate employment for job-seekers, pushing them into low-wage, insecure unorganised informal jobs.
  5. Social and Religious Spending: Poor families incur heavy debt to fulfill marriage, religious rituals, and social obligations, falling into chronic debt traps.
CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Identification of vulnerable social groups (SC, ST) and economic groups (casual labour)1.5 Marks
Historical colonial de-industrialization cause1.0 Mark
Population pressure, land inequality, and job shortage causes1.5 Marks
Social obligations and debt-trap factors1.0 Mark
Question 3
Describe the current two-pronged government strategy for poverty alleviation in India. Give the salient features of MGNREGA 2005.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The Government of India's anti-poverty strategy is structured on two core pillars: (1) Promotion of Economic Growth and (2) Targeted Anti-Poverty Programmes.

Rapid economic growth widens opportunities and provides financial resources to invest in human development. However, because the poor cannot immediately benefit from growth, targeted welfare schemes are essential.

Salient Features of MGNREGA 2005 (Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act):

  1. Guaranteed 100 Days of Wage Employment: Aims to provide at least 100 days of guaranteed wage employment in a financial year to every rural household whose adult members volunteer to do unskilled manual work.
  2. Right to Unemployment Allowance: If an applicant is not provided employment within 15 days of applying, they are legally entitled to a daily unemployment allowance paid by the state government.
  3. Women Empowerment: One-third (33%) of the proposed jobs are legally reserved for women.
  4. Creation of Durable Rural Assets: Focuses on sustainable works addressing causes of drought, deforestation, and soil erosion (water harvesting, canal building, rural road connectivity).
CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Two-pronged strategy: Economic growth + Targeted welfare schemes1.5 Marks
MGNREGA: 100 days guaranteed unskilled wage employment1.5 Marks
Unemployment allowance within 15 days rule1.0 Mark
33% reservation for women and durable asset creation1.0 Mark
Question 4
What are the major causes of poverty in India? Describe the key targeted anti-poverty programmes launched by the government.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Major Causes of Poverty in India:

  1. Historical Colonial Exploitation: British colonial policies ruined traditional handicrafts and discouraged indigenous textile and manufacturing development, stifling income growth.
  2. Population Explosion: Rapid population growth combined with low economic growth rate kept per capita income low.
  3. Unequal Distribution of Land and Assets: Failure of land reform measures in several states led to large numbers of landless agricultural labourers.
  4. Socio-Cultural Factors: Heavy social expenditures on marriages, religious rituals, and indebtedness to informal moneylenders.

Major Targeted Anti-Poverty Programmes:

  • MGNREGA (2005): Guarantees 100 days of wage employment per year to every rural household, reserving 1/3 of jobs for women.
  • Prime Minister Rozgar Yojana (PMRY): Provides subsidized financial assistance to educated unemployed youth in rural and small towns to set up micro-enterprises.
  • Pradhan Mantri Gramodaya Yojana (PMGY): Provides central assistance to states for basic rural services—primary health, primary education, rural drinking water, and rural housing.
  • Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY): Provides highly subsidized foodgrains (rice at ₹3/kg, wheat at ₹2/kg) to the poorest of the poor families under BPL.
CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Four root causes of poverty in India2.0 Marks
MGNREGA 2005 features and guarantees1.5 Marks
PMRY, PMGY, and Antyodaya Anna Yojana features1.5 Marks

Interstate Disparity Analysis
Analyse the significant interstate disparities in poverty within India. How have states like Kerala, Punjab, and Tamil Nadu successfully reduced poverty?

Poverty is highly uneven across Indian states. States like Bihar (33.7%) and Odisha (32.6%) continue to suffer from the highest poverty ratios, characterized by rural landlessness and frequent natural disasters. Conversely, several states achieved dramatic reductions through targeted policy interventions:

(1) Punjab and Haryana: Succeeded by maintaining high agricultural growth rates driven by Green Revolution investments; (2) Kerala: Focused aggressively on Human Resource Development, achieving near 100% literacy, exceptional healthcare, and high female education; (3) West Bengal: Implemented comprehensive land reforms (Operation Barga), securing land rights for sharecroppers; (4) Tamil Nadu & Andhra Pradesh: Relied on an efficient Public Distribution System (PDS) ensuring cheap, subsidized food grains reached all poor households.

Multidimensional Poverty & Global Standards
What is the concept of 'Multidimensional Poverty', and how does it provide a more comprehensive picture of human deprivation than mere income?

Traditional income-based poverty measures only whether a household can spend a few rupees on basic calories. In contrast, Multidimensional Poverty measures overlapping deprivations that people experience simultaneously across three vital dimensions: Health (nutrition, child mortality), Education (years of schooling, attendance), and Standard of Living (cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, assets).

A family may earn above the income line but still have no access to clean drinking water, live in a leaking shanty, and have malnourished children. Multidimensional assessment helps governments target specific deprivations rather than relying on cash handouts alone.
