History: India and the Contemporary World – I

Forest Society and Colonialism

India and the Contemporary World – I | Chapter 4 — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

NCERT Textbook Exercises — 100% Question Coverage
Question 1
Discuss how the changes in forest management in the colonial period affected the following groups: (a) Shifting cultivators, (b) Nomadic and pastoralist communities, (c) Firms trading in timber/forest produce, (d) Plantation owners, (e) Kings/British officials engaged in shikar (hunting).
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The implementation of colonial forest laws (such as the Indian Forest Act of 1865 and 1878) radically altered the lives of various communities:

  1. Shifting Cultivators: European foresters considered shifting cultivation (jhum, bewar, podu) harmful because fires could damage valuable timber trees (teak and sal) and calculate taxes accurately. The British banned shifting cultivation, displacing thousands from their ancestral homes and forcing them into low-wage bonded labour.
  2. Nomadic and Pastoralist Communities: Traditional pastoral groups (Koravas, Karachas) lost their customary grazing rights as vast forests were categorized as 'Reserved'. They were prevented from entering forests, labeled under the Criminal Tribes Act (1871), and compelled to work in factories, mines, and plantations under armed surveillance.
  3. Firms Trading in Timber/Forest Produce: European firms received exclusive monopoly concessions to exploit forest timber. Small local traders were dispossessed, while large British corporate cartels made astronomical profits exporting teak and sleeper wood.
  4. Plantation Owners: Large tracts of natural forests were cleared and handed over to European planters at nominal rates for lucrative commercial tea, coffee, and rubber plantations.
  5. Kings and British Officials (Shikar): While customary tribal hunting for food was declared illegal poaching, large-scale trophy hunting (shikar) became a sport of civilised domination. Over 80,000 tigers, 150,000 leopards, and 200,000 wolves were slaughtered between 1875 and 1925 under government bounties.

Impact on shifting cultivators (loss of livelihood and eviction)1.0 Mark
Impact on pastoralists (Criminal Tribes Act and loss of pasture)1.0 Mark

Question 2
What are the similarities between colonial management of the forests in Bastar and in Java?
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The colonial forest policies of the British in Bastar (Central India) and the Dutch in Java (Indonesia) exhibited striking similarities:

  • State Monopoly over Forests: Both the British and Dutch enacted forest laws that declared state ownership over timberlands and stripped indigenous communities of their customary rights to collect fuel, food, and building materials.
  • Ban on Shifting Agriculture: Both regimes prohibited shifting cultivation, claiming it ruined valuable teak timber and caused soil degradation.
  • Forced Exploitation of Tribal Labour: In Java, the Dutch introduced the Blandongdiensten system, exempting villages from rent if they provided free labour and buffaloes for cutting and hauling timber. Similarly, in Bastar, forest villagers were forced to provide free labour (begar) to build roads and cut trees.
  • Armed Tribal Rebellions: Brutal colonial policies triggered massive peasant rebellions. In Java, Surontiko Samin led the Saminist movement, questioning state ownership of wind, water, and wood. In Bastar, the Bastar Rebellion of 1910 led by Gunda Dhur fought British occupation.

State forest monopoly and confiscation of customary tribal rights1.0 Mark
Prohibition of shifting cultivation in both territories1.0 Mark
Forced labour systems (Blandongdiensten in Java vs Begar in Bastar)1.0 Mark
Indigenous resistance movements (Saminism vs Bastar Rebellion)1.0 Mark
Question 3
Between 1880 and 1920, forest cover in the Indian subcontinent declined by 9.7 million hectares. Discuss the role of the following factors in this decline: (a) Railways, (b) Shipbuilding, (c) Agricultural expansion, (d) Commercial farming, (e) Tea/Coffee plantations.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Colonial exploitation systematically caused catastrophic deforestation in India between 1880 and 1920 due to five primary drivers:

  1. Railways: Railways were vital for British colonial troop movements and the transport of raw materials to ports. Wood was needed for two purposes: as fuel to run locomotives and as wooden sleepers to hold railway tracks together. Each mile of track required 1,760 to 2,000 sleepers. By 1910, over 51,650 km of track was laid, consuming hundreds of thousands of teak and sal trees annually.
  2. Shipbuilding for the Royal Navy: By the early 19th century, oak forests in England were exhausted, jeopardizing Britain's naval supremacy. Search parties were dispatched to India, and massive oak/teak logs from Malabar and Central India were harvested and shipped to Britain for warship construction.
  3. Agricultural Expansion: The British viewed uncultivated forests as 'waste lands' generating no land revenue. The colonial state promoted agricultural clearing to maximize land revenue and feed growing British industrial cities with grain.
  4. Commercial Farming: Large tracts of diverse forests were cleared to grow commercial cash crops required by British factories—cotton, jute, indigo, and sugarcane.
  5. Tea and Coffee Plantations: Colonial legislation enabled European companies to buy pristine forest land at nominal rates, enclosing natural biodiverse jungles into monoculture tea and coffee estates.
CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Demand for railway sleepers (1760-2000 per mile) and locomotive fuel1.5 Marks
Royal Navy timber demands due to exhaustion of English oak forests1.0 Mark
Revenue imperative: Conversion of 'waste land' into taxable farmland1.0 Mark
Export cash crops (cotton, jute) and enclosed European tea/coffee plantations1.5 Marks
Question 4
Why are forests severely affected by wars?
3 Marks (50-60 शब्द / words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Modern warfare inflicts severe, often irreversible damage on forest ecosystems due to strategic, industrial, and defensive requirements:

  • Exploitation for Military Supplies: During World War I and World War II, forest departments abandoned scientific working plans and carried out rampant, unmonitored felling to meet enormous wartime demands for timber, trenches, packaging, and military ships.
  • 'Scorched Earth' Policies: Before retreating from an advancing enemy, armies deliberately burn and destroy forests and sawmills to prevent them from falling into enemy hands. For instance, the Dutch destroyed huge teak sawmills in Java before the Japanese arrived in World War II.
  • Exploitation by Local Populations during Chaos: When central colonial authority collapsed during wartime, local villagers cut down restricted forests to reclaim ancestral agricultural lands.
CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Rampant military extraction of timber for war materials and packaging1.0 Mark
Scorched earth strategy: Deliberate destruction of sawmills and forests1.0 Mark
Loss of conservation oversight and post-war ecological collapse1.0 Mark

Historical Movement & Tribal Leadership
Analyse the causes and significance of the 1910 Bastar Rebellion against the British colonial administration.

In 1905, the British proposed to reserve two-thirds of the forest in Bastar, ban shifting cultivation, and strictly regulate the collection of forest produce. This threatened the very survival of tribes like the Maria and Muria Gonds. Under the leadership of Gunda Dhur from village Nethanar, tribal leaders circulated mango twigs, lumps of earth, chillies, and arrows to mobilise resistance.

Rebels looted bazars, attacked colonial schools and police stations, and redistributed grain. Although the rebellion was brutally suppressed by British troops, the administration was forced to suspend reservation work and reduce the reserved area to half of what had been planned.

Ecological Evaluation & Forest Management
Critically examine Dietrich Brandis's system of 'Scientific Forestry'. Why was it fundamentally unscientific from an ecological standpoint?

Dietrich Brandis, appointed India's first Inspector General of Forests in 1864, introduced 'Scientific Forestry' under which diverse natural forests with multiple tree species were cleared and replaced by a single species (monoculture) planted in straight rows, such as teak or sal plantations.

From an ecological perspective, this system was disastrous: it destroyed biological diversity, wiped out medicinal herbs, disrupted wildlife food chains, made forests vulnerable to pests, and deprived villagers of fruits, tubers, and fodder. It was 'scientific' only for maximizing commercial timber profit for the British Empire.
