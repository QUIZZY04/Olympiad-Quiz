Drainage

Contemporary India – I | Chapter 3 — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

NCERT Textbook Exercises — 100% Question Coverage
Question 1
Answer the following questions briefly:
(i) What is meant by a water divide? Give an example.
(ii) Which is the largest river basin in India?
(iii) Where do the rivers Indus and Ganga have their origin?
(iv) Name the two headstreams of the Ganga. Where do they meet to form the Ganga?
(v) Why does the Brahmaputra in its Tibetan part have less silt, despite a longer course?
(vi) Which two Peninsular rivers flow through trough/rift valleys?
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(i) Water Divide: Any elevated area, such as a mountain ridge or upland, that separates two adjacent drainage basins is known as a water divide. Example: The upland of Ambala acts as a water divide between the Indus and the Ganga river systems.

(ii) Largest River Basin: The Ganga Basin (covering over 8.6 lakh sq. km in India with a length of over 2,500 km).

(iii) Origins:

  • Indus: Originates in Tibet, near Lake Mansarowar.
  • Ganga: Originates as Bhagirathi from the Gangotri Glacier in Uttarkashi district, Uttarakhand.

(iv) Two headstreams of Ganga: The Bhagirathi and the Alaknanda. They meet at Devprayag in Uttarakhand to form the river Ganga.

(v) Why Brahmaputra carries less silt in Tibet: In Tibet (where it is called the Tsangpo), the river flows through a cold and dry high-altitude plateau. There is little rainfall and sparse vegetation, resulting in minimal surface erosion. Hence, despite its long course in Tibet, it carries a smaller volume of water and very little silt until it enters heavy rainfall zones in Arunachal Pradesh and Assam.

(vi) Rivers flowing through rift valleys: The Narmada and the Tapi (flowing westwards into the Arabian Sea between the Satpura and Vindhya/Ajanta ranges).

Question 2
Compare the Himalayan and the Peninsular rivers.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The rivers of India are fundamentally divided into two major geological and hydrological groups:

Feature Himalayan Rivers (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra) Peninsular Rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Narmada, etc.)
Nature of Flow Perennial: Flow throughout the year; fed by both melting glaciers/snow and monsoonal rainfall. Seasonal: Dependent purely on monsoonal rainfall; during dry summer months, even large rivers shrink into narrow channels.
Origin & Basin Originate in lofty snow-capped northern Himalayan glaciers; possess immense, deep basins and long courses to the sea. Originate in the Western Ghats or Central Highlands; possess shorter courses and shallower, mature basins.
Valley Shape & Drainage Flow through steep V-shaped gorges, perform intense erosional work in upper courses, form meanders, oxbow lakes, and vast deltas (Sundarbans). Flow through broad, shallow, graded valleys with gentle gradients; have reached base level of erosion; West-flowing rivers form estuaries, East-flowing form deltas.

Nature of flow: Perennial (glacier+rain) vs Seasonal (rain-fed)1.5 Marks
Source, course length, and basin scale comparison1.5 Marks
Erosional stages: Deep gorges & meanders vs broad graded valleys & estuaries2.0 Marks
Question 3
Why are rivers important for the country’s economy? Discuss the causes of river water pollution and government remedial measures.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Economic Importance of Rivers:
Water from rivers is a basic natural resource essential for human survival and economic progress: (1) Agriculture: Drives canal irrigation in an agrarian economy where millions depend on farming; (2) Hydroelectric Power Generation: Clean, renewable electricity generated via dams (Bhakra Nangal, Tehri); (3) Inland Navigation: Cost-effective bulk transport of freight (National Waterways); (4) Industrial & Urban Water Supply: Sustains drinking water and industrial manufacturing.

Causes of River Pollution:
A growing domestic, municipal, industrial, and agricultural demand is affecting the quality of water. Rivers are treated as dumping grounds for: (1) Untreated urban sewage and solid wastes; (2) Highly toxic chemical industrial effluents; (3) Runoff containing synthetic chemical fertilizers and pesticides; (4) Immersion of idols, dead bodies, and religious refuse.

Remedial Measures:
The government initiated the Ganga Action Plan (GAP) in 1985, expanded into the National River Conservation Plan (NRCP). Key initiatives like Namami Gange focus on intercepting and diverting sewage, setting up modern Sewage Treatment Plants (STPs), strict penalties on polluting industries, riverfront development, and massive afforestation along river basins.

Multifaceted economic value of rivers (irrigation, hydro-power, navigation, urban supply)2.0 Marks

Question 4
Describe the role and importance of lakes in the human economy and natural ecosystem.
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Lakes are of immense ecological, aesthetic, and economic value to human civilization:

  1. Regulation of River Flow: During periods of heavy torrential rain, lakes help prevent catastrophic downstream floods. During dry seasons, they maintain an even flow of water in river channels.
  2. Hydel Power Generation: Man-made reservoir lakes created behind river dams (e.g., Guru Gobind Sagar on Bhakra Nangal) are utilized for generating hydroelectric power.
  3. Climate Moderation: Large water bodies moderate the local microclimate of surrounding regions.
  4. Ecological Balance: Lakes sustain diverse aquatic biodiversity, provide sanctuary for migratory birds (e.g., Keoladeo National Park, Chilika Lake), and support inland fishery livelihoods.
  5. Tourism and Recreation: Natural freshwater lakes like Dal Lake, Nainital, and Loktak Lake promote recreation, water sports, and tourism revenue.

Flood mitigation and dry season flow regulation1.5 Marks
Hydel power generation and ecosystem/microclimate moderation1.5 Marks

Question 5
What are the major causes of river water pollution in India? Outline the measures initiated to conserve river systems.
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Causes of River Pollution:

  • Untreated Municipal Sewage: Millions of litres of untreated domestic sewage from towns and cities are discharged directly into rivers, increasing biological oxygen demand (BOD).
  • Industrial Effluents: Chemical, textile, leather, and paper industries dump toxic heavy metals and toxic chemicals without prior treatment.
  • Agricultural Runoff: Excessive use of chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and weedicides leaches into water bodies causing severe eutrophication.
  • Religious and Cultural Practices: Immersion of non-biodegradable idols painted with synthetic chemicals, ashes, and disposal of corpses pollute sacred rivers like Ganga and Yamuna.

Conservation Measures: The government launched the National River Conservation Plan (NRCP) and Namami Gange Mission. Measures include construction of Sewage Treatment Plants (STPs), industrial effluent monitoring, riverfront development, afforestation along river basins, and public awareness campaigns.

Four major causes of river pollution (sewage, industrial, agricultural, cultural)2.0 Marks
Conservation programmes (NRCP, Namami Gange, STPs, effluent treatment)2.0 Marks

Comparative Drainage & Hydro-Geomorphology
Why do the east-flowing Peninsular rivers (Godavari, Mahanadi, Krishna, Kaveri) form large deltas, while the west-flowing rivers (Narmada, Tapi) form estuaries?

East-flowing rivers traverse long distances across gentle, undulating plains of the Peninsular plateau, flowing through softer alluvial rocks and carrying substantial sediment loads. As they reach the calm waters of the Bay of Bengal, their velocity drops sharply, causing extensive sediment deposition that builds wide, fertile triangular deltas (e.g., Sunderbans, Krishna-Godavari deltas).

Conversely, west-flowing rivers like Narmada and Tapi flow through narrow, hard-rock fault/rift valleys between steep mountain escarpments (Vindhyas and Satpuras). They have short courses, steep gradients, fast currents, and carry minimal silt; their rapid flow directly enters the deep waters of the Arabian Sea without depositing sediments, thus carving funnel-shaped estuaries.

Lakes & Ecosystem Services Competency
Examine the ecological and socioeconomic importance of natural and artificial lakes in India.

Lakes are of immense value to human society: (1) Regulating River Flow: During heavy rains, lakes absorb excess water and prevent flash floods; during dry seasons, they release water to maintain minimum river flow; (2) Hydro-electricity: Artificial reservoir lakes (like Guru Gobind Sagar on Bhakra Nangal) generate clean hydroelectric power; (3) Biodiversity & Fisheries: Natural lagoons (Chilika Lake) and freshwater lakes (Wular, Loktak) support migratory birds, aquaculture, and aquatic biodiversity; (4) Tourism & Climate Moderation: Lakes like Dal Lake (Srinagar) and Nainital moderate surrounding microclimates and generate massive tourism revenue.
