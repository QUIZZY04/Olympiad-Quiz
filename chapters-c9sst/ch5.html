History: India and the Contemporary World – I

Pastoralists in the Modern World

India and the Contemporary World – I | Chapter 5 — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

NCERT Textbook Exercises — 100% Question Coverage
Question 1
Explain why nomadic pastoralists need to move continuously from one place to another. What are the benefits of this continuous movement to the environment and the pastoralists?
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Nomadic pastoralists (such as the Gaddi shepherds of Himachal Pradesh, Gujjar Bakarwals of Jammu and Kashmir, and Dhangars of Maharashtra) move continuously to adapt to seasonal variations and pasture availability:

  • Survival and Fodder Needs: In winter, high mountain pastures are covered in snow, forcing pastoralists down to low scrub forests (Shiwaliks). In summer, when lowlands dry up, melting mountain snow yields lush green bugyals (alpine meadows) rich in nutrient grass for livestock.
  • Benefits to the Environment:
    • Pasture Regeneration: Continuous cyclical movement prevents overgrazing and allows vegetation to naturally regenerate and restore soil fertility.
    • Ecological Symbiosis with Settled Farmers: The livestock manure fertilises agricultural fields after harvest, while sheep feed on the stubble, creating a mutually beneficial ecological relationship with settled agriculturalists.
CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Seasonal adaptation to snow and arid dry spells1.5 Marks
Environmental benefit: Prevention of overgrazing and natural pasture recovery1.5 Marks
Manuring of farmland and symbiotic farmer-pastoralist trade1.0 Mark
Question 2
Discuss why the colonial government in India brought in the following laws. In each case, explain how the law changed the lives of pastoralists: (a) Waste Land Rules, (b) Forest Acts, (c) Criminal Tribes Act, (d) Grazing Tax.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The colonial British administration perceived nomadic pastoralism as uncivilized, unproductive, and untaxable. They introduced four major sets of disruptive laws:

  1. Waste Land Rules: The British viewed uncultivated grazing lands as unproductive 'waste'. Under Waste Land Rules, grazing grounds were taken over, enclosed, and given to settled farmers to cultivate grain and augment land revenue. Pastoralists lost vast grazing pastures.
  2. Forest Acts: Classified forests as 'Reserved' (producing commercially valuable timber; pastoral entry strictly banned) and 'Protected' (customary grazing severely curtailed through cumbersome entry permits and time limits). Pastoralists faced constant harassment and extortion from forest guards.
  3. Criminal Tribes Act (1871): British officials distrusted mobile people who had no fixed home. This Act categorized hundreds of nomadic pastoralist and trading communities as 'criminals by birth'. They were forbidden from moving outside assigned settlements without police permits.
  4. Grazing Tax: Introduced in the mid-19th century to extract maximum revenue from livestock. Pastoralists had to pay a tax on every animal they took into pasture, and by the 1880s, the right to collect tax was auctioned to private contractors who extracted extortionate rates.
CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Waste Land Rules: Conversion of pastures to taxable cultivation1.25 Marks
Forest Acts: Division into Reserved/Protected and severe grazing curbs1.25 Marks
Criminal Tribes Act 1871: Stigmatisation and forced confinement of nomads1.25 Marks
Grazing Tax: Per-head animal taxation and harassment by contractors1.25 Marks
Question 3
Give reasons to explain why the Maasai community of East Africa lost their grazing lands.
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The Maasai pastoralists of East Africa lost over 60% of their ancestral grazing lands during the 19th and 20th centuries due to European colonial imperialism:

  1. Colonial Partition of Africa (1885): European powers divided East Africa into colonial territories. In 1885, an international boundary between British Kenya and German Tanganyika cut Maasailand in half. The Maasai were pushed into an arid, water-deficient southern reserve with poor pastures.
  2. Expansion of White Settler Agriculture: The best well-watered pastures were seized by the British and German administrations and handed over to white European settlers for commercial cash-crop farming.
  3. Creation of Game Reserves: Massive grazing areas were converted into national parks and game reserves (like the Maasai Mara and Amboseli National Park in Kenya, and Serengeti Park in Tanzania). Pastoralists were evicted, forbidden from entering, hunting, or grazing their cattle within park borders.
CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Colonial partition of East Africa in 1885 and creation of artificial borders1.5 Marks
Encroachment and appropriation of fertile pastures by white settlers1.5 Marks
Conversion of grazing lands into game reserves (Maasai Mara, Serengeti)1.0 Mark
Question 4
There are many similarities in the way in which the modern world forced changes in the lives of pastoral communities in India and East Africa. Write about any two of these similarities.
3 Marks (50-60 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Pastoral communities in both colonial India and East Africa experienced strikingly parallel crises:

  • Loss of Grazing Grounds through Cultivation Enclosure: In India, the British enacted Waste Land Rules to convert pastures into revenue-generating agricultural fields. Similarly, in East Africa, colonial authorities took away the fertile highland pastures of the Maasai and handed them over to white commercial settlers. Both faced shrinking grazing reserves and declining herd sizes.
  • Drastic Restrictions on Free Mobility: In India, the Forest Acts and the Criminal Tribes Act restricted pastoral movements into forests and compelled them to carry passes. In Africa, the Maasai were confined to arid, demarcated reserves and forbidden from moving across boundaries with their cattle without special permits.
CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown 2026-27
Parallel 1: Seizure and privatization of pastures for settled farming1.5 Marks
Parallel 2: Imposition of permits and restriction of seasonal mobility1.5 Marks

Adaptation & Modern Survival Strategy
How did pastoralist communities in India adapt and survive the challenges posed by colonial laws and post-independence development?

Pastoralists did not passively accept their marginalisation; they adapted through diverse strategies: (1) Reducing Herd Sizes: When pastures shrank, they reduced the number of cattle/sheep to prevent herd starvation; (2) Discovering New Migratory Routes: After the 1947 Partition blocked the Raikas from migrating to Sindh (Pakistan), they changed routes to Haryana and Uttar Pradesh; (3) Diversifying Livelihoods: Many poorer pastoralists took up day labour in agriculture, construction, or transport, while wealthier pastoralists bought land and settled as farmers.

Environmental Sustainability & Policy Evaluation
Why do contemporary environmentalists and scientists now argue that pastoral nomadism is an ecologically sustainable way of life in arid and semi-arid zones?

Modern ecological science recognizes that arid, semi-arid, and high alpine grasslands are ill-suited for intensive settled agriculture because plowing destroys thin topsoils and depletes groundwater. Nomadic pastoralism respects natural climatic limits: seasonal movement prevents overgrazing, allows soil to recuperate, and distributes manure naturally.

In dry zones like the Thar Desert and East African savannas, mobility is the only viable, ecologically sustainable method to produce milk, meat, and wool without causing desertification.
