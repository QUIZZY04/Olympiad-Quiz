Geography: Contemporary India – I

Physical Features of India

Contemporary India – I | Chapter 2 — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

NCERT Textbook Exercises — 100% Question Coverage
Question 1
Answer the following questions briefly:
(i) What is the bhabar?
(ii) Name the three major divisions of the Himalayas from north to south.
(iii) Which plateau lies between the Aravali and the Vindhyan ranges?
(iv) Name the island group of India having coral origin.
4 Marks (1 Mark each)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(i) Bhabar: A narrow belt of about 8 to 16 km width running parallel to the slopes of the Shiwaliks from Indus to Teesta. Rivers descending from the mountains deposit pebbles in this zone. All streams disappear underground in the porous bhabar pebble belt.

(ii) Three major longitudinal divisions of Himalayas:

  1. Great or Inner Himalayas (Himadri): Northernmost, highest continuous range (average height 6,000 m), contains all prominent peaks and perennial snow/glaciers.
  2. Lesser Himalayas (Himachal): Rugged mountain system (height 3,700–4,500 m; width 50 km), famous for Pir Panjal, Dhaula Dhar, and hill stations (Shimla, Kullu, Manali).
  3. Outer Himalayas (Shiwaliks): Outermost range (altitude 900–1,100 m; width 10–50 km), composed of unconsolidated sediments brought down by rivers; contains longitudinal valleys called Duns (Dehradun, Kotlidun, Patlidun).

(iii) Plateau between Aravali and Vindhyan ranges: The Malwa Plateau.

(iv) Coral origin island group: The Lakshadweep Islands.

Three Himalayan divisions: Himadri, Himachal, Shiwaliks with elevations1.0 Mark

Question 2
Distinguish between:
(i) Converging and Diverging Tectonic Plates.
(ii) Bhangar and Khadar.
(iii) Western Ghats and Eastern Ghats.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(i) Converging vs Diverging Plates:

  • Converging Plates: Two tectonic plates move towards each other. They may either collide and crumble (forming fold mountains like the Himalayas) or one plate slides beneath the other (subduction zone), triggering earthquakes and volcanic arcs.
  • Diverging Plates: Two tectonic plates pull apart or move away from each other, creating rift valleys, fault lines, and mid-oceanic ridges (e.g., Mid-Atlantic Ridge) where magma wells up to create new crust.

(ii) Bhangar vs Khadar (Alluvial Soils):

FeatureBhangarKhadar
AgeOld alluvium deposited away from river floodplains.New, young alluvium deposited on active floodplains.
CompositionContains calcareous deposits known as Kankar.Fine-grained, silty, clayey texture without kankar.
FertilityLess fertile; requires intensive fertilisation.Extremely fertile; renewed annually by floods; ideal for intensive agriculture.

(iii) Western Ghats vs Eastern Ghats:

  • Western Ghats: Continuous mountain chain; can be crossed only through passes (Thal, Bhor, Pal Ghats); higher average elevation (900–1,600 m); highest peak is Anaimudi (2,695 m); causes orographic rainfall along the western coast.
  • Eastern Ghats: Discontinuous, irregular mountain chain dissected by major east-flowing rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Mahanadi, Kaveri) draining into the Bay of Bengal; lower average elevation (600 m); highest peak is Mahendragiri (1,501 m).

Plate tectonics: Converging (collision/fold) vs Diverging (rift/ridge)1.5 Marks
Alluvium contrast: Bhangar (old, kankar, less fertile) vs Khadar (new, fine, fertile)1.5 Marks
Western vs Eastern Ghats: Continuity, elevation (1600m vs 600m), highest peaks (Anaimudi vs Mahendragiri)2.0 Marks
Question 3
Describe how the Himalayas were formed based on the Theory of Plate Tectonics.
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The formation of the Himalayas is explained by the Theory of Plate Tectonics over millions of years:

  1. Millions of years ago, the Earth's crust consisted of the northern landmass Angaraland (Laurasia) and the southern landmass Gondwanaland, separated by a long, shallow geosyncline sea called the Tethys Sea.
  2. Convection currents broke Gondwanaland into pieces. The Indo-Australian Plate drifted northward towards the much larger Eurasian Plate.
  3. Due to this northward collision, the sedimentary rocks accumulated in the geosyncline of the Tethys were compressed, buckled, and folded upwards.
  4. This massive folding uplifted the ocean floor into the majestic young fold mountain system known as the Himalayas. A depression formed to the south, which was later filled with alluvium by Himalayan and peninsular rivers, forming the fertile Northern Plains.

Concept of Angaraland, Gondwanaland, and the Tethys Sea1.0 Mark
Northward drift of Indo-Australian plate towards Eurasian plate1.5 Marks
Compression and folding of Tethys marine sediments into fold mountains1.0 Mark
Resultant formation of the Himalayan arc and Northern Plain depression0.5 Mark
Question 4
Which are the major physiographic divisions of India? Contrast the relief of the Himalayan region with that of the Peninsular Plateau.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The physical features of India are grouped into six major physiographic divisions: (1) The Himalayan Mountains, (2) The Northern Plains, (3) The Peninsular Plateau, (4) The Indian Desert, (5) The Coastal Plains, and (6) The Islands.

Contrasting the Himalayan Region and the Peninsular Plateau:

ParameterHimalayan RegionPeninsular Plateau
Geological Age & NatureGeologically young, unstable, and tectonic fold mountains.Oldest, most stable landmass, composed of ancient crystalline, igneous, and metamorphic rocks.
Topography & ReliefExtremely rugged topography with towering peaks, deep V-shaped valleys, and fast-flowing perennial rivers.Gently undulating relief with shallow, broad valleys, flat-topped tablelands, and rounded hills.
River SystemsPerennial rivers fed by glaciers and rainfall (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra); youthful erosional activity.Seasonal, rain-fed rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Narmada); mature drainage reaching base level of erosion.

Question 5
Give an account of the Northern Plains of India, detailing their formation, relief features (Bhabar, Terai, Bhangar, Khadar), and economic significance.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The Northern Plains of India have been formed by the interplay of the three major Himalayan river systems—the Indus, the Ganga, and the Brahmaputra along with their tributaries:

  1. Formation & Dimensions: The plains were formed over millions of years by the continuous deposition of alluvium in a vast geological basin (foredeep) lying at the foothills of the rising Himalayas. It spreads over an area of 7 lakh sq. km, with a length of about 2400 km and a breadth of 240 to 320 km.
  2. Relief / Morphological Features:
    • Bhabar: A narrow belt of 8 to 16 km width parallel to the slope of the Shiwaliks, consisting of pebble deposits where streams disappear underground.
    • Terai: South of Bhabar, where streams re-emerge creating a wet, swampy, marshy region with dense forests and rich wildlife (e.g., Dudhwa National Park).
    • Bhangar: The largest part of the plains formed of older alluvium, lying above the floodplains with terrace-like features and calcareous deposits (kankar).
    • Khadar: Newer, younger alluvium deposited on the flood plains renewed almost every year, making it exceedingly fertile for intensive cultivation.
  3. Economic Significance: With a rich soil cover, abundant water supply, and favourable climate, it is the agriculturally most productive and densely populated region (granary of India).

River origin and formation in foredeep (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra)1.5 Marks
Accurate distinction among Bhabar, Terai, Bhangar, Khadar2.5 Marks
Economic significance as granary of India and population core1.0 Mark
Question 6
Write short explanatory notes on: (a) The Central Highlands, (b) The Island groups of India.
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(a) The Central Highlands: The part of the Peninsular Plateau lying to the north of the Narmada River, covering a major area of the Malwa Plateau, is known as the Central Highlands. It is bounded by the Vindhyan range on the south and the Aravallis on the northwest. The rivers draining this region—Chambal, Sind, Betwa, and Ken—flow from southwest to northeast. Its eastward extensions are locally known as Bundelkhand and Baghelkhand, while the Chota Nagpur Plateau marks the further eastward extension rich in minerals.

(b) The Island Groups of India:

  • Lakshadweep Islands: Located close to the Malabar coast of Kerala in the Arabian Sea. It consists of small coral islands covering an area of barely 32 sq. km with Kavaratti as administrative headquarters. Pitti Island has an uninhabited bird sanctuary.
  • Andaman and Nicobar Islands: An elongated chain of islands located in the Bay of Bengal, running from north to south. They are bigger, more numerous, and represent elevated portions of submarine mountains. They lie close to the equator, experience equatorial climate, and have thick forest cover. Barren Island houses India's only active volcano.

Physiographic Interdependence & Economic Value
Demonstrate how each physiographic division of India complements the other and makes the country rich in its natural resources.

India's diverse physical features form an integrated, interdependent economic ecosystem: (1) The Himalayas are major sources of water and forest wealth, feeding perennial rivers; (2) The Northern Plains are the granaries of the nation, providing agricultural surplus; (3) The Peninsular Plateau is a storehouse of metallic and non-metallic minerals (coal, iron ore, bauxite) driving heavy industrialization; (4) The Coastal Plains and Islands provide vital sites for maritime trade, fishing, tourism, and ports. This complementary diversity provides India with comprehensive economic self-sufficiency.

Geological Hazard & Ecological Fragility
Why is the Shiwalik range prone to frequent landslides and flash floods during the monsoon season?

The Shiwalik range is the outermost and youngest Himalayan range, composed of unconsolidated sedimentary debris, sand, silt, and gravel brought down by rivers from the higher ranges. These sediments have not yet solidified into hard rock. Furthermore, the region is tectonically active. When torrential monsoon rainfall saturates these loose, steep slopes, deforestation and construction of roads trigger severe slope failures, catastrophic landslides, and mudflows that block river courses.
