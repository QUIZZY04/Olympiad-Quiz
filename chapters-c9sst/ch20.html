Economics: Understanding Economic Development – I

Food Security in India

Economics | Chapter 4 — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

NCERT Textbook Exercises — 100% Question Coverage
Question 1
How is food security ensured in India? Explain the three essential dimensions of food security.
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Food security means something more than getting two square meals a day; it encompasses three vital dimensions:

  1. Availability of Food: Total food production in the country plus food imports and the previous years' stocks stored in government granaries (FCI).
  2. Accessibility of Food: Food is within reach of every person, without physical or social barriers.
  3. Affordability of Food: An individual has sufficient financial resources to buy enough, nutritious, and safe food to meet dietary needs.

In India, food security is ensured through a carefully designed two-component food security system: (1) Buffer Stock maintained by the Food Corporation of India (FCI), and (2) The Public Distribution System (PDS) distributing subsidized food to poor families through ration shops.

Question 2
Write short explanatory notes on: (a) Buffer Stock, (b) Minimum Support Price (MSP), (c) Issue Price, (d) Fair Price Shops.
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(a) Buffer Stock: The stock of food grains (wheat and rice) procured by the government through the Food Corporation of India (FCI). It is created to distribute food grains in deficit areas and among poorer strata at subsidized prices, and to resolve food shortages during calamities or adverse weather.

(b) Minimum Support Price (MSP): The pre-announced price at which the FCI purchases wheat and rice directly from farmers who have surplus production. Announced by the government before each sowing season to provide price assurances and incentivize farmers to invest in crop production.

(c) Issue Price: The subsidized price (substantially lower than the open market price) at which the government releases food grains from its buffer stock to vulnerable citizens through ration shops.

(d) Fair Price Shops (Ration Shops): Government-licensed neighborhood retail outlets (around 5.5 lakh across India) that sell subsidized food grains (wheat, rice), sugar, and kerosene oil to ration cardholders.

Question 3
What is the role of cooperatives in providing food security in India? Give examples.
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Cooperative societies play a monumental role in ensuring food security, particularly in the southern and western regions of India:

  • Running Fair Price Shops: In Tamil Nadu, approximately 94% of all fair price shops are operated by cooperatives, ensuring equitable distribution without private dealer corruption.
  • The White Revolution (AMUL): The Gujarat Cooperative Milk Marketing Federation, through Amul, organized millions of small milk producers, revolutionizing milk production and making milk products affordable nationwide.
  • Subsidized Consumer Milk & Vegetables: Mother Dairy in Delhi provides controlled, subsidized milk and vegetables to consumers.
  • Grain Banks for Tribals (ADS): In Maharashtra, the Academy of Development Science (ADS) facilitated an extensive network of NGO Grain Banks, providing food security to remote tribal communities during monsoon hunger months.

Question 4
What is the Public Distribution System (PDS)? Discuss its critical role in ensuring food security, its key limitations, and recent reforms.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The Public Distribution System (PDS) is an essential instrument of government food security policy in India:

  1. Mechanism & Role: Food procured by the Food Corporation of India (FCI) at Minimum Support Price (MSP) is distributed through a network of around 5.5 lakh Fair Price Shops (ration shops) to poorer sections of society at issue prices lower than market prices. It distributes foodgrains, sugar, and kerosene oil.
  2. Role in Food Security: PDS has been instrumental in averting widespread famines, stabilising consumer prices, providing price incentives to farmers, and ensuring food entitlement to vulnerable low-income households.
  3. Major Limitations & Criticisms:
    • High level of diversion and leakage of subsidized grains to open markets by corrupt ration shop owners.
    • Irregular opening of shops and stocking of substandard, poor quality grain.
    • Overflowing FCI godowns where grain rots or is eaten by rats, while millions remain undernourished.
    • High cost of carrying buffer stocks and fiscal burden of food subsidies.
  4. Reforms: Introduction of Targeted PDS (TPDS) with differential pricing for APL, BPL, and AAY; digitisation of ration cards, biometric authentication using Aadhaar, and the One Nation, One Ration Card (ONORC) scheme allowing migrant labourers to draw rations anywhere in India.

Famine Analysis & Historic Memory
Describe the catastrophic Famine of Bengal in 1943. What lessons does it offer for modern food security governance?

The Famine of Bengal in 1943 was the most devastating famine recorded in Indian history, in which over 30 lakh (3 million) people starved to death in Bengal province. The most affected were agricultural labourers, fishermen, transport workers, and casual labourers whose real wages plummeted while the price of rice skyrocketed due to wartime hoarding, British military diversions, and disruption of Burmese rice imports.

It taught independent India that food availability must be accompanied by state intervention: the government must maintain buffer stocks, regulate hoarding, and guarantee that purchasing power and access exist for the poorest citizens even during wartime or natural disasters.

Right to Food & Legal Framework
How did the National Food Security Act (NFSA) 2013 revolutionize food security into a legal entitlement in India?

Passed in 2013, the National Food Security Act (NFSA) marked a paradigm shift from a welfare approach to a rights-based legal framework. Key provisions include: (1) Covers up to 75% of the rural population and 50% of the urban population (nearly two-thirds of India's population); (2) Entitles eligible priority households to receive 5 kg of food grains per person per month at highly subsidized prices (₹3/kg rice, ₹2/kg wheat); (3) Guarantees Antyodaya households 35 kg per family per month; (4) Provides pregnant women and lactating mothers maternity benefit of not less than ₹6,000, legally embedding food and nutritional security into the Right to Life.
