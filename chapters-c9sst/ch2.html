Socialism in Europe and the Russian Revolution

India and the Contemporary World – I | Chapter 2 — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

NCERT Textbook Exercises — 100% Question Coverage
Question 1
What were the social, economic and political conditions in Russia before 1905?
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Before 1905, the Russian Empire ruled by Tsar Nicholas II was characterized by deep feudal backwardness, extreme autocratic repression, and sharp socio-economic disparities:

  1. Autocratic Political System: Russia was an absolute autocracy. The Tsar ruled without any constitutional limits or elected parliament. Political parties were illegal, censorship was brutal, and the secret police (Okhrana) ruthlessly suppressed dissent.
  2. Agrarian Dominance and Peasant Exploitation: Nearly 85% of the Russian population depended on agriculture (far higher than France or Germany's 40-50%). Most fertile land was owned by the crown, nobility, and Orthodox Church. Peasants cultivated small, fragmented plots, paid heavy redemption dues, and were subject to corporal punishment by landlords.
  3. Rapid, Unequal Industrialisation: Industrial pockets developed around St. Petersburg and Moscow, heavily financed by foreign capital. Workers endured appalling working conditions—12 to 15-hour workdays, miserable wages, lack of sanitation, and hazardous dormitories. Trade unions were banned, leading to frequent underground strikes.
  4. Unique Social Fabric (The Mir/Commune): Russian peasants pooled their land periodically and redistributed it according to family needs (Mir), making them naturally predisposed to collectivism.
  5. Russo-Japanese War (1904-05) Crisis: Russia's humiliating defeat by Japan exposed military corruption, triggered runaway inflation, and escalated food shortages, culminating in the 1905 Bloody Sunday massacre.

Question 2
In what ways was the working population in Russia different from other countries in Europe, before 1917?
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The working population in Russia differed significantly from Western European working classes in three fundamental aspects:

  1. Agrarian Working Population: In Western Europe (Britain, France, Germany), industrialisation had turned the majority into urban industrial proletarians. In Russia, 85% of people remained peasants. Russian peasants had no traditional respect for the nobility; they frequently refused to pay rent, seized estates, and murdered landlords.
  2. Internal Divisions within Industrial Workers: Russian factory workers were divided by village origins and occupational skill. Metalworkers considered themselves the 'aristocrats' among workers because their work required more training. Women made up 31% of the factory labour force by 1914, but were paid between half and three-quarters of a man's wage.
  3. Unmatched Solidarity and Collective Action: Despite internal divisions, Russian workers exhibited unprecedented class unity during strikes. They united with revolutionary socialist parties (Bolsheviks and Mensheviks) much more rapidly and militantly than workers in Western parliamentary democracies.

Question 3
Why did the Tsarist autocracy collapse in 1917?
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The collapse of Tsarist autocracy in the February Revolution of 1917 was caused by the catastrophic military, economic, and political collapse engineered by the First World War:

  1. Disastrous Casualties in World War I: Russia's poorly equipped armies suffered humiliating defeats against Germany and Austria. By 1917, over 7 million Russian soldiers were casualties, and retreating armies destroyed crops and buildings, creating 3 million refugees. The army lost all will to fight for the Tsar.
  2. Economic Breakdown and Bread Riots: Able-bodied men were conscripted into the war, causing severe farm labour shortages and widespread food scarcity. Railway lines broke down under military transport. By winter 1916-17, bread and flour shortages in Petrograd triggered daily bread lines and consumer riots.
  3. Tsar's Incompetence and Rasputin's Influence: Tsar Nicholas II dissolved the Duma repeatedly and refused to cooperate with moderate politicians. The Tsar's German wife, Tsarina Alexandra, and her corrupt, scandalous adviser, the monk Rasputin, completely discredited the royal court.
  4. The Petrograd Mutiny (February 1917): On 23 February 1917, women led factory strikes in Petrograd. When the Tsar ordered the army to fire on demonstrators, the soldiers mutinied, joined the strikers, and formed the Petrograd Soviet. Abandoned by his generals, Nicholas II abdicated on 2 March 1917, ending three centuries of Romanov rule.

Question 4
Compare the February Revolution and October Revolution of 1917 in terms of events, leaders, and impact.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The Russian Revolution of 1917 unfolded in two distinct, interconnected phases:

Feature February Revolution (1917) October Revolution (1917)
Nature & Events Spontaneous mass uprising in Petrograd triggered by food shortages, strikes, and army mutinies. Carefully planned armed Bolshevik insurrection seizing government buildings, telecom, and the Winter Palace.
Key Leaders No single leader; initiated by women workers, soldiers' committees, Duma leaders (Kerensky). Vladimir Lenin and Leon Trotsky (head of the Military Revolutionary Committee).
Outcome & Impact Overthrew Tsar Nicholas II; established a Provisional Government; Russia became a republic. Overthrew Provisional Government; established world's first socialist state under Bolshevik rule.

Question 5
What were the main changes brought about by the Bolsheviks immediately after the October Revolution?
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Immediately after seizing power in October 1917, the Bolshevik government led by Lenin implemented radical socialist transformations:

  1. Nationalisation of Banks and Industries: All private banks and major industrial units were nationalised in November 1917; ownership and management passed to the workers' state.
  2. Abolition of Landlordism: Land was declared social property, and peasants were legally permitted to seize and redistribute the estates of the nobility and the church.
  3. End to World War I: In March 1918, the Bolsheviks signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk with Germany, pulling Russia out of the war despite humiliating territorial concessions.
  4. Housing and Social Equality: Large urban mansions were partitioned based on family size. Old aristocratic titles of nobility were banned, and egalitarian uniforms/caps (Budeonovka) were introduced.
  5. Creation of a One-Party State: The Bolshevik Party was renamed the Russian Communist Party (Bolshevik); opposition was suppressed by the secret police (Cheka).

Question 6
Write short explanatory notes on: (a) Kulaks, (b) The Duma, (c) Women workers between 1900 and 1930, (d) Stalin’s collectivisation programme.
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(a) Kulaks: The Russian term for well-to-do, prosperous peasants who owned substantial land, hired farm labourers, and hoarded grain. Under Stalin's collectivisation in 1928, Kulaks were demonised, raided, their lands confiscated, and thousands were deported or executed to modernise agriculture.

(b) The Duma: An elected consultative parliament established by Tsar Nicholas II following the 1905 Revolution. However, the Tsar repeatedly dismissed successive Dumas (1st Duma in 75 days) and packed the 3rd Duma with conservative aristocrats, rendering it politically impotent.

(c) Women Workers (1900–1930): Women made up 31% of the factory workforce by 1914 but suffered acute wage disparity (paid 1/2 to 3/4 of men's wages). On 23 February 1917, women textile workers bravely led strikes that ignited the February Revolution (now celebrated as International Women's Day). Under Soviets, factory crèches, public dining halls, and maternity benefits were introduced.

(d) Stalin’s Collectivisation (Kolkhoz): Initiated in 1929 to eliminate grain shortages by ending peasant individual farming. Peasant lands were merged into large collective farms (Kolkhoz). Peasants who resisted were brutally punished. In response, peasants slaughtered 1/3 of cattle. Coupled with bad harvests, it caused the catastrophic famine of 1930-1933, claiming over 4 million lives.

Source-Based Analysis Question
Analyse the significance of Vladimir Lenin's 'April Theses' (1917) in shaping the trajectory of the Russian Revolution.

Upon returning from exile in Switzerland in April 1917, Vladimir Lenin issued three decisive demands known as the April Theses: (1) An immediate end to World War I; (2) Transfer of all land to the peasantry; and (3) Nationalisation of all banks. He also advocated transferring 'All Power to the Soviets'.

These demands resonated with the war-weary soldiers, starving urban proletariat, and land-hungry peasants, completely isolating the moderate Provisional Government and paving the ideological highway for the Bolshevik insurrection in October 1917.

Competency & Global Impact Question
How did the Russian Revolution exert a worldwide influence on the idea of socialism and anti-colonial movements?

The Russian Revolution demonstrated for the first time that a socialist state founded on the ideals of Karl Marx could be established by working people without monarchies or private capitalists. In many countries, communist parties were formed (e.g., Communist Party of Great Britain, CPI in India). The Bolsheviks founded Comintern (an international union of pro-Bolshevik socialist parties) and supported anti-colonial national liberation struggles across Asia and Africa.

However, by the mid-20th century, international observers acknowledged that while the USSR eradicated mass illiteracy and transformed into an industrial superpower, it did so by denying democratic freedoms and human rights.
