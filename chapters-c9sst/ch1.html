Question 1
Describe the circumstances leading to the outbreak of revolutionary protest in France.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The outbreak of the French Revolution on 14 July 1789 was the cumulative outcome of severe structural crises spanning economic, social, political, and intellectual spheres under the Ancien Régime:

  1. Social Inequality and Privilege System: French society was divided into Three Estates. The First Estate (Clergy) and Second Estate (Nobility), comprising barely 2% of the population, owned over 60% of land and enjoyed complete exemption from state taxes. The entire tax burden (taille, tithes, indirect taxes on salt and tobacco) fell solely on the Third Estate (peasants, artisans, merchants, professionals).
  2. Economic Bankruptcy and Financial Crisis: Years of war, including France's involvement in the American War of Independence (which added over a billion livres of debt), coupled with the extravagant lifestyle of Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette at Versailles, drained the royal treasury. More than half the state budget was consumed by interest payments.
  3. Subsistence Crisis: Rapid population growth (from 23 million in 1715 to 28 million in 1789) escalated food demand. Severe droughts and hailstorms destroyed harvests, leading to soaring bread prices, hoarding by bakers, and widespread starvation riots.
  4. Influence of Enlightenment Philosophers: Thinkers like John Locke (refuted divine right of kings), Jean-Jacques Rousseau (proposed government based on a social contract), and Montesquieu (advocated division of powers among legislative, executive, and judiciary) inspired the educated bourgeoisie.
  5. The Spark — Convocation of Estates General: On 5 May 1789, Louis XVI convened the Estates General to pass new taxes. The Third Estate demanded voting per head instead of voting per estate. When the King rejected this, representatives walked out, declared themselves the National Assembly on the Tennis Court (20 June 1789), and stormed the Bastille on 14 July 1789.

Question 2
Which groups of French society benefited from the revolution? Which groups were forced to relinquish power? Which sections of society would have been disappointed with the outcome of the revolution?
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The French Revolution radically reordered the socio-political hierarchy of France:

  • Groups that Benefited: The middle class (Bourgeoisie)—lawyers, merchants, traders, educated professionals, and prosperous peasants—benefited the most. They gained political representation, security of private property, abolition of feudal dues, and equal access to public offices. Peasants were freed from oppressive seigneurial dues and tithes.
  • Groups Forced to Relinquish Power: The Aristocracy (Nobility) and the Catholic Clergy lost their feudal monopolies, privileges by birth, tax exemptions, and vast estates, which were nationalised and auctioned. Feudal courts and tithes were permanently abolished.
  • Groups Disappointed with the Outcome:
    • Women: Despite active revolutionary participation, women were relegated to 'passive citizens' without voting or political rights.
    • Poorer working-class men (San-culottes): Landless peasants, day-labourers, and servants remained disenfranchised under the 1791 Constitution because voting was restricted to 'active citizens' who paid property taxes. The brief Jacobin era gave them rights, but the subsequent Directory took them away.

Question 3
Describe the legacy of the French Revolution for the peoples of the world during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.
5 Marks (100-120 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The French Revolution was a monumental turning point in world history whose legacy reshaped modern governance, political ideology, and liberation movements across the globe:

  1. Ideals of Liberty, Equality, and Fraternity: The motto 'Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité' became the foundational bedrock of all modern democratic systems. It dismantled the feudal dogma of divine kingship and entrenched the doctrine of popular sovereignty.
  2. Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen: It universalised fundamental human rights—freedom of speech, expression, association, equality before law, religious tolerance, and presumption of innocence. These principles were subsequently embedded into the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948).
  3. Abolition of Feudalism and Serfdom: The revolutionary decrees demolished feudal privileges across Western Europe as French revolutionary armies spread ideas of national self-determination across Germany, Italy, and Poland.
  4. Rise of Modern Nationalism: The concept of a sovereign nation defined by united citizens with a common flag and national anthem (La Marseillaise) rather than loyalty to a dynasty became the template for European nation-states in the 19th century.
  5. Inspiration for Anti-Colonial Struggles: Colonised peoples in Asia, Africa, and Latin America derived philosophical inspiration from the French Revolution. In India, pioneer reformers like Raja Rammohan Roy and sovereign rulers like Tipu Sultan embraced its ideals to challenge foreign subjugation and champion liberty.

Question 4
Draw up a list of democratic rights we enjoy today whose origins could be traced to the French Revolution.
3 Marks (50-60 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Several fundamental democratic rights enshrined in contemporary constitutions, including the Constitution of India (Part III), trace their institutional origin to the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789):

  1. Right to Equality: Equality before the law and equal protection of laws (Article 14).
  2. Freedom of Speech and Expression: Freedom of speech, press, and communication (Article 19).
  3. Right to Personal Liberty: Protection of life and personal freedom; no arrest without due process of law (Article 21).
  4. Freedom of Religion and Conscience: Freedom to practice and propagate one's faith without state persecution (Articles 25-28).
  5. Right to Property and Fair Taxation: Security against arbitrary confiscation of property and taxation only through representative consent.

Question 5
Would you agree with the view that the message of universal rights was beset with contradictions? Explain.
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Yes, despite its noble rhetoric of universality, the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen suffered from deep, inherent historical contradictions:

  • Exclusion of Women: The rights were framed strictly as the rights of 'Man' (male citizens). Women, who actively marched to Versailles and ran political clubs, were denied the vote and designated as 'passive citizens'. When feminist leader Olympe de Gouges drafted the Declaration of the Rights of Woman and Citizen (1791), she was executed during the Reign of Terror.
  • Wealth-Based Franchise: The Constitution of 1791 created a discriminatory division between 'active' and 'passive' citizens. Only men over 25 years paying taxes equal to at least 3 days of a labourer's wage could vote, denying political agency to the poor and working classes.
  • Continuation of Colonial Slavery: Although the National Convention briefly abolished slavery in French colonies in 1794, Napoleon Bonaparte reinstated it in 1802 to satisfy wealthy plantation owners. Slavery in French colonies was not finally abolished until 1848.

Question 6
How would you explain the rise of Napoleon Bonaparte?
4 Marks (60-80 words)
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The rise of Napoleon Bonaparte can be attributed to the political instability, social chaos, and military vulnerabilities of post-revolutionary France:

  1. Instability of the Directory: After the fall of Robespierre and the Jacobin regime (1794), political power was transferred to the Directory (an executive body of five members). Frequent clashes between the Directors and legislative councils created chronic political paralysis and widespread corruption.
  2. Military Genius and Popular Hero: Napoleon earned immense prestige as a brilliant artillery general who successfully crushed internal counter-revolutionary rebellions and achieved sensational victories against Austrian and Italian coalitions.
  3. Desire for Order and Stability: The French populace, exhausted by a decade of terror, civil unrest, inflation, and political factionalism, longed for a decisive leader capable of establishing law, order, and national pride.
  4. Coup of 18 Brumaire (1799): Exploiting this leadership vacuum, Napoleon staged a military coup in 1799, overthrew the Directory, declared himself First Consul, and in 1804 crowned himself Emperor of France. He modernized administration through the Napoleonic Code (1804), uniform weights/measures, and the protection of private property.

Source & Competency-Based Question
Explain the significance of the Storming of the Bastille on 14 July 1789. Why did the fortress evoke such hatred among Parisians?

The fortress-prison of the Bastille stood as the supreme symbol of the despotic and arbitrary tyranny of the Bourbon monarchy. It housed state prisoners arrested without trial and held stockpiles of ammunition. On the morning of 14 July 1789, an enraged Parisian crowd stormed the fortress, freed the prisoners, and demolished its stone walls.

Its destruction marked the psychological collapse of royal absolutism, demonstrated the power of the armed common citizenry, and ignited the nationwide popular revolution. Today, 14 July is celebrated as France's National Day (Bastille Day).

Assertion-Reason & Value-Based Question
Evaluate the measures taken by Maximilien Robespierre's government (1793-1794). Why is this period known as the 'Reign of Terror'?

Robespierre followed a policy of severe control and draconian punishment. The Revolutionary Tribunal executed nearly 17,000 citizens deemed 'enemies of the republic' by the guillotine. However, his regime also introduced radical egalitarian measures: maximum ceilings on wages and prices, rationing of meat and bread, the mandatory 'equality bread' (pain d'égalité), the abolition of slavery in colonies, and addressing citizens as 'Citoyen/Citoyenne' instead of Sir/Madam.

It is called the 'Reign of Terror' because state coercion eliminated dissent, civil liberties were crushed, and paranoia ruled until Robespierre himself was guillotined in Thermidor (July 1794).
