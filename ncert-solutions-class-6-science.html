Chapter 1: The Wonderful World of Science

NCERT Curiosity (Class 6) — Nature of Scientific Inquiry, Observation, Asking Questions, Hypothesis, Experimentation & Laboratory Safety | CBSE 2026-27

📌 Key Scientific Concepts & Principles
  • Nature of Science: Science is an organized, systematic way of exploring, understanding, and explaining natural phenomena around us through careful observation, experimentation, and logical reasoning.
  • Steps of Scientific Method:
    • Observation: Noticing an interesting pattern or occurrence using our senses or measuring tools.
    • Formulating a Question: Asking "Why?", "How?", or "What happens if...?" regarding the observation.
    • Hypothesis: Proposing a tentative, testable explanation based on prior knowledge.
    • Experimentation: Designing controlled trials with variables to test the hypothesis.
    • Data Collection & Analysis: Recording measurements accurately in tables or graphs.
    • Conclusion: Deciding whether the evidence supports or refutes the original hypothesis.
  • Branches of Science: Biology (study of living organisms), Chemistry (study of substances and their transformations), Physics (study of matter, energy, and forces), and Earth & Space Science (study of our planet and the universe).
  • Laboratory Safety & Ethics: Always wear safety goggles and lab apron, handle glassware gently, avoid smelling or tasting unknown chemicals directly, and dispose of wastes in designated containers.

NCERT Exercise 1.1: Foundations of Scientific Inquiry
Q1
What is science? Explain why curiosity and observation are considered the twin pillars of scientific discovery with suitable examples.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Definition of Science:
Science is the systematic pursuit and application of knowledge and understanding of the natural and physical world following a verifiable methodology based on evidence.
Role of Curiosity and Observation:
  • Curiosity (The Starting Spark): Curiosity is the innate desire to learn and understand. It drives a person to question why the sky is blue, how birds fly, or why milk curdles. Without curiosity, no scientific questions would ever be formulated.
  • Observation (The Evidence Collector): Observation involves carefully watching, listening, measuring, and noting down details of objects and events using our senses and scientific instruments.
  • Historical Example: Sir Isaac Newton observed an apple falling from a tree to the ground. Driven by curiosity regarding why the fruit fell straight down rather than moving sideways or upwards, he formulated the Universal Law of Gravitation.

Q2
List the sequential steps of the scientific method. Why is a controlled experiment essential in testing a hypothesis?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Sequential Steps of the Scientific Method:
1. Making an accurate Observation.
2. Raising a well-defined Question.
3. Formulating an educated, testable Hypothesis.
4. Designing and executing a Controlled Experiment.
5. Recording and analyzing Data.
6. Drawing a logical Conclusion and communicating findings.
Importance of a Controlled Experiment:
In a controlled experiment, only one factor (the independent variable) is altered at a time while all other conditions are kept strictly constant. This ensures that any observed result is directly caused by the tested variable, eliminating accidental influences and bias.

Q3
State four important laboratory safety rules that every science student must follow while conducting experiments.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Key Safety Rules:
1. Wear Protective Gear: Always wear safety goggles, cotton lab coats, and closed shoes to protect eyes and skin from chemical spills.

Q4
Differentiate between qualitative and quantitative observations with two examples of each. Which type of observation is preferred in scientific experiments, and why?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
1. Qualitative Observation:
Observations described using words, characteristics, or sensory qualities without numerical measurements.
Examples: "The solution turned dark blue", "The leaf has a rough surface."
2. Quantitative Observation:
Observations described using measurable numbers, quantities, and standard units.
Examples: "The liquid volume is 250 mL", "The temperature increased by 5 °C."
Scientific Preference:
Quantitative observations are strongly preferred in scientific research because they are objective, exact, reproducible by other scientists worldwide, and can be statistically verified without personal interpretation bias.

CBSE Competency-Based Question (Inquiry & Real-Life Case Study)
Case Study: Testing Seed Germination Conditions:
Aarav noticed that gram (chana) seeds kept in a dry container in the kitchen did not sprout, while seeds soaked in moist cloth germinated within two days. He decided to investigate the exact conditions needed for seed germination by setting up four test cups:
• Cup A: Dry seeds exposed to room temperature and sunlight.
• Cup B: Seeds completely submerged in water with oil layer on top (no air) at room temperature.
• Cup C: Seeds kept on moist cotton with access to air at room temperature.
• Cup D: Seeds kept on moist cotton placed inside a refrigerator at 4 °C.

(a) In which cup will the seeds successfully germinate? What was Aarav's hypothesis?
(b) What was the purpose of Cup B with the oil layer?
(c) Identify the independent variable and the controlled factors in this experiment.
Detailed Analytical Solution & Marking Scheme

(a) Successful Germination & Hypothesis:
Seeds in Cup C will successfully germinate. Aarav's hypothesis was: "Seeds require both moisture (water), oxygen (air), and suitable temperature to break dormancy and germinate."

(b) Purpose of Cup B:
The oil layer forms an impermeable barrier that prevents atmospheric oxygen (air) from dissolving into the water. This cup tests whether water alone is sufficient for germination in the absence of air.

(c) Variables Identified:
• Independent Variable: Environmental growth conditions (Moisture, Air, and Temperature availability).
• Controlled Factors (Constants): Same type and count of gram seeds, same type of container, and identical testing duration.

Chapter 2: Diversity in the Living World

NCERT Curiosity (Class 6) — Plant Diversity, Roots & Venation, Animal Habitats, Adaptations & Biodiversity Conservation | CBSE 2026-27

📌 Key Biological Concepts & Organism Classification
  • Biodiversity: The vast variety of plants, animals, and microorganisms living across various terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems on Earth.
  • Plant Classification by Stature:
    • Herbs: Small plants with green, soft, and tender stems (e.g., Tomato, Mint, Wheat).
    • Shrubs: Medium-sized plants with a hard, woody stem branching out near ground level (e.g., Rose, Lemon).
    • Trees: Tall plants with a thick, hard, brown woody trunk branching high above ground (e.g., Mango, Neem, Banyan).
    • Creepers & Climbers: Weak-stemmed plants that trail on soil (Pumpkin) or climb up supports using tendrils (Money plant).
  • Leaf Venation & Root Architecture:
    • Reticulate Venation (Net-like): Associated with a Taproot System with a single thick primary root and lateral secondary roots (e.g., Gram, Mustard, Radish, Kidney Bean).
    • Parallel Venation (Linear): Associated with a Fibrous Root System with a cluster of slender roots originating from stem base (e.g., Wheat, Grass, Maize, Bamboo).
  • Animal Classification: Grouping animals by habitat (Aquatic, Terrestrial, Amphibious), reproduction (Oviparous egg-layers vs Viviparous live-bearers), and locomotion methods.

Q1
Here are two types of seeds: Wheat and Kidney bean. What differences do you find among the roots and leaf venation of their plants?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Plant / Seed Type Type of Seed Leaf Venation Pattern Root Architecture
Wheat Plant Monocotyledon (Monocot): Seed consists of a single cotyledon. Parallel Venation: Veins run parallel and straight alongside each other from base to leaf tip. Fibrous Root System: A dense cluster of slender, thread-like roots of nearly equal thickness emerging from the base of the stem.

Reticulate Venation: Veins branch out repeatedly, forming a complex net-like or webbed pattern over the lamina. Biological Principle: Seeds with one cotyledon (monocots) always develop parallel leaf venation and fibrous roots, whereas seeds with two cotyledons (dicots) develop reticulate leaf venation and a taproot system.

Q3
Examine a radish plant. Identify its root type and leaf venation. Can you predict the type of root of a plant just by observing its leaves without pulling it out?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
1. Radish Observations:
• Root System: The edible radish is a modified, swollen Taproot (fusiform taproot) that stores food, with fine lateral root hairs emerging from its sides.
• Leaf Venation: The leaves of a radish plant exhibit prominent Reticulate Venation with an intricate net-like web of veins.
2. Prediction Without Digging:
Yes, absolutely. There is a direct, reliable biological correlation in flowering plants:
• If a plant's leaves have Reticulate Venation, it will invariably possess a Taproot System.
• If a plant's leaves have Parallel Venation, it will possess a Fibrous Root System.
Therefore, by simply observing the net-like venation of radish leaves, one can confidently deduce that its root is a taproot without uprooting it.

Q6
Discuss how large-scale deforestation affects our surroundings and the living world. Suggest three practical ways to address this environmental challenge.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
1. Adverse Effects of Deforestation:
• Loss of Biodiversity & Wildlife Habitat: Forests provide shelter and nourishment to thousands of species. Cutting trees renders animals homeless, leading to human-animal conflicts and species extinction.
• Soil Erosion & Flash Floods: Tree roots anchor the topsoil firmly. In their absence, torrential rains wash away fertile topsoil, silting rivers and causing floods.
• Climate Change & Global Warming: Trees absorb carbon dioxide during photosynthesis. Fewer trees result in higher atmospheric CO₂ levels, accelerating global warming and altering rainfall patterns.
2. Practical Solutions:
1. Afforestation & Reforestation: Organizing mass tree-plantation drives like Van Mahotsav in school campuses, community parks, and degraded forest lands.
2. Practicing the 3Rs (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle): Recycling paper notebooks and packaging materials to significantly reduce the commercial demand for cutting down pulpwood trees.
3. Strict Enforcement & Protected Biospheres: Enforcing strict anti-poaching laws and preserving sacred groves and national sanctuaries.

CBSE Competency-Based Question (Ecological Habitat Case Study)
Case Study: Desert vs Aquatic Plant Morphological Adaptations:
During an environmental science field study, students inspected a prickly Opuntia (Cactus) thriving in arid sand and a Lotus plant floating gracefully in a village pond.
(a) How has the Cactus modified its leaves, stem, and root system to survive extreme desert drought?
(b) How are the leaves and stems of a Lotus specially adapted for an aquatic lifestyle?
(c) Why do submerged freshwater plants (like Hydrilla and Vallisneria) possess thin, ribbon-like leaves?
Detailed Analytical Solution & Marking Scheme

(a) Xerophytic Adaptations of Cactus:
• Leaves: Modified into sharp spines to drastically eliminate transpiration water loss and deter grazing herbivores.
• Stem: Green, succulent, and spongy; performs photosynthesis and stores substantial water reserves beneath a thick waxy cuticle.
• Roots: Deep-penetrating taproots that spread horizontally over vast areas near the surface to quickly absorb fleeting rainfall.

(b) Hydrophytic Adaptations of Lotus:
• Leaves: Broad circular leaves coated with a waxy water-repellent layer that prevents rot. Stomata are located solely on the upper sun-exposed surface.
• Stem (Petiole): Long, slender, hollow stalks containing abundant air chambers (aerenchyma) that provide buoyancy and flex with water currents.

(c) Function of Ribbon-like Submerged Leaves:
Narrow, thin ribbon leaves bend easily with flowing water currents without offering mechanical resistance, preventing the plant from tearing or being uprooted.

Chapter 3: Mindful Eating: A Path to a Healthy Body

NCERT Curiosity (Class 6) — Nutrients, Chemical Testing (Starch, Fats, Proteins), Balanced Diet, Traditional Culinary Wisdom & Deficiency Diseases | CBSE 2026-27

📌 Key Nutritional Principles & Food Components
  • Major Essential Nutrients:
    • Carbohydrates: Primary source of instant energy (Starches & Sugars in Rice, Wheat, Potatoes, Maize).
    • Fats: Concentrated energy reserves (Butter, Ghee, Mustard oil, Groundnuts, Almonds).
    • Proteins: Bodybuilding nutrients essential for tissue growth, cell repair, and enzyme synthesis (Pulses, Gram, Soya bean, Milk, Paneer, Eggs).
    • Vitamins & Minerals: Protective nutrients defending against infections, regulating biochemical pathways, maintaining bone, teeth, and vision health.
    • Dietary Fibre (Roughage) & Water: Aids digestion, maintains gut microbiome, and prevents constipation.
  • Millets (Shree Anna / Nutri-Cereals): Jowar (Sorghum), Bajra (Pearl millet), and Ragi (Finger millet) are climate-resilient, mineral-rich superfoods high in calcium, iron, and fibre.
  • Mindful Eating: Conscious selection of freshly cooked, minimally processed foods that nourish bodily organs over calorie-dense, ultra-processed junk food.

Q5
Describe the step-by-step laboratory procedures to test for: (i) Starch, (ii) Fats, and (iii) Proteins in given food samples. Include required chemical reagents and expected color changes.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
(i) Test for Starch (Carbohydrates):
• Procedure: Take a small portion of the food sample (e.g., a freshly cut slice of raw potato or boiled rice paste). Add 2 to 3 drops of dilute Iodine solution using a dropper.
• Observation: The food surface turns an intense Blue-Black color.
• Inference: Formation of blue-black complex confirms the presence of starch.
(ii) Test for Fats:
• Procedure: Wrap a small piece of food (e.g., crushed groundnut kernel or a drop of mustard oil) in a clean piece of white paper and crush it gently without tearing the paper.
• Observation: An oily, translucent patch appears on the paper.
• Inference: When held against light, the patch lets faint light pass through, proving the presence of fats.
(iii) Test for Proteins (Biuret Test):
• Procedure: Grind a small quantity of food (e.g., boiled egg white or powdered dal paste) and transfer it into a clean test tube with 10 drops of water. Shake thoroughly. Add 2 drops of Copper Sulphate (CuSO₄) solution and 10 drops of Caustic Soda (NaOH) solution. Shake well and leave undisturbed for 5 minutes.
• Observation: The mixture changes to a distinct Violet / Purple color.
• Inference: The violet hue confirms the presence of proteins.

📌 Key Magnetic Principles & Field Concepts
  • Discovery & Types of Magnets: Natural magnet is Magnetite (iron ore, Fe₃O₄). Artificial magnets come in various shapes: Bar, Horseshoe, Cylindrical, Ring, and Ball-ended magnets.
  • Magnetic vs Non-Magnetic Materials:
    • Magnetic: Materials attracted towards a magnet (Iron, Nickel, Cobalt, Steel).
    • Non-Magnetic: Materials experiencing no magnetic force (Wood, Plastic, Paper, Aluminium, Copper, Glass).
  • Poles of a Magnet: Every magnet has two distinct poles where magnetic attraction is concentrated: North Pole (N) and South Pole (S). Magnetic poles always exist in inseparable pairs. Monopoles do not exist.

    • Unlike (opposite) poles ATTRACT: North attracts South (N ↔ S).
    • Like (similar) poles REPEL: North repels North (N ⇄ N); South repels South (S ⇄ S). Repulsion is the only sure test of magnetism!
  • Directive Property & Magnetic Compass: A freely suspended magnet always settles along Earth's geographic North-South direction. This directive property is utilized in the magnetic compass for navigation.

NCERT Curiosity (Class 6) — SI Standard Units, Metrology, Parallax Error, Measuring Curved Lines, Rest & Motion, Types of Motion | CBSE 2026-27

📌 Key Metrological & Kinematic Principles
  • Need for Standard Units: Ancient non-standard units (handspan, cubit, foot, pace) varied from person to person. The International System of Units (SI) standardized the metre (m) as the universal base unit of length.
  • Standard Length Conversions:
    • 1 kilometre (km) = 1,000 metres (m)
    • 1 metre (m) = 100 centimetres (cm) = 1,000 millimetres (mm)
    • 1 centimetre (cm) = 10 millimetres (mm)
  • Three Essential Rules for Accurate Ruler Measurement:
    • Alignment: Place the ruler exactly parallel and touching the length being measured.
    • Avoiding Damaged Ends: If the zero mark is broken, start from any clear integer mark (e.g., 2.0 cm) and subtract that from the final reading: True Length = Final Mark - Initial Mark.
    • Eliminating Parallax Error: The eye must be placed vertically directly above the scale mark. Looking from an oblique angle gives false readings.
  • Rest vs Motion: An object is in motion if its position changes relative to a stationary reference point (frame of reference) over time.
  • Classification of Motion:
    • Linear (Rectilinear) Motion: Movement along a straight line (e.g., car moving on a straight road, falling apple).
    • Circular Motion: Movement along a circular path maintaining a constant distance from a fixed center (e.g., hands of a clock, blades of ceiling fan).
    • Periodic / Oscillatory Motion: Motion that repeats itself at regular fixed intervals of time (e.g., pendulum of a clock, child on a swing, vibrating guitar string).

    • Lustre (Shine): Freshly cut surfaces of metals (Gold, Silver, Copper, Aluminium) shine brightly. Non-metals (wood, rubber, chalk) are dull.
    • Hardness vs Softness: Hard materials cannot be scratched or compressed easily (Iron, Diamond). Soft materials can be compressed or scratched easily (Sponge, Wax, Cotton).
    • Solubility in Water: Substances that dissolve completely and disappear in water are soluble (Sugar, Salt). Those that do not mix are insoluble (Sand, Chalk powder, Mustard oil).
    • Density (Floating vs Sinking): Objects less dense than water float (Dry leaves, Wood, Oil). Denser objects sink (Iron nail, Stone).
  • Optical Transparency:
    • Transparent: Materials through which light passes freely, allowing objects to be seen clearly (Clean glass, Air, Pure water).
    • Translucent: Materials allowing partial light to pass through, so objects are seen hazily/blurred (Oiled paper, Butter paper, Frosted glass).
    • Opaque: Materials blocking all light; cannot see through at all (Wood, Cardboard, Metals).

Chapter 7: Temperature and its Measurement

NCERT Curiosity (Class 6) — Clinical vs Laboratory Thermometers, Kink Function, Least Count, Reading Temperature & Digital Thermometers | CBSE 2026-27

📌 Key Thermometric Principles & Measurement Standards
  • Definition of Temperature: A reliable quantitative measure of the degree of hotness or coldness of an object. The standard SI unit is Kelvin (K); commonly measured in degree Celsius (°C) and degree Fahrenheit (°F).
  • Normal Human Body Temperature: 37.0 °C (equivalent to 98.6 °F).
  • Clinical Thermometer:
    • Used specifically to measure human body temperature.
    • Range: 35 °C to 42 °C (or 94 °F to 108 °F).
    • Features a narrow constriction (kink) just above the bulb that prevents the mercury level from dropping immediately when taken out of the mouth or armpit.
  • Laboratory Thermometer:
    • Used to measure temperatures in scientific experiments.
    • Range: Typically -10 °C to 110 °C.
    • Has NO kink; must be read while the bulb is still immersed in the liquid.
  • Least Count of a Thermometer: The value of the smallest subdivision on the graduated capillary stem.

Chapter 8: A Journey through States of Water

NCERT Curiosity (Class 6) — Evaporation, Condensation, Transpiration, Water Cycle & Water Conservation | CBSE 2026-27

📌 Key Hydrological Principles & Phase Changes
  • Three Physical States of Water:
    • Solid (Ice/Snow): Rigid form with definite shape and volume; found in glaciers and polar ice sheets.
    • Liquid (Water): Fluid form with definite volume but taking container shape; oceans, rivers, groundwater.
    • Gas (Water Vapour / Steam): Invisible gaseous state expanding to fill space; present continuously in the atmosphere.
  • Phase Transformation Processes:
    • Evaporation: Conversion of liquid water into water vapour below boiling point; absorbs latent heat, creating a cooling effect. Accelerated by high temperature, large surface area, wind, and low humidity.
    • Condensation: Transformation of water vapour into tiny liquid droplets upon cooling; responsible for cloud, dew, and fog formation.
    • Transpiration: Biological loss of water vapour through microscopic leaf stomata into the atmosphere.
  • The Water Cycle: The continuous cyclical circulation of water between Earth's surface and the atmosphere driven by solar radiation.

Chapter 9: Methods of Separation in Everyday Life

NCERT Curiosity (Class 6) — Handpicking, Threshing, Winnowing, Sieving, Sedimentation, Decantation, Filtration, Churning & Evaporation | CBSE 2026-27

📌 Key Separation Principles & Techniques
  • Purpose of Separation: To remove harmful or undesirable impurities, separate useful components from mixtures, or isolate pure substances for analysis.
  • Separation of Solid-Solid Mixtures:
    • Handpicking: Manually sorting visibly different substances based on size, colour, or shape when impurities are in small quantities.
    • Threshing: Beating harvested stalks against a hard surface to separate grain seeds.
    • Winnowing: Separating heavier components (grains) from lighter components (husk) using blowing wind.
    • Sieving: Separating particles of different sizes through wire mesh pores.
  • Separation of Insoluble Solid-Liquid Mixtures:
    • Sedimentation: Allowing heavier insoluble solid particles to settle down at the bottom of a container.
    • Decantation: Gently pouring out the upper clear liquid layer without disturbing the settled sediment.
    • Filtration: Passing a mixture through a porous filter medium (filter paper, muslin cloth) to completely retain insoluble residue.
  • Separation of Soluble Solid-Liquid Mixtures:
    • Evaporation: Heating a solution until the liquid solvent evaporates away, leaving the solid solute crystals behind (e.g., obtaining salt from seawater).
    • Churning (Centrifugation): Rapid spinning of curd/milk to separate lighter cream/butter from heavier liquid whey.

📌 Key Concepts & Formulas
  • Ball and Socket Joint: Allows movement in all directions (Shoulder and Hip joints).

NCERT Science (Classic) (Class 6) — Electric Cell (Terminal Anode & Cathode), Electric Bulb & Filament, Closed & Open Electric Circuit, Electric Switch, Electrical Conductors & Insulators | CBSE 2026-27

📌 Key Concepts & Formulas
  • Electric Cell: Converts chemical energy into electrical energy. Has a positive (+) metal cap and a negative (-) flat metal disc.
  • Electric Bulb: Has a thin coiled wire called filament (made of Tungsten) that glows when current passes. Supported by two thick wires.
  • Electric Circuit: Complete closed conducting loop allowing current to flow from positive terminal to negative terminal.

NCERT Textbook Exercise & Practice Questions
Q1
Explain why the bulb does not glow in an open circuit. What happens when the filament of an electric bulb breaks?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Circuit Continuity:
• Open Circuit: An electric current requires an unbroken, continuous closed loop of conducting material from the positive terminal of the cell to the negative terminal. When the circuit is open (switch OFF or wire disconnected), the conducting path is broken, current ceases to flow, and the bulb does not glow.
• Fused Bulb: When the filament of an electric bulb breaks, the circuit inside the bulb is fractured. Because the path of current between the cell's terminals is broken, current cannot flow, and the fused bulb will not glow.

Chapter 10: Fun with Magnets

NCERT Science (Classic) (Class 6) — Discovery of Magnets (Magnes shepherd), Magnetic and Non-Magnetic Materials, Poles of Magnet (North and South), Magnetic Compass for Navigation, Attraction and Repulsion Rules, Caring for Magnets | CBSE 2026-27

📌 Key Concepts & Formulas
  • Magnetic Materials: Attracted by a magnet (Iron, Nickel, Cobalt). Non-magnetic: Wood, Plastic, Glass, Copper.
  • Poles: Regions near the two ends of a magnet where magnetic attraction is strongest (North Pole N and South Pole S). Poles always exist in pairs.
  • Law of Magnetic Poles: Like poles repel each other (N-N or S-S repel); Unlike poles attract each other (N-S attract).
  • Direction Finding: A freely suspended bar magnet always aligns in the North-South direction.
NCERT Textbook Exercise & Practice Questions
Q1
Where are the poles of a bar magnet located? How will you find the north pole of a magnet if it is not marked?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Poles & Direction Finding:
• Location of Poles: The poles are located at the two extreme ends of the bar magnet. Iron filings sprinkled on a sheet over a magnet accumulate most heavily at these two terminal points.
• Identifying North Pole:
1. Suspend the unmarked bar magnet freely from its center using a light thread so it can rotate freely in a horizontal plane.
2. Wait until the magnet comes to rest.
3. The end that consistently points towards the geographical North is its North-seeking pole (North pole); the opposite end pointing South is its South pole.

[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Magnetic Interaction & Storage:
• Law of Magnetic Poles:
1. Like poles repel each other (North repels North; South repels South).
2. Unlike poles attract each other (North attracts South).
• Safe Storage of Magnets:
1. Bar magnets should always be kept in pairs with opposite poles alongside each other.
2. They must be separated by a strip of wood.
3. Two pieces of soft iron (magnetic keepers) must be placed across their ends.
4. Never heat, hammer, or drop magnets from a height.

  • Atmosphere: Thick blanket of air surrounding Earth, extending up to hundreds of kilometres.

Q2
How do plants and animals help each other in the exchange of gases in the atmosphere? Why do mountaineers carry oxygen cylinders?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
Ecological Gas Balance & High Altitude:
• Mutual Gas Exchange:
1. Animals consume oxygen during respiration and exhale carbon dioxide.
2. Green plants absorb carbon dioxide from air to prepare food by photosynthesis in sunlight and release oxygen back into the atmosphere.
3. Because plants produce far more oxygen during photosynthesis than they consume in respiration, the atmospheric balance between O₂ and CO₂ is maintained.
• Mountaineers: As altitude increases, atmospheric pressure drops and air becomes progressively thinner with lower oxygen density. To prevent altitude sickness and hypoxia, mountaineers carry oxygen cylinders.

During winter months in metropolitan cities, a thick haze of smog blankets the city. (a) What two components combine to form smog? (b) Why are tall chimneys installed in factories with electrostatic precipitators? (c) Why do traffic police officers stationed at busy crossroads wear respirators?
