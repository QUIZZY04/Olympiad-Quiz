Exercises 12.1 to 12.17
Q 12.1
Exercise 12.1
Choose the correct alternative from the clues given at the end of each statement:
(a) The size of the atom in Thomson’s model is .......... the atomic size in Rutherford’s model. (much greater than/no different from/much less than)
(b) In the ground state of .......... electrons are in stable equilibrium, while in .......... electrons always experience a net force. (Thomson’s model/ Rutherford’s model)
(c) A classical atom based on .......... is doomed to collapse. (Thomson’s model/ Rutherford’s model)
(d) An atom has a nearly continuous mass distribution in .......... but a highly non-uniform mass distribution in .......... (Thomson’s model/ Rutherford’s model)
(e) The positively charged part of the atom possesses most of the mass in .......... (Rutherford’s model/both the models).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Answers with Explanations:

  1. (a) no different from. Both atomic models specify an atomic radius of the order of $10^{-10} \text{ m}$ ($1 \text{ \AA}$).
  2. (b) Thomson’s model; Rutherford’s model. In Thomson's plum pudding model, stationary electrons are embedded in a positive sphere experiencing zero net force at equilibrium positions. In Rutherford's planetary model, orbiting electrons undergo centripetal acceleration ($a = v^2/r$) and experience continuous net inward electrostatic attraction.
  3. (c) Rutherford’s model. According to classical electromagnetic theory, an accelerated revolving electron must continuously radiate electromagnetic energy, spiral inward, and collapse into the nucleus within $\sim 10^{-8} \text{ s}$.
  4. (d) Thomson’s model; Rutherford’s model. Thomson's model assumed positive mass is distributed continuously over the entire $10^{-10} \text{ m}$ sphere, whereas Rutherford's model localized $>99.9\%$ of the atom's mass in a tiny central nucleus of radius $\sim 10^{-15} \text{ m}$.
  5. (e) both the models. In both Thomson's and Rutherford's models, the electrons account for a negligible fraction of atomic mass, meaning the positively charged portion contains almost all the mass of the atom.

Q 12.2
Exercise 12.2
Suppose you are given a chance to repeat the alpha-particle scattering experiment using a thin sheet of solid hydrogen in place of the gold foil. (Hydrogen is a solid at temperatures below $14 \text{ K}$). What results do you expect?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Analysis:
• An alpha-particle is a helium nucleus ($^4_2\text{He}$) with mass $m_\alpha \approx 4 \text{ u}$.
• The target nucleus in solid hydrogen is a proton ($^1_1\text{H}$) with mass $m_p \approx 1 \text{ u}$.
• Thus, the projectile is four times heavier than the target nucleus ($m_\alpha = 4 m_p$).

Collision Mechanics:
In a head-on elastic collision of a heavy particle of mass $m_1$ with a stationary light target $m_2$ ($m_1 > m_2$): \[ v_1' = \left( \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2} \right) v_1 = \left( \frac{4 - 1}{4 + 1} \right) v_1 = +\frac{3}{5} v_1 \] The alpha-particle continues to move forward in the forward direction ($v_1' > 0$). It can never rebound backwards (maximum scattering angle is $\arcsin(m_p/m_\alpha) = \arcsin(0.25) \approx 14.5^\circ$).

Expected Results:
1. No alpha-particles will be scattered at large angles or bounced back through $180^\circ$.
2. Instead, the incident $\alpha$-particles will knock the light hydrogen protons forcefully in the forward direction.
3. Hence, the experiment would fail to demonstrate the existence of a concentrated, massive atomic nucleus.

Q 12.3
Exercise 12.3
What is the shortest wavelength present in the Paschen series of spectral lines?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Rydberg Formula for Hydrogen:
\[ \frac{1}{\lambda} = R \left( \frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2} \right) \] where $R = 1.097 \times 10^7 \text{ m}^{-1}$.

Condition for Shortest Wavelength (Series Limit):
For the Paschen series, the lower energy level is $n_1 = 3$.
The shortest wavelength corresponds to maximum photon energy, which occurs for the transition from $n_2 = \infty$ to $n_1 = 3$: \[ \frac{1}{\lambda_{min}} = R \left( \frac{1}{3^2} - \frac{1}{\infty^2} \right) = \frac{R}{9} \] \[ \lambda_{min} = \frac{9}{R} = \frac{9}{1.097 \times 10^7 \text{ m}^{-1}} \approx 8.204 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m} = 820.4 \text{ nm} = 8204 \text{ \AA} \]

Result: The shortest wavelength is $820.4 \text{ nm}$ (located in the infrared region).

Q 12.4
Exercise 12.4
A difference of $2.3 \text{ eV}$ separates two energy levels in an atom. What is the frequency of radiation emitted when the atom makes a transition from the upper level to the lower level?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Energy difference $\Delta E = 2.3 \text{ eV} = 2.3 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J} = 3.685 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}$
Planck's constant $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}$

Bohr Frequency Condition:
\[ \Delta E = h \nu \implies \nu = \frac{\Delta E}{h} \] \[ \nu = \frac{3.685 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}}{6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}} \approx 5.56 \times 10^{14} \text{ Hz} \]

Result: The frequency of emitted radiation is $5.56 \times 10^{14} \text{ Hz}$ (in the green-yellow visible region).

Q 12.6
Exercise 12.6
A hydrogen atom initially in the ground level absorbs a photon, which excites it to the $n = 4$ level. Determine the wavelength and frequency of photon.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Initial level $n_1 = 1$, Final level $n_2 = 4$
$E_n = -\frac{13.6}{n^2} \text{ eV}$

Step 1: Energy Levels:
• $E_1 = -13.6 \text{ eV}$
• $E_4 = -\frac{13.6}{4^2} = -\frac{13.6}{16} = -0.85 \text{ eV}$
Energy of absorbed photon: \[ \Delta E = E_4 - E_1 = -0.85 - (-13.6) = 12.75 \text{ eV} \] In Joules: \[ \Delta E = 12.75 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J} \approx 2.043 \times 10^{-18} \text{ J} \]

Step 2: Frequency of Photon:
\[ \nu = \frac{\Delta E}{h} = \frac{2.043 \times 10^{-18} \text{ J}}{6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}} \approx 3.083 \times 10^{15} \text{ Hz} \]

Step 3: Wavelength of Photon:
\[ \lambda = \frac{c}{\nu} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}}{3.083 \times 10^{15} \text{ s}^{-1}} \approx 9.73 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m} = 97.3 \text{ nm} = 973 \text{ \AA} \] (Belongs to the Lyman series in the ultraviolet region).

Q 12.7
Exercise 12.7
(a) Using the Bohr’s model calculate the speed of the electron in a hydrogen atom in the $n = 1, 2,$ and $3$ levels.
(b) Calculate the orbital period in each of these levels.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Formulas for Hydrogen ($Z = 1$):
1. Orbital speed: $v_n = \frac{v_1}{n}$ where $v_1 = \frac{e^2}{2\varepsilon_0 h} = \frac{c}{137} \approx 2.186 \times 10^6 \text{ m s}^{-1}$.
2. Orbit radius: $r_n = n^2 r_1$ where $r_1 = 0.529 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m}$.
3. Orbital period: $T_n = \frac{2\pi r_n}{v_n} = \frac{2\pi (n^2 r_1)}{(v_1 / n)} = n^3 T_1$.

(a) Speeds in Levels:
• $n = 1$: $v_1 = 2.19 \times 10^6 \text{ m s}^{-1}$
• $n = 2$: $v_2 = \frac{2.186 \times 10^6}{2} = 1.093 \times 10^6 \text{ m s}^{-1} \approx 1.09 \times 10^6 \text{ m s}^{-1}$
• $n = 3$: $v_3 = \frac{2.186 \times 10^6}{3} = 7.287 \times 10^5 \text{ m s}^{-1} \approx 7.29 \times 10^5 \text{ m s}^{-1}$

(b) Orbital Periods:
For $n = 1$: \[ T_1 = \frac{2\pi r_1}{v_1} = \frac{2\pi \times (0.529 \times 10^{-10})}{2.186 \times 10^6} \approx 1.52 \times 10^{-16} \text{ s} \] • For $n = 2$: $T_2 = 2^3 T_1 = 8 \times (1.52 \times 10^{-16}) = 1.216 \times 10^{-15} \text{ s} \approx 1.22 \times 10^{-15} \text{ s}$
• For $n = 3$: $T_3 = 3^3 T_1 = 27 \times (1.52 \times 10^{-16}) = 4.104 \times 10^{-15} \text{ s} \approx 4.10 \times 10^{-15} \text{ s}$

Q 12.9
Exercise 12.9
A $12.5 \text{ eV}$ electron beam is used to bombard gaseous hydrogen at room temperature. What series of wavelengths will be emitted?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
At room temperature, hydrogen atoms reside in the ground state ($n = 1, E_1 = -13.6 \text{ eV}$).
Maximum energy absorbed $\le 12.5 \text{ eV}$.
Maximum achievable energy of atom: \[ E_{max} = -13.6 + 12.5 = -1.1 \text{ eV} \]

Energy Levels of Hydrogen:
• $E_1 = -13.6 \text{ eV}$
• $E_2 = -3.4 \text{ eV} \quad (\Delta E_{1\to 2} = 10.2 \text{ eV} < 12.5 \text{ eV})$
• $E_3 = -1.51 \text{ eV} \quad (\Delta E_{1\to 3} = 12.09 \text{ eV} < 12.5 \text{ eV})$
• $E_4 = -0.85 \text{ eV} \quad (\Delta E_{1\to 4} = 12.75 \text{ eV} > 12.5 \text{ eV})$
Thus, electrons can only excite the hydrogen atoms up to the $n = 3$ level.

Possible De-excitation Transitions:
Total spectral lines $= \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \frac{3 \times 2}{2} = 3$ lines:
1. $3 \to 1$ (Lyman series): \[ \frac{1}{\lambda} = R \left(\frac{1}{1^2} - \frac{1}{3^2}\right) = \frac{8}{9} R \implies \lambda = \frac{9}{8 \times 1.097 \times 10^7} \approx 102.5 \text{ nm} \] 2. $2 \to 1$ (Lyman series): \[ \frac{1}{\lambda} = R \left(\frac{1}{1^2} - \frac{1}{2^2}\right) = \frac{3}{4} R \implies \lambda = \frac{4}{3 \times 1.097 \times 10^7} \approx 121.5 \text{ nm} \] 3. $3 \to 2$ (Balmer series): \[ \frac{1}{\lambda} = R \left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{3^2}\right) = \frac{5}{36} R \implies \lambda = \frac{36}{5 \times 1.097 \times 10^7} \approx 656.3 \text{ nm} \]

Result: Two lines of the Lyman series ($102.5 \text{ nm}, 121.5 \text{ nm}$) and one line of the Balmer series ($656.3 \text{ nm}$) will be emitted.

Q 12.10
Exercise 12.10
In accordance with the Bohr’s model, find the quantum number that characterises the earth’s revolution around the sun in an orbit of radius $1.5 \times 10^{11} \text{ m}$ with orbital speed $3.0 \times 10^4 \text{ m s}^{-1}$. (Mass of earth $= 6.0 \times 10^{24} \text{ kg}$).
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Mass of Earth $m = 6.0 \times 10^{24} \text{ kg}$
Orbital radius $r = 1.5 \times 10^{11} \text{ m}$
Orbital speed $v = 3.0 \times 10^4 \text{ m s}^{-1}$
Planck's constant $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}$

Bohr’s Angular Momentum Quantization:
\[ L = m v r = \frac{n h}{2\pi} \implies n = \frac{2\pi m v r}{h} \]

Calculation:
\[ n = \frac{2\pi \times (6.0 \times 10^{24} \text{ kg}) \times (3.0 \times 10^4 \text{ m s}^{-1}) \times (1.5 \times 10^{11} \text{ m})}{6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}} \] \[ n = \frac{2\pi \times 2.7 \times 10^{40}}{6.626 \times 10^{-34}} = \frac{1.696 \times 10^{41}}{6.626 \times 10^{-34}} \approx 2.56 \times 10^{74} \]

Physical Significance: The quantum number $n \sim 10^{74}$ is astronomically large. At such colossal quantum numbers, successive quantum energy states are virtually continuous, completely masking quantum effects in macroscopic planetary orbits (Bohr's correspondence principle).

Q 12.11
Exercise 12.11
Answer the following questions contrasting Thomson’s and Rutherford’s models:
(a) In what way did Rutherford explain the large-angle scattering of $\alpha$-particles that Thomson’s model failed to account for?
(b) Why did Geiger and Marsden choose a gold foil rather than an aluminum or copper foil?
(c) Why was the gold foil chosen to be extremely thin (approx. $10^{-7} \text{ m}$)?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

(a) Large-Angle Scattering:
• In Thomson’s model, the positive charge and mass are spread uniformly throughout the sphere of radius $\sim 10^{-10} \text{ m}$. The electric field inside such a diffuse distribution is weak and incapable of exerting enough electrostatic force to deflect a fast-moving, massive $\alpha$-particle by more than a fraction of a degree.
• Rutherford posited that all positive charge and nearly the entire mass are concentrated in a tiny central core (the nucleus) of radius $< 10^{-14} \text{ m}$. An $\alpha$-particle making a near head-on approach experiences a colossal repulsive Coulomb force, resulting in large deflections and backward scattering ($> 90^\circ$).

(b) Selection of Gold Foil:
1. Gold has high atomic number ($Z = 79$). The repulsive Coulomb force ($F \propto Z$) is very large, maximizing scattering angles.
2. Gold is exceptionally malleable, allowing sheets as thin as $10^{-7} \text{ m}$ (about 400 atoms thick) to be fabricated.

(c) Reason for Extreme Thinness:
To ensure single scattering. If the foil were thick, $\alpha$-particles would undergo multiple successive scatterings by different atoms, making mathematical extraction of single nuclear collision cross-sections impossible.

High $Z$ and malleability for gold selection1 Mark

Q 12.12
Exercise 12.12
State Bohr’s three basic postulates for the hydrogen atom. Derive the expressions for the radius of the $n$-th orbit and the total energy of an electron, showing that $E_n = -\frac{13.6}{n^2} \text{ eV}$.
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Bohr’s Postulates:

  1. Postulate of Stationary Orbits: An electron revolves around the nucleus only in certain stable, non-radiating orbits (stationary states).
  2. Bohr's Quantization Rule: The orbital angular momentum of the revolving electron is an integral multiple of $\frac{h}{2\pi}$: \[ L = m v r = \frac{n h}{2\pi}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots \]
  3. Bohr's Frequency Condition: An electron emits or absorbs a single photon only when jumping from one discrete stationary orbit to another: \[ h\nu = E_i - E_f \]

Derivation of Radius $r_n$:
The electrostatic attraction provides the requisite centripetal force: \[ \frac{m v^2}{r} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{e^2}{r^2} \implies m v^2 r = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \quad \text{--- (1)} \] From Bohr’s quantization condition: \[ v = \frac{n h}{2\pi m r} \quad \text{--- (2)} \] Substituting (2) into (1): \[ m \left( \frac{n h}{2\pi m r} \right)^2 r = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \implies \frac{n^2 h^2}{4\pi^2 m r} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \] \[ r_n = \frac{\varepsilon_0 n^2 h^2}{\pi m e^2} \] For $n = 1$, $r_1 = 0.529 \text{ \AA} = 5.29 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}$.

Derivation of Total Energy $E_n$:
• Kinetic Energy: $K = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = \frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$
• Potential Energy: $U = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$
• Total Energy: \[ E_n = K + U = \frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r} - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = -\frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r_n} \] Substituting $r_n$: \[ E_n = -\frac{m e^4}{8 \varepsilon_0^2 n^2 h^2} = -\frac{13.6}{n^2} \text{ eV} \]

Q 12.13
Exercise 12.13
Explain de Broglie’s explanation of Bohr’s second postulate of quantization of angular momentum using standing electron matter waves.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

de Broglie’s Hypothesis and Standing Waves:
Louis de Broglie argued that an electron revolving in an atomic orbit behaves as a matter wave having wavelength: \[ \lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{m v} \]

Condition for Stationary (Stable) Orbit:
• For an electron orbit to be stationary and non-radiating, the matter wave associated with the electron must form a continuous, constructive standing wave around the circular perimeter.
• If the ends of the wave did not meet in phase, destructive interference would cause the wave to cancel itself out upon successive revolutions.
• Therefore, the circumference of the circular orbit must contain an exact integral number of de Broglie wavelengths: \[ 2\pi r = n \lambda, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots \]

Derivation of Bohr's Quantization Condition:
Substitute $\lambda = \frac{h}{m v}$ into the standing wave condition: \[ 2\pi r = n \left( \frac{h}{m v} \right) \] Rearranging terms: \[ m v r = \frac{n h}{2\pi} \] Thus, Bohr’s second postulate ($L = n \hbar$) is naturally explained as a direct consequence of the wave nature of matter.

Q 12.14
Exercise 12.14
In a Geiger-Marsden experiment, what is the distance of closest approach to the nucleus of a $7.7 \text{ MeV} \ \alpha$-particle before it comes momentarily to rest and reverses its direction? ($Z = 79$ for gold).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Kinetic energy of $\alpha$-particle $K = 7.7 \text{ MeV} = 7.7 \times 10^6 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J} \approx 1.234 \times 10^{-12} \text{ J}$
Target atomic number for Gold $Z = 79$
Charge of $\alpha$-particle $q_1 = 2e$, Charge of nucleus $q_2 = Z e$
$\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 9.0 \times 10^9 \text{ N m}^2 \text{ C}^{-2}$

Energy Conservation at Distance of Closest Approach ($r_0$):
At $r = r_0$, the $\alpha$-particle momentarily stops; its entire initial kinetic energy is converted into electrostatic potential energy: \[ K = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{(2e)(Ze)}{r_0} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2 Z e^2}{r_0} \] \[ r_0 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2 Z e^2}{K} \]

Calculation:
\[ r_0 = \frac{(9.0 \times 10^9) \times 2 \times 79 \times (1.602 \times 10^{-19})^2}{1.234 \times 10^{-12}} = \frac{1.422 \times 10^{12} \times 2.566 \times 10^{-38}}{1.234 \times 10^{-12}} \] \[ r_0 = \frac{3.649 \times 10^{-26}}{1.234 \times 10^{-12}} \approx 2.96 \times 10^{-14} \text{ m} = 29.6 \text{ fm} \approx 30 \text{ fm} \]

Result: The distance of closest approach is approximately $3.0 \times 10^{-14} \text{ m}$ ($30 \text{ fm}$), providing an upper limit for the size of the gold nucleus.

Q 12.15
Exercise 12.15
Calculate the impact parameter of a $5.5 \text{ MeV} \ \alpha$-particle scattered by a gold nucleus ($Z = 79$) through an angle of $90^\circ$.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
$K = 5.5 \text{ MeV} = 5.5 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-13} \text{ J} = 8.811 \times 10^{-13} \text{ J}$
$Z = 79$, $\theta = 90^\circ \implies \frac{\theta}{2} = 45^\circ$

Impact Parameter Formula:
\[ b = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Z e^2 \cot(\theta/2)}{K} \] Since $\cot(45^\circ) = 1$: \[ b = \frac{(9.0 \times 10^9) \times 79 \times (1.602 \times 10^{-19})^2 \times 1}{8.811 \times 10^{-13}} \] \[ b = \frac{7.11 \times 10^{11} \times 2.566 \times 10^{-38}}{8.811 \times 10^{-13}} = \frac{1.824 \times 10^{-26}}{8.811 \times 10^{-13}} \approx 2.07 \times 10^{-14} \text{ m} = 2.07 \times 10^{-12} \text{ cm} \]

Result: The impact parameter is $2.07 \times 10^{-14} \text{ m}$ ($20.7 \text{ fm}$).

Q 12.17
Exercise 12.17
State four major limitations / shortcomings of Bohr's atomic model.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Shortcomings of Bohr’s Model:

  1. Inapplicable to Multi-Electron Atoms: Bohr's model is strictly valid only for hydrogen and hydrogen-like single-electron ions ($ ext{He}^+, ext{Li}^{2+}, ext{Be}^{3+}$). It cannot predict the spectrum of atoms with two or more electrons (such as helium) because it neglects mutual electron-electron repulsive interactions.
  2. Fine Structure of Spectral Lines: High-resolution spectroscopy reveals that many single spectral lines are actually close multiplets (fine structure). Bohr's model cannot account for this splitting caused by relativistic velocity corrections and electron spin-orbit coupling.
  3. Zeeman and Stark Effects: It fails to explain the splitting of spectral lines into multiple components in the presence of external magnetic fields (Zeeman effect) and electric fields (Stark effect).
  4. Relative Intensities of Spectral Lines: Bohr's model gives the frequencies of spectral transitions but provides no framework to calculate the transition probabilities or relative intensities of different spectral lines.
  5. Violation of Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle: Bohr assumes well-defined circular orbits where both position $r$ and momentum $p$ are simultaneously precisely determined, in direct violation of $Delta x Delta p ge rac{hbar}{2}$.
