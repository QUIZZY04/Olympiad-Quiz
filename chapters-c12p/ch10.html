Q 10.1
Exercise 10.1
Monochromatic light of wavelength $589 \text{ nm}$ is incident from air on a water surface. What are the wavelength, frequency and speed of:
(a) reflected light, and
(b) refracted light?
Refractive index of water is $1.33$.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Wavelength in air $\lambda = 589 \text{ nm} = 5.89 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}$
Speed of light in air $c = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$
Refractive index of water $\mu = 1.33$

Frequency of Light:
Frequency is an intrinsic property of the source and remains invariant upon reflection and refraction: \[ \nu = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}}{5.89 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}} \approx 5.093 \times 10^{14} \text{ Hz} \]

(a) Reflected Light:
The reflected light remains in air: • Speed: $v = c = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$
• Frequency: $\nu = 5.09 \times 10^{14} \text{ Hz}$
• Wavelength: $\lambda = 589 \text{ nm}$

(b) Refracted Light:
The refracted light propagates in water: • Speed: \[ v = \frac{c}{\mu} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{1.33} \approx 2.256 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1} \] • Frequency: Unchanged, $\nu = 5.09 \times 10^{14} \text{ Hz}$
• Wavelength: \[ \lambda' = \frac{\lambda}{\mu} = \frac{589 \text{ nm}}{1.33} \approx 442.85 \text{ nm} \approx 443 \text{ nm} \]

Q 10.2
Exercise 10.2
What is the shape of the wavefront in each of the following cases:
(a) Light diverging from a point source.
(b) Light emerging out of a convex lens when a point source is placed at its focus.
(c) The portion of the wavefront of light from a distant star intercepted by the Earth.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

(a) Point source:
Light propagates outward equally in all directions from a point source in an isotropic medium. The locus of points having the same phase is a sphere. Hence, the wavefront is Spherical.

(b) Point source at the focus of a convex lens:
After passing through the convex lens, the refracted rays emerge as a parallel beam of light parallel to the principal axis. The surface normal to a parallel beam of rays is a plane. Hence, the wavefront is a Plane wavefront.

(c) Distant star intercepted by Earth:
The star acts as a point source located at astronomical distance ($r \to \infty$). The wavefront originating from it is spherical, but over the relatively small aperture of an observation instrument or the Earth's radius, a tiny portion of a sphere of enormous radius is effectively flat. Hence, it is a Plane wavefront.

Q 10.3
Exercise 10.3
(a) The refractive index of glass is $1.5$. What is the speed of light in glass? (Speed of light in vacuum is $3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$).
(b) Is the speed of light in glass independent of the colour of light? If not, which of the two colours red and violet travels slower in a glass prism?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

(a) Speed of Light in Glass:
\[ v = \frac{c}{\mu} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}}{1.5} = 2.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1} \]

(b) Dependence on Colour (Wavelength):
• No, the speed of light in glass is NOT independent of colour.
• According to Cauchy's dispersion formula: \[ \mu(\lambda) = A + \frac{B}{\lambda^2} + \dots \] Since wavelength of red light is greater than that of violet light ($\lambda_{red} > \lambda_{violet}$), the refractive index for violet is greater than that for red: \[ \mu_{violet} > \mu_{red} \] Since $v = \frac{c}{\mu}$: \[ v_{violet} < v_{red} \] Therefore, violet light travels slower than red light in glass.

Q 10.4
Exercise 10.4
In a Young’s double-slit experiment, the slits are separated by $0.28 \text{ mm}$ and the screen is placed $1.4 \text{ m}$ away. The distance between the central bright fringe and the fourth bright fringe is measured to be $1.2 \text{ cm}$. Determine the wavelength of light used in the experiment.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Slit separation $d = 0.28 \text{ mm} = 0.28 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}$
Screen distance $D = 1.4 \text{ m}$
Order of fringe $n = 4$
Distance to 4th bright fringe $y_4 = 1.2 \text{ cm} = 1.2 \times 10^{-2} \text{ m}$

Formula for $n$-th Bright Fringe:
\[ y_n = n \frac{\lambda D}{d} \implies \lambda = \frac{y_n d}{n D} \]

Calculation:
\[ \lambda = \frac{(1.2 \times 10^{-2} \text{ m}) \times (0.28 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m})}{4 \times 1.4 \text{ m}} = \frac{0.336 \times 10^{-5}}{5.6} = 0.06 \times 10^{-5} = 6.0 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m} \] In nanometres: \[ \lambda = 600 \text{ nm} = 6000 \text{ \AA} \]

Q 10.5
Exercise 10.5
In Young’s double-slit experiment using monochromatic light of wavelength $\lambda$, the intensity of light at a point on the screen where path difference is $\lambda$ is $K$ units. What is the intensity of light at a point where path difference is $\lambda/3$?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Formula for Resultant Intensity in Interference:
For two identical coherent sources of intensity $I_0$: \[ I = 4 I_0 \cos^2\left(\frac{\phi}{2}\right) \] where phase difference is $\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \Delta x$.

Step 1: When Path Difference $\Delta x_1 = \lambda$:
Phase difference: \[ \phi_1 = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} (\lambda) = 2\pi \] Intensity: \[ I_1 = 4 I_0 \cos^2\left(\frac{2\pi}{2}\right) = 4 I_0 \cos^2(\pi) = 4 I_0 (-1)^2 = 4 I_0 = K \implies I_0 = \frac{K}{4} \]

Step 2: When Path Difference $\Delta x_2 = \frac{\lambda}{3}$:
Phase difference: \[ \phi_2 = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \left( \frac{\lambda}{3} \right) = \frac{2\pi}{3} = 120^\circ \] Intensity: \[ I_2 = 4 I_0 \cos^2\left( \frac{2\pi/3}{2} \right) = 4 I_0 \cos^2\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = 4 I_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = 4 I_0 \times \frac{1}{4} = I_0 \] Since $4I_0 = K$, we have: \[ I_2 = \frac{K}{4} \]

Result: The intensity of light at the point is $\frac{K}{4}$ units.

Q 10.6
Exercise 10.6
A beam of light consisting of two wavelengths, $650 \text{ nm}$ and $520 \text{ nm}$, is used to obtain interference fringes in a Young’s double-slit experiment ($d = 2.0 \text{ mm}, D = 120 \text{ cm}$).
(a) Find the distance of the third bright fringe on the screen from the central maximum for wavelength $650 \text{ nm}$.
(b) What is the least distance from the central maximum where the bright fringes due to both the wavelengths coincide?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
$\lambda_1 = 650 \text{ nm} = 6.5 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}$, $\lambda_2 = 520 \text{ nm} = 5.2 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}$
$d = 2.0 \text{ mm} = 2.0 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}$, $D = 120 \text{ cm} = 1.2 \text{ m}$

(a) Distance of 3rd Bright Fringe for $\lambda_1 = 650 \text{ nm}$:
\[ y_3 = 3 \frac{\lambda_1 D}{d} = 3 \times \frac{(6.5 \times 10^{-7}) \times 1.2}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 3 \times \frac{7.8 \times 10^{-7}}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 3 \times (3.9 \times 10^{-4}) = 1.17 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m} = 1.17 \text{ mm} \]

(b) Coincidence of Bright Fringes:
Let the $n$-th bright fringe of $\lambda_1$ coincide with the $m$-th bright fringe of $\lambda_2$ at distance $y$: \[ y = n \frac{\lambda_1 D}{d} = m \frac{\lambda_2 D}{d} \implies n \lambda_1 = m \lambda_2 \] \[ \frac{n}{m} = \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1} = \frac{520 \text{ nm}}{650 \text{ nm}} = \frac{52}{65} = \frac{4}{5} \] The smallest non-zero integers are $n = 4$ and $m = 5$.
The least distance from the central maximum is: \[ y = 4 \frac{\lambda_1 D}{d} = 4 \times \frac{(6.5 \times 10^{-7}) \times 1.2}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 4 \times (3.9 \times 10^{-4}) = 1.56 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m} = 1.56 \text{ mm} \]

Result: The new angular width of the fringe in water is $0.15^\circ$.

Brewster's law states that when unpolarized light is incident at Brewster's angle $i_B$ (or polarizing angle $p$), the reflected light is completely plane polarized perpendicular to the plane of incidence, and the reflected and refracted rays are mutually perpendicular ($i_B + r = 90^\circ$): \[ \tan i_B = \mu \]

Light of wavelength $5000 \text{ \AA}$ falls on a plane reflecting surface. What are the wavelength and frequency of reflected light? For what angle of incidence is the reflected ray normal to the incident ray?

Result: The angle of incidence is $45^\circ$.

Q 10.12
Exercise 10.12
State Huygens' Principle of wave propagation. Using this principle, construct the reflected wavefront for a plane wave incident on a plane reflecting surface and derive the laws of reflection.
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Huygens' Principle:

  1. Every point on a primary wavefront acts as a fresh source of secondary spherical wavelets which spread out in all directions with the speed of the wave in that medium.
  2. The forward common envelope (tangential surface) to these secondary wavelets at any later time gives the new position of the wavefront.

Proof of Laws of Reflection:
Consider a plane wavefront $AB$ incident at angle $i$ on a plane reflecting surface $MN$.
Let $v$ be the speed of light in the medium.
At $t = 0$, end $A$ touches the mirror while end $B$ is at distance $BC$ from the mirror ($BC = v \tau$).
In time $\tau$, secondary wavelets from $A$ grow into a hemisphere of radius $AD = v \tau$.
Tangential envelope $CD$ represents the reflected wavefront, making angle $r$ with $MN$.

Geometric Congruence:
Consider $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle ADC$ on base $AC$: 1. $BC = AD = v \tau$ (by construction)
2. $\angle ABC = \angle ADC = 90^\circ$ (rays are perpendicular to wavefronts)
3. $AC = AC$ (common hypotenuse)
By RHS congruence theorem: \[ \triangle ABC \cong \triangle ADC \] Therefore: \[ \angle BAC = \angle DCA \implies i = r \] (Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection).
Furthermore, the incident wavefront, reflecting surface, and reflected wavefront are all perpendicular to the plane of the paper, proving that the incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in the same plane.

Q 10.13
Exercise 10.13
Using Huygens' principle, verify Snell’s law of refraction: \[ \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{\mu_2}{\mu_1} \] for a plane wave propagating from a rarer medium 1 to a denser medium 2 across a plane interface.
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Refraction Construction by Huygens' Wave Theory:
Let $MN$ be a plane boundary separating medium 1 (speed $v_1$, refractive index $\mu_1$) and medium 2 (speed $v_2$, refractive index $\mu_2$).
A plane wavefront $AB$ is incident at angle $i$ on $MN$.
Let $\tau$ be the time taken by point $B$ to reach $C$ on the interface: \[ BC = v_1 \tau \] During this same time $\tau$, the secondary wavelet originating from $A$ propagates into medium 2 with speed $v_2$, forming a sphere of radius: \[ AE = v_2 \tau \] The tangent plane $CE$ drawn from $C$ to this wavelet represents the refracted wavefront, making angle of refraction $r$ with $MN$.

Mathematical Derivation:
From right-angled $\triangle ABC$: \[ \sin i = \frac{BC}{AC} = \frac{v_1 \tau}{AC} \] From right-angled $\triangle AEC$: \[ \sin r = \frac{AE}{AC} = \frac{v_2 \tau}{AC} \] Dividing the two equations: \[ \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \frac{v_1 \tau / AC}{v_2 \tau / AC} = \frac{v_1}{v_2} \] Since refractive index $\mu = \frac{c}{v} \implies v_1 = \frac{c}{\mu_1}$ and $v_2 = \frac{c}{\mu_2}$: \[ \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \frac{c/\mu_1}{c/\mu_2} = \frac{\mu_2}{\mu_1} = \, ^1\mu_2 \] This proves Snell's law of refraction. If $v_1 > v_2$, $\sin i > \sin r \implies i > r$, meaning the refracted ray bends towards the normal in a denser medium.

Q 10.15
Exercise 10.15
State four distinct points of difference between Interference and Diffraction of light.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Comparison Table:

Feature Interference Diffraction
Source Superposition of waves originating from two separate coherent sources (slits). Superposition of secondary wavelets originating from different parts of the same wavefront.

Q 10.16
Exercise 10.16
In Young’s double-slit experiment, describe how the interference fringe pattern is affected if:
(a) the separation between the slits $d$ is decreased,
(b) the screen is moved further away ($D$ is increased),
(c) the source slit $S$ is moved closer to the double-slit plane ($S_1-S_2$),
(d) the monochromatic source is replaced by a white light source.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Fringe Width Formula: $\beta = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$

  1. (a) Slit separation $d$ decreased:
    Since $\beta \propto \frac{1}{d}$, the fringe width $\beta$ increases. The fringes become wider and more spread out on the screen.
  2. (b) Screen distance $D$ increased:
    Since $\beta \propto D$, the fringe width $\beta$ increases proportionally. However, the brightness (intensity) of fringes decreases slightly due to greater spreading.
  3. (c) Source slit moved closer to double-slit plane:
    For sustained interference fringes, the condition $\frac{s}{S} < \frac{\lambda}{d}$ must be satisfied (where $s$ is source width and $S$ is distance from source to double slit). If $S$ is decreased, $s/S$ increases. If $S$ becomes too small, interference patterns from different portions of the broad source overlap and wash out the pattern. As long as the condition holds, the pattern becomes brighter.
  4. (d) Monochromatic source replaced by white light:
    • At the centre of the screen, path difference $\Delta x = 0$ for all wavelengths; hence the central fringe is pure white.
    • On either side, since $\beta \propto \lambda$, violet fringes ($\,\lambda_v \approx 400 \text{ nm}$) form closest to the centre, followed by other colors, ending with red ($\,\lambda_r \approx 700 \text{ nm}$). Thus, a few colored fringes are observed.
    • At larger distances from the centre, multiple orders of different colors overlap completely, resulting in a uniform white illumination.

Q 10.17
Exercise 10.17
What are coherent sources of light? Why can two independent incandescent light bulbs never act as coherent sources? How are coherent sources produced in the laboratory?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Definition of Coherent Sources:
Two light sources are defined to be coherent if they emit light waves that have:
1. Exactly the same frequency (and same wavelength), and
2. A constant phase difference (zero or fixed) with respect to time.

Why Two Independent Bulbs Cannot Be Coherent:
• Light emission from an ordinary bulb occurs through spontaneous de-excitation of excited atoms.
• Individual atoms emit independent wave-packets lasting only about $10^{-8} \text{ s}$.
• After each $10^{-8} \text{ s}$, a fresh atom emits a wave-packet with an arbitrary, completely random initial phase.
• Consequently, the phase difference between waves from two independent bulbs changes abruptly millions of times per second ($10^8 \text{ times s}^{-1}$).
• The human eye and optical detectors can only record the time-averaged intensity over many cycles, washing out interference fringes into uniform illumination ($I = I_1 + I_2$).

Laboratory Production of Coherent Sources:
Coherent sources are derived from a single parent light source using two main methods: 1. Division of Wavefront: Dividing a single wavefront into two parts using two pinholes/slits (Young’s experiment), Fresnel’s biprism, or Lloyd’s mirror.
2. Division of Amplitude: Dividing the amplitude of an incident beam into reflected and transmitted parts using partial reflection at thin films (Newton's rings, thin dielectric films).
