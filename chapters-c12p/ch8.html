Exercises 8.1 to 8.15
Q 8.1
Exercise 8.1
Figure shows a capacitor made of two circular plates each of radius $12 \text{ cm}$, and separated by $5.0 \text{ mm}$. The capacitor is being charged by an external source. The charging current is constant and equal to $0.15 \text{ A}$.
(a) Calculate the capacitance and the rate of change of potential difference between the plates.
(b) Obtain the displacement current between the plates.
(c) Is Kirchhoff’s first rule (junction rule) valid at each plate of the capacitor? Explain.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Radius of plates $R = 12 \text{ cm} = 0.12 \text{ m}$
Area $A = \pi R^2 = \pi (0.12)^2 = 0.0144\pi \text{ m}^2 \approx 0.04524 \text{ m}^2$
Separation $d = 5.0 \text{ mm} = 5.0 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}$
Conduction current $I_C = 0.15 \text{ A}$
Permittivity $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \text{ C}^2 \text{ N}^{-1} \text{ m}^{-2}$

(a) Capacitance and Rate of Change of Potential:
\[ C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} = \frac{(8.854 \times 10^{-12}) \times (0.04524)}{5.0 \times 10^{-3}} \approx 8.01 \times 10^{-11} \text{ F} = 80.1 \text{ pF} \] Since $q = C V$, differentiating with respect to time gives: \[ \frac{dq}{dt} = C \frac{dV}{dt} \implies \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{I_C}{C} \] \[ \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{0.15 \text{ A}}{8.01 \times 10^{-11} \text{ F}} \approx 1.87 \times 10^9 \text{ V s}^{-1} \]

(b) Displacement Current $I_D$:
Displacement current between plates is given by: \[ I_D = \varepsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} = \varepsilon_0 \frac{d}{dt}(E A) = \varepsilon_0 A \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{V}{d}\right) = \left(\frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}\right) \frac{dV}{dt} = C \frac{dV}{dt} = I_C \] Therefore: \[ I_D = 0.15 \text{ A} \]

(c) Validity of Kirchhoff’s First Rule:
Yes. If we include both conduction current and displacement current, Kirchhoff's first rule holds strictly at each plate:
• At the positive plate, conduction current $I_C = 0.15 \text{ A}$ flows in via the wire, while an identical displacement current $I_D = 0.15 \text{ A}$ leaves the plate through the dielectric gap.
• Thus, total incoming current equals total outgoing current ($I_C = I_D$), maintaining generalized charge-continuity.

Q 8.2
Exercise 8.2
A parallel plate capacitor made of circular plates each of radius $R = 6.0 \text{ cm}$ has a capacitance $C = 100 \text{ pF}$. The capacitor is connected to a $230 \text{ V}$ ac supply with a frequency of $\omega = 300 \text{ rad s}^{-1}$.
(a) What is the rms value of the conduction current?
(b) Is the conduction current equal to the displacement current?
(c) Determine the amplitude of $B$ at a point $3.0 \text{ cm}$ from the axis between the plates.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Radius $R = 6.0 \text{ cm} = 0.06 \text{ m}$, $C = 100 \text{ pF} = 100 \times 10^{-12} \text{ F} = 10^{-10} \text{ F}$
$V_{rms} = 230 \text{ V}$, $\omega = 300 \text{ rad s}^{-1}$

(a) RMS Conduction Current $I_{rms}$:
Capacitive reactance: \[ X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C} = \frac{1}{300 \times 10^{-10}} = \frac{10^{10}}{300} = \frac{10^8}{3} \ \Omega \] RMS Current: \[ I_{rms} = \frac{V_{rms}}{X_C} = V_{rms} \omega C = 230 \times 300 \times 10^{-10} = 6.9 \times 10^{-6} \text{ A} = 6.9 \ \mu\text{A} \]

(b) Conduction Current vs Displacement Current:
Yes, at every instant the displacement current between the plates equals the conduction current in the connecting wires: \[ I_D(t) = I_C(t) \implies I_{D, rms} = I_{C, rms} = 6.9 \ \mu\text{A} \]

(c) Amplitude of Magnetic Field $B_0$ at $r = 3.0 \text{ cm}$:
Peak conduction/displacement current: \[ I_0 = \sqrt{2} I_{rms} = \sqrt{2} \times (6.9 \times 10^{-6}) \approx 9.76 \times 10^{-6} \text{ A} \] Using Ampere-Maxwell law for a circle of radius $r = 3.0 \text{ cm} < R = 6.0 \text{ cm}$: \[ \oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 I_{D, enc} = \mu_0 \left( I_0 \frac{\pi r^2}{\pi R^2} \right) = \mu_0 I_0 \frac{r^2}{R^2} \] \[ B_0 (2\pi r) = \mu_0 I_0 \frac{r^2}{R^2} \implies B_0 = \frac{\mu_0 I_0 r}{2\pi R^2} \] \[ B_0 = \left( \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi} \right) \frac{I_0 r}{R^2} = (2 \times 10^{-7}) \times \frac{(9.76 \times 10^{-6}) \times 0.03}{(0.06)^2} \] \[ B_0 = (2 \times 10^{-7}) \times \frac{2.928 \times 10^{-7}}{0.0036} = 2 \times 10^{-7} \times 8.133 \times 10^{-5} \approx 1.63 \times 10^{-11} \text{ T} \]

Q 8.3
Exercise 8.3
What physical quantity is the same for X-rays of wavelength $10^{-10} \text{ m}$, red light of wavelength $6800 \text{ \AA}$ and radiowaves of wavelength $500 \text{ m}$?
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Answer:
The physical quantity that is identical for all electromagnetic waves in vacuum is their speed of propagation in vacuum: \[ c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}} \approx 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1} \] Regardless of their wildly different wavelengths, frequencies, or photon energies, all electromagnetic radiations (X-rays, visible light, radio waves) travel with precisely the same speed $c$ through free space.

Identification of speed of light in vacuum $c = 3 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}$1 Mark
Q 8.4
Exercise 8.4
A plane electromagnetic wave travels in vacuum along the z-direction. What can you say about the directions of its electric and magnetic field vectors? If the frequency of the wave is $30 \text{ MHz}$, what is its wavelength?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

1. Directions of Electric and Magnetic Field Vectors:
Electromagnetic waves are strictly transverse in nature. The electric field $\mathbf{E}$, magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$, and the direction of wave propagation $\mathbf{k}$ (or Poynting vector $\mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{\mu_0} \mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{B}$) are mutually perpendicular.
• Since the wave travels along the $+z$-axis, both $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ must lie entirely in the $xy$-plane.
• If $\mathbf{E}$ oscillates along the $x$-axis, then $\mathbf{B}$ oscillates along the $y$-axis such that $\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{B}$ points along the $+z$-axis.

2. Wavelength Calculation:
Given frequency $\nu = 30 \text{ MHz} = 30 \times 10^6 \text{ Hz} = 3 \times 10^7 \text{ Hz}$
Speed $c = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$
\[ \lambda = \frac{c}{\nu} = \frac{3 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}}{3 \times 10^7 \text{ Hz}} = 10 \text{ m} \]

Result: The wavelength of the wave is $10 \text{ m}$ (which falls in the short-wave radio band).

Perpendicularity of $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ in $xy$-plane ($E \perp B \perp z$)1 Mark
Calculation of wavelength $\lambda = c/\nu = 10\text{ m}$1 Mark
Q 8.5
Exercise 8.5
A radio can tune in to any station in the $7.5 \text{ MHz}$ to $12 \text{ MHz}$ band. What is the corresponding wavelength band?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Minimum frequency $\nu_1 = 7.5 \text{ MHz} = 7.5 \times 10^6 \text{ Hz}$
Maximum frequency $\nu_2 = 12 \text{ MHz} = 12 \times 10^6 \text{ Hz}$
Speed of light $c = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$

Calculations:
1. Maximum wavelength (corresponding to $\nu_1$): \[ \lambda_{max} = \frac{c}{\nu_1} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{7.5 \times 10^6} = \frac{300}{7.5} = 40 \text{ m} \] 2. Minimum wavelength (corresponding to $\nu_2$): \[ \lambda_{min} = \frac{c}{\nu_2} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{12 \times 10^6} = \frac{300}{12} = 25 \text{ m} \]

Result: The corresponding wavelength band extends from $25 \text{ m}$ to $40 \text{ m}$.

Q 8.6
Exercise 8.6
A charged particle oscillates about its mean equilibrium position with a frequency of $10^9 \text{ Hz}$. What is the frequency of the electromagnetic waves produced by the oscillator?
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Answer:
An oscillating charge accelerates back and forth with frequency $\nu = 10^9 \text{ Hz}$.
According to Maxwell’s theory of electromagnetism, an accelerated or oscillating charge produces oscillating electric and magnetic fields of precisely the same frequency as the oscillating source charge.
Therefore, the frequency of the generated electromagnetic waves is: \[ \nu_{EM} = 10^9 \text{ Hz} = 1.0 \text{ GHz} \] (This frequency falls in the microwave / UHF radio region of the electromagnetic spectrum).

Q 8.7
Exercise 8.7
The amplitude of the magnetic field part of a harmonic electromagnetic wave in vacuum is $B_0 = 510 \text{ nT}$. What is the amplitude of the electric field part of the wave?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Magnetic field amplitude $B_0 = 510 \text{ nT} = 510 \times 10^{-9} \text{ T} = 5.1 \times 10^{-7} \text{ T}$
Speed of light $c = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$

Formula:
In a plane electromagnetic wave propagating in free space: \[ c = \frac{E_0}{B_0} \implies E_0 = c B_0 \]

Calculation:
\[ E_0 = (3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}) \times (5.1 \times 10^{-7} \text{ T}) = 15.3 \times 10^1 = 153 \text{ N C}^{-1} \text{ (or V m}^{-1}\text{)} \]

Result: The electric field amplitude is $153 \text{ V m}^{-1}$.

Q 8.8
Exercise 8.8
Suppose that the electric field amplitude of an electromagnetic wave is $E_0 = 120 \text{ N C}^{-1}$ and that its frequency is $\nu = 50.0 \text{ MHz}$.
(a) Determine $B_0, \omega, k$, and $\lambda$.
(b) Find expressions for $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
$E_0 = 120 \text{ N C}^{-1}$
$\nu = 50.0 \text{ MHz} = 50 \times 10^6 \text{ Hz} = 5.0 \times 10^7 \text{ Hz}$
$c = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$

(a) Determination of Parameters:
1. Magnetic field amplitude $B_0$: \[ B_0 = \frac{E_0}{c} = \frac{120}{3.0 \times 10^8} = 4.0 \times 10^{-7} \text{ T} = 400 \text{ nT} \] 2. Angular frequency $\omega$: \[ \omega = 2\pi \nu = 2\pi \times (5.0 \times 10^7) = 1.0\pi \times 10^8 \approx 3.14 \times 10^8 \text{ rad s}^{-1} \] 3. Wavelength $\lambda$: \[ \lambda = \frac{c}{\nu} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{5.0 \times 10^7} = 6.0 \text{ m} \] 4. Propagation constant (wave number) $k$: \[ k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} = \frac{2\pi}{6.0} = \frac{\pi}{3} \approx 1.05 \text{ rad m}^{-1} \]

(b) Expressions for $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ vectors:
Assuming propagation along the $+x$-direction, with $\mathbf{E}$ along $\hat{j}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ along $\hat{k}$: \[ \mathbf{E}(x, t) = E_0 \sin(kx - \omega t) \hat{j} = (120 \text{ N C}^{-1}) \sin(1.05 x - 3.14 \times 10^8 t) \hat{j} \] \[ \mathbf{B}(x, t) = B_0 \sin(kx - \omega t) \hat{k} = (4.0 \times 10^{-7} \text{ T}) \sin(1.05 x - 3.14 \times 10^8 t) \hat{k} \]

Q 8.9
Exercise 8.9
Use the formula $E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$ for energy of a photon and obtain the photon energy in units of eV for different parts of the EM spectrum:
(Radio waves, Microwaves, Infrared, Visible light, Ultraviolet, X-rays, Gamma rays).
In what way are the different scales of photon energies related to the sources of electromagnetic radiation?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Conversion Factor:
$h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}$, $c = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$, $1 \text{ eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}$
\[ E (\text{eV}) = \frac{hc}{\lambda e} = \frac{12400 \text{ eV \AA}}{\lambda (\text{\AA})} = \frac{1.24 \times 10^{-6} \text{ eV m}}{\lambda (\text{m})} \]

Region Typical $\lambda$ Photon Energy ($E$) Physical Mechanism / Source
Radio Waves $100 \text{ m}$ $\sim 10^{-8} \text{ eV}$ Macroscopic accelerating charges in LC antennas
Microwaves $1 \text{ cm}$ $\sim 10^{-4} \text{ eV}$ Special vacuum tubes (klystron, magnetron), molecular rotations
Infrared $10 \ \mu\text{m}$ $\sim 0.1 \text{ eV}$ Thermal vibrations of molecules and hot bodies
Visible Light $5000 \text{ \AA}$ $1.8 \text{ eV} - 3.1 \text{ eV}$ Outer shell valence electronic transitions in excited atoms
Ultraviolet $1000 \text{ \AA}$ $\sim 10 \text{ eV}$ Inner valence electronic jumps, sun, mercury arcs
X-Rays $1 \text{ \AA}$ $\sim 10 \text{ keV}$ Inner shell transitions in heavy atoms / bremsstrahlung
Gamma Rays $10^{-12} \text{ m}$ $\sim 1 \text{ MeV}$ Nuclear energy level de-excitations and radioactive decay

Significance: The photon energies correspond directly to the quantum energy level spacings of the radiating physical systems (radio: macroscopic, infrared: molecular, visible/UV: atomic valence, X-rays: inner atomic, gamma: nuclear).

Q 8.10
Exercise 8.10
In a plane electromagnetic wave, the electric field oscillates sinusoidally at a frequency of $2.0 \times 10^{10} \text{ Hz}$ and amplitude $48 \text{ V m}^{-1}$.
(a) What is the wavelength of the wave?
(b) What is the amplitude of the oscillating magnetic field?
(c) Show that the average energy density of the $\mathbf{E}$ field equals the average energy density of the $\mathbf{B}$ field.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Frequency $\nu = 2.0 \times 10^{10} \text{ Hz}$
$E_0 = 48 \text{ V m}^{-1}$
$c = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$

(a) Wavelength $\lambda$:
\[ \lambda = \frac{c}{\nu} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{2.0 \times 10^{10}} = 1.5 \times 10^{-2} \text{ m} = 1.5 \text{ cm} \] (Microwave region).

(b) Magnetic Field Amplitude $B_0$:
\[ B_0 = \frac{E_0}{c} = \frac{48}{3.0 \times 10^8} = 1.6 \times 10^{-7} \text{ T} = 160 \text{ nT} \]

(c) Equipartition of Average Energy Density ($u_E = u_B$):
• Average electric energy density: \[ u_E = \frac{1}{4} \varepsilon_0 E_0^2 \] • Average magnetic energy density: \[ u_B = \frac{1}{4} \frac{B_0^2}{\mu_0} \] Using $E_0 = c B_0$ and $c^2 = \frac{1}{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}$: \[ u_E = \frac{1}{4} \varepsilon_0 (c B_0)^2 = \frac{1}{4} \varepsilon_0 c^2 B_0^2 = \frac{1}{4} \varepsilon_0 \left( \frac{1}{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0} \right) B_0^2 = \frac{1}{4} \frac{B_0^2}{\mu_0} = u_B \] Hence, exactly half of the total electromagnetic energy density is carried by the electric field and the remaining half by the magnetic field.

Q 8.11
Exercise 8.11
About $5\%$ of the power of a $100 \text{ W}$ light bulb is converted to visible radiation. What is the average intensity of visible radiation:
(a) at a distance of $1.0 \text{ m}$ from the bulb?
(b) at a distance of $10 \text{ m}$?
Assume that the radiation is emitted isotropically and neglect reflection.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Total power $P = 100 \text{ W}$
Efficiency in visible spectrum $\eta = 5\%$
Useful radiant power $P_{vis} = 0.05 \times 100 \text{ W} = 5.0 \text{ W}$

Formula for Isotropic Emission:
The radiation spreads uniformly across a spherical wavefront of radius $r$: \[ I = \frac{P_{vis}}{4\pi r^2} \]

(a) At Distance $r_1 = 1.0 \text{ m}$:
\[ I_1 = \frac{5.0}{4\pi (1.0)^2} = \frac{5.0}{4\pi} = \frac{5.0}{12.566} \approx 0.398 \text{ W m}^{-2} \approx 0.40 \text{ W m}^{-2} \]

(b) At Distance $r_2 = 10 \text{ m}$:
Since intensity obeys the inverse square law ($I \propto 1/r^2$): \[ I_2 = I_1 \times \left( \frac{r_1}{r_2} \right)^2 = 0.398 \times \left( \frac{1}{10} \right)^2 = \frac{0.398}{100} \approx 3.98 \times 10^{-3} \text{ W m}^{-2} \]

Q 8.12
Exercise 8.12
Use the formula $\lambda_m T = 0.29 \text{ cm K}$ (Wien’s displacement law) to obtain the characteristic temperature ranges for different parts of the electromagnetic spectrum. What do the numbers that you obtain tell you?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Wien's Displacement Law:
\[ \lambda_m T = b = 0.29 \text{ cm K} = 2.9 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m K} \implies T = \frac{2.9 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m K}}{\lambda_m} \]

Calculations for Spectral Bands:
1. Visible Region ($\lambda_m \approx 5000 \text{ \AA} = 5 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}$): \[ T = \frac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{5 \times 10^{-7}} = 5800 \text{ K} \] (This corresponds closely to the surface temperature of the Sun, explaining why our eyes evolved to be most sensitive to visible light).
2. Infrared Region ($\lambda_m \approx 10^{-5} \text{ m}$): \[ T = \frac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{10^{-5}} = 290 \text{ K} \] (This corresponds to typical ambient terrestrial temperatures and human body temperature $\approx 300 \text{ K}$, indicating that everyday objects radiate predominantly in the infrared).
3. Microwave Region ($\lambda_m \approx 10^{-1} \text{ m} = 10 \text{ cm}$): \[ T = \frac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{0.1} = 0.029 \text{ K} \approx 10^{-2} \text{ K} \] (Deep cosmic background / cryogenic temperatures).
4. X-ray Region ($\lambda_m \approx 10^{-10} \text{ m}$): \[ T = \frac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{10^{-10}} \approx 2.9 \times 10^7 \text{ K} \] (Extreme temperatures found only in stellar cores and supernova explosions).

Conclusion: The calculated temperatures reveal the physical thermal conditions necessary for a black body to radiate maximally in each respective frequency domain.

Q 8.13
Exercise 8.13
Given below are some famous numbers associated with electromagnetic radiations in different contexts in physics. State the part of the em spectrum to which each belongs:
(a) $21 \text{ cm}$ (wavelength emitted by atomic hydrogen in interstellar space).
(b) $1057 \text{ MHz}$ (frequency of radiation arising from two close energy levels in hydrogen; known as Lamb shift).
(c) $2.7 \text{ K}$ (temperature associated with isotropic cosmic microwave background).
(d) $5890 \text{ \AA} - 5896 \text{ \AA}$ (double lines of sodium).
(e) $14.4 \text{ keV}$ (energy of a particular transition in $^{57}\text{Fe}$ nucleus associated with Mössbauer effect).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Classification:

  1. (a) $21 \text{ cm}$ line:
    $\lambda = 0.21 \text{ m}$. This belongs to the Radio wave (short radio wave / microwave) region of the spectrum (neutral hydrogen hyperfine spin-flip transition).
  2. (b) $1057 \text{ MHz}$ Lamb shift:
    $\nu = 1.057 \times 10^9 \text{ Hz} \implies \lambda = \frac{c}{\nu} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{1.057 \times 10^9} \approx 0.28 \text{ m} = 28 \text{ cm}$.
    This belongs to the Radio wave (UHF band) region.
  3. (c) $2.7 \text{ K}$ Cosmic Microwave Background:
    By Wien's law $\lambda_m = \frac{0.29 \text{ cm K}}{2.7 \text{ K}} \approx 0.11 \text{ cm} = 1.1 \text{ mm}$.
    This belongs to the Microwave region.
  4. (d) $5890 \text{ \AA} - 5896 \text{ \AA}$ Sodium D-lines:
    $\lambda \approx 589 \text{ nm}$. This falls squarely within the Visible light (yellow) region.
  5. (e) $14.4 \text{ keV}$ $^{57}\text{Fe}$ transition:
    $\lambda = \frac{hc}{E} = \frac{12400 \text{ eV \AA}}{14400 \text{ eV}} \approx 0.86 \text{ \AA} = 8.6 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}$.
    Since it originates from nuclear transitions, it is officially classified as Gamma rays (or very hard X-rays).

Q 8.14
Exercise 8.14
Answer the following conceptual questions:
(a) Long distance radio broadcasts use short-wave bands. Why?
(b) It is necessary to use satellites for long distance TV transmission and microwave communication. Why?
(c) Optical and radio telescopes are built on the ground, but X-ray astronomy is possible only from satellites orbiting the earth. Why?
(d) The small ozone layer on top of the stratosphere is crucial for human survival. Why?
(e) If the earth did not have an atmosphere, would its average surface temperature be higher or lower than what it is now?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

(a) Short-wave bands for long-distance broadcasts:
Short-wave radio frequencies ($3 \text{ MHz}$ to $30 \text{ MHz}$) are reflected back to Earth by the charged layers of the ionosphere (via total internal reflection in the ionized plasma). This enables long-distance transmission around the curvature of the earth via successive sky-wave reflections.

(b) Satellites for TV and microwave transmissions:
Television broadcast signals operate at VHF and UHF frequencies ($54 \text{ MHz}$ to $890 \text{ MHz}$). At these high frequencies, waves easily penetrate straight through the ionosphere without reflecting. Therefore, geostationary artificial satellites are required to receive, amplify, and retransmit the signals back over large terrestrial areas.

(c) Space satellites for X-ray astronomy:
Earth's atmosphere is transparent to visible light and radio waves (atmospheric optical and radio windows). However, the upper atmosphere completely absorbs high-energy X-rays and gamma rays through photoelectric absorption and Compton scattering. Thus, X-ray telescopes must be placed aboard satellites orbiting outside the atmosphere.

(d) Importance of Stratospheric Ozone:
The stratospheric ozone layer ($O_3$) strongly absorbs lethal solar ultraviolet-B (UV-B) and UV-C radiations ($200 - 315 \text{ nm}$), protecting biological organisms from DNA cellular mutations, skin cancer, and cataracts.

(e) Earth’s temperature without atmosphere:
It would be significantly lower (around $-18^\circ\text{C}$ instead of the present $+15^\circ\text{C}$). Greenhouse gases ($CO_2$, water vapour) trap outgoing terrestrial infrared radiation, keeping the planet warm via the natural greenhouse effect.

Q 8.15
Exercise 8.15
State Maxwell’s four equations in integral form for vacuum. Explain the physical significance of each equation. Describe how Maxwell modified Ampere’s circuital law by introducing the displacement current.
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Maxwell's Four Fundamental Equations of Electromagnetism:

  1. Gauss's Law for Electrostatics: \[ \oint \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{q_{enc}}{\varepsilon_0} \] Significance: States that electric flux through any closed surface is proportional to the total net enclosed electric charge. It implies that electric charges act as sources and sinks of electric fields.
  2. Gauss's Law for Magnetism: \[ \oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = 0 \] Significance: The net magnetic flux through any closed Gaussian surface is always zero. This establishes that isolated magnetic monopoles do not exist; magnetic field lines form continuous closed loops.
  3. Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction: \[ \oint \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = -\frac{d}{dt} \int \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{A} \] Significance: A time-varying magnetic flux produces an induced non-electrostatic, non-conservative electric field.
  4. Ampere-Maxwell Law: \[ \oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 (I_C + I_D) = \mu_0 I_C + \mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \] Significance: Magnetic fields are generated not only by conduction electric currents $I_C$, but also by time-varying electric fields (displacement current $I_D$).

Maxwell’s Modification of Ampere’s Law:
Ampere's original law $\oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 I_C$ was inconsistent with the continuity equation when applied to a charging capacitor.
If a closed loop surrounds the wire leading to a plate, $\oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 I_C$. But for a flat surface passing between the plates where no conduction current exists, Ampere's law predicted $\oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$, leading to a physical contradiction.
Maxwell resolved this by recognizing that between the plates, the changing electric field $\mathbf{E}$ creates a displacement current: \[ I_D = \varepsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \] This term ensures that the total current $I = I_C + I_D$ is continuous across any closed boundary, completing the symmetry of electromagnetic theory.
