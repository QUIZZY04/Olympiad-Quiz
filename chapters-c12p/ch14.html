Q 14.1
Exercise 14.1
In an n-type silicon, which of the following statements is true:
(a) Electrons are majority carriers and trivalent atoms are the dopants.
(b) Electrons are minority carriers and pentavalent atoms are the dopants.
(c) Holes are minority carriers and pentavalent atoms are the dopants.
(d) Holes are majority carriers and trivalent atoms are the dopants.
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Correct Option: (c) Holes are minority carriers and pentavalent atoms are the dopants.

Explanation:
• An n-type semiconductor is created by doping tetravalent pure silicon with pentavalent impurity atoms (such as Phosphorus, Arsenic, or Antimony) that possess five valence electrons.
• Four valence electrons form covalent bonds with neighbouring silicon atoms, while the fifth weakly bound electron becomes free into the conduction band at room temperature.
• Consequently, electrons are majority charge carriers ($n_e \gg n_h$) and holes are minority charge carriers.

Q 14.2
Exercise 14.2
Which of the statements given in Exercise 14.1 is true for p-type semiconductors?
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Correct Option: (d) Holes are majority carriers and trivalent atoms are the dopants.

Explanation:
• A p-type semiconductor is created by doping tetravalent silicon with trivalent impurity atoms (such as Boron, Aluminium, or Indium) possessing three valence electrons.
• The deficiency of one electron to complete the four covalent bonds creates an electron vacancy or hole in the valence band.
• Thermal generation adds few electron-hole pairs, making holes the majority carriers ($n_h \gg n_e$) and electrons the minority carriers.

Q 14.3
Exercise 14.3
Carbon, silicon and germanium have four valence electrons each. These are characterised by valence and conduction bands separated by energy band gaps respectively equal to $(E_g)_C, (E_g)_{Si}$ and $(E_g)_{Ge}$. Which of the following statements is true?
(a) $(E_g)_{Si} < (E_g)_{Ge} < (E_g)_C$
(b) $(E_g)_C < (E_g)_{Ge} > (E_g)_{Si}$
(c) $(E_g)_C > (E_g)_{Si} > (E_g)_{Ge}$
(d) $(E_g)_C = (E_g)_{Si} = (E_g)_{Ge}$
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Correct Option: (c) $(E_g)_C > (E_g)_{Si} > (E_g)_{Ge}$

Physical Explanation:
• Carbon (diamond structure) has its valence electrons in the small $n = 2$ shell, held extremely tightly by the nucleus, resulting in an enormous band gap $(E_g)_C \approx 5.4 \text{ eV}$ (insulator).
• Silicon has valence electrons in the larger $n = 3$ shell, with $(E_g)_{Si} \approx 1.1 \text{ eV}$ (semiconductor).
• Germanium has valence electrons in the even larger $n = 4$ shell, shielded by inner shells, yielding $(E_g)_{Ge} \approx 0.7 \text{ eV}$ (semiconductor).
As we move down Group 14 of the periodic table, the atomic radius increases and bond energy decreases, causing the band gap $E_g$ to decrease monotonically.

Q 14.4
Exercise 14.4
In an unbiased p-n junction, holes diffuse from the p-region to the n-region because:
(a) free electrons in the n-region attract them.
(b) they move across the junction by the potential barrier.
(c) hole concentration in the p-region is more as compared to n-region.
(d) All the above.
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Correct Option: (c) hole concentration in the p-region is more as compared to n-region.

Explanation:
• Diffusion is a macroscopic statistical transport phenomenon driven strictly by a spatial concentration gradient ($dn/dx$).
• In a p-n junction, the concentration of holes in the p-side is orders of magnitude greater than in the n-side ($p_p \gg p_n$).
• Therefore, holes naturally diffuse across the metallurgical junction from the region of higher concentration (p-side) to the region of lower concentration (n-side).
(Note: The built-in potential barrier actually opposes this diffusion, while electron attraction is not the driving mechanism).

Q 14.5
Exercise 14.5
When a forward bias is applied to a p-n junction, it:
(a) raises the potential barrier.
(b) reduces the majority carrier current to zero.
(c) lowers the potential barrier.
(d) None of the above.
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Correct Option: (c) lowers the potential barrier.

Explanation:
• In forward bias, the positive terminal of the external battery is connected to the p-side and the negative terminal to the n-side.
• The applied electric field opposes the internal built-in electric field of the barrier ($E_{app}$ opposes $E_{barrier}$).
• Consequently, the effective barrier height is reduced from $V_0$ to $(V_0 - V)$, and the depletion layer width shrinks, allowing majority carriers to diffuse freely across the junction and establish a large forward current.

Q 14.6
Exercise 14.6
In half-wave rectification, what is the output frequency if the input ac frequency is $50 \text{ Hz}$? What is the output frequency of a full-wave rectifier for the same input frequency?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

1. Half-Wave Rectifier:
• In a half-wave rectifier, current flows through the load only during the positive half-cycles of the AC input voltage, while the negative half-cycles are blocked.
• Thus, the pulsating DC output completes one ripple cycle for every single full cycle of the AC input.
• Output frequency: \[ f_{out} = f_{in} = 50 \text{ Hz} \]

2. Full-Wave Rectifier:
• In a full-wave rectifier (centre-tapped or bridge configuration), both positive and negative half-cycles of the AC input are converted into unidirectional output pulses across the load.
• Thus, there are two output pulses for every single input AC cycle.
• Output frequency: \[ f_{out} = 2 f_{in} = 2 \times 50 \text{ Hz} = 100 \text{ Hz} \]

Q 14.7
Exercise 14.7
A p-n photodiode is fabricated from a semiconductor with a band gap of $2.8 \text{ eV}$. Can it detect a wavelength of $6000 \text{ nm}$?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Band gap $E_g = 2.8 \text{ eV}$
Incident wavelength $\lambda = 6000 \text{ nm} = 6000 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m} = 6.0 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}$

Step 1: Energy of the Incident Photon $E$:
\[ E = \frac{h c}{\lambda} = \frac{12400 \text{ eV \AA}}{60000 \text{ \AA}} = \frac{12400}{60000} \approx 0.207 \text{ eV} \] Alternatively using SI units: \[ E = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}) \times (3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1})}{6.0 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}} = 3.313 \times 10^{-20} \text{ J} \] \[ E = \frac{3.313 \times 10^{-20} \text{ J}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J eV}^{-1}} \approx 0.207 \text{ eV} \]

Step 2: Threshold Condition for Photodetection:
For a photodiode to detect radiation, the incident photon must possess sufficient energy to excite an electron from the valence band to the conduction band across the forbidden energy gap: \[ E \ge E_g \] Here $E = 0.207 \text{ eV} \ll E_g = 2.8 \text{ eV}$.

Conclusion: No, the photodiode cannot detect this radiation. (Maximum detectable wavelength is $\lambda_{max} = \frac{12400}{2.8} \approx 4429 \text{ \AA} = 443 \text{ nm}$).

Q 14.8
Exercise 14.8
The number of silicon atoms per $\text{m}^3$ is $5 \times 10^{28}$. This is doped simultaneously with $5 \times 10^{22} \text{ atoms m}^{-3}$ of Arsenic and $5 \times 10^{20} \text{ atoms m}^{-3}$ of Indium. Calculate the number of electrons and holes. Given that $n_i = 1.5 \times 10^{16} \text{ m}^{-3}$. Is the material n-type or p-type?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Donor concentration (Arsenic, pentavalent) $N_D = 5 \times 10^{22} \text{ m}^{-3}$
Acceptor concentration (Indium, trivalent) $N_A = 5 \times 10^{20} \text{ m}^{-3}$
Intrinsic carrier concentration $n_i = 1.5 \times 10^{16} \text{ m}^{-3}$

Step 1: Net Donor Concentration ($n_e$):
Since $N_D \gg N_A$, the acceptors compensate an equal number of donors: \[ n_e \approx N_D - N_A = 5 \times 10^{22} - 0.05 \times 10^{22} = 4.95 \times 10^{22} \text{ m}^{-3} \]

Step 2: Number of Holes ($n_h$):
Applying the Mass-Action Law for semiconductors in thermal equilibrium: \[ n_e n_h = n_i^2 \implies n_h = \frac{n_i^2}{n_e} \] \[ n_h = \frac{(1.5 \times 10^{16})^2}{4.95 \times 10^{22}} = \frac{2.25 \times 10^{32}}{4.95 \times 10^{22}} \approx 4.545 \times 10^9 \text{ m}^{-3} \]

Type of Material:
Since $n_e \approx 4.95 \times 10^{22} \text{ m}^{-3} \gg n_h \approx 4.55 \times 10^9 \text{ m}^{-3}$, the material is strongly n-type.

Q 14.9
Exercise 14.9
In an intrinsic semiconductor the energy gap $E_g$ is $1.2 \text{ eV}$. Its hole mobility is much smaller than electron mobility and independent of temperature. What is the ratio between conductivity at $600 \text{ K}$ and that at $300 \text{ K}$? Assume that the temperature dependence of intrinsic carrier concentration is given by $n_i = n_0 \exp\left(-\frac{E_g}{2 k_B T}\right)$.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
$E_g = 1.2 \text{ eV}$, $T_1 = 300 \text{ K}$, $T_2 = 600 \text{ K}$
Boltzmann constant $k_B = 8.617 \times 10^{-5} \text{ eV K}^{-1}$

Conductivity Relation:
Conductivity $\sigma = e n_i (\mu_e + \mu_h)$. Since mobilities are independent of temperature: \[ \frac{\sigma(T_2)}{\sigma(T_1)} = \frac{n_i(T_2)}{n_i(T_1)} = \frac{\exp\left(-\frac{E_g}{2 k_B T_2}\right)}{\exp\left(-\frac{E_g}{2 k_B T_1}\right)} = \exp\left[ \frac{E_g}{2 k_B} \left( \frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2} \right) \right] \]

Calculation:
\[ \frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{300} - \frac{1}{600} = \frac{1}{600} \text{ K}^{-1} \] Exponent factor: \[ x = \frac{E_g}{2 k_B} \left( \frac{1}{600} \right) = \frac{1.2 \text{ eV}}{2 \times (8.617 \times 10^{-5} \text{ eV K}^{-1}) \times 600 \text{ K}} = \frac{1.2}{0.1034} \approx 11.605 \] \[ \frac{\sigma(600 \text{ K})}{\sigma(300 \text{ K})} = e^{11.605} \approx 1.096 \times 10^5 \approx 1.1 \times 10^5 \]

Result: The conductivity increases by a factor of approximately $1.1 \times 10^5$ (over 100,000 times) as temperature increases from $300 \text{ K}$ to $600 \text{ K}$.

Q 14.10
Exercise 14.10
In a p-n junction diode, the current $I$ can be expressed as: \[ I = I_0 \left[ \exp\left(\frac{e V}{k_B T}\right) - 1 \right] \] where $I_0 = 5.0 \times 10^{-12} \text{ A}$ is the reverse saturation current, $V$ is the voltage across the diode. Estimate the dynamic resistance of the diode at $T = 300 \text{ K}$ for:
(a) a forward bias of $0.6 \text{ V}$,
(b) a forward bias of $0.7 \text{ V}$.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Thermal Voltage:
At $T = 300 \text{ K}$, $V_T = \frac{k_B T}{e} = \frac{(1.38 \times 10^{-23}) \times 300}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}} \approx 0.02585 \text{ V} \approx 26 \text{ mV}$.

Dynamic Resistance ($r_d$):
Differentiating the diode equation with respect to $V$: \[ \frac{dI}{dV} = I_0 \left( \frac{e}{k_B T} \right) \exp\left(\frac{e V}{k_B T}\right) = \frac{I + I_0}{V_T} \approx \frac{I}{V_T} \quad (\text{for forward bias where } I \gg I_0) \] Dynamic resistance is: \[ r_d = \frac{dV}{dI} \approx \frac{V_T}{I} \]

(a) For $V = 0.6 \text{ V}$:
\[ I = (5.0 \times 10^{-12}) \times \exp\left( \frac{0.6}{0.02585} \right) = 5.0 \times 10^{-12} \times e^{23.21} \approx 5.0 \times 10^{-12} \times 1.202 \times 10^{10} \approx 0.0601 \text{ A} = 60.1 \text{ mA} \] \[ r_d = \frac{V_T}{I} = \frac{0.02585 \text{ V}}{0.0601 \text{ A}} \approx 0.43 \ \Omega \]

(b) For $V = 0.7 \text{ V}$:
\[ I = (5.0 \times 10^{-12}) \times \exp\left( \frac{0.7}{0.02585} \right) \approx 2.87 \text{ A} \] \[ r_d = \frac{0.02585 \text{ V}}{2.87 \text{ A}} \approx 0.009 \ \Omega = 9 \text{ m}\Omega \]

Conclusion: The dynamic resistance drops sharply as forward bias voltage exceeds the threshold / knee voltage.

Q 14.11
Exercise 14.11
Explain the action of a Zener diode as a DC voltage regulator with the help of a neat labelled circuit diagram and its reverse I-V characteristic curve.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Principle of Zener Voltage Regulator:
A Zener diode is a heavily doped silicon p-n junction diode designed to operate continuously in the reverse breakdown region (Zener breakdown voltage $V_Z$) without getting damaged.
In this region, the voltage across the Zener diode remains essentially constant at $V_Z$ over a wide range of reverse currents.

Circuit Arrangement:
• An unregulated fluctuating DC input voltage $V_i$ ($V_i > V_Z$) is connected across the series combination of a current-limiting resistor $R_s$ and the Zener diode (connected in reverse bias).
• The load resistance $R_L$ is connected in parallel across the Zener diode, so output voltage $V_o = V_Z$.

Working Mechanism:
1. If Input Voltage $V_i$ Increases:
The current through the circuit increases. Because the Zener diode dynamic resistance is virtually zero in breakdown, the Zener current $I_Z$ increases sharply while the voltage across it remains fixed at $V_Z$. The excess voltage drop appears entirely across the series resistor $R_s$ ($Delta V_i = Delta I R_s$). Hence, load voltage $V_o = V_Z$ remains constant.
2. If Load Resistance $R_L$ Changes:
If load current $I_L$ increases, the Zener diode automatically decreases its current $I_Z$ such that total current $I = I_Z + I_L$ across $R_s$ stays constant, preserving constant load voltage $V_Z$.

Q 14.12
Exercise 14.12
Why are elemental semiconductors limited to Silicon and Germanium, while Carbon is an insulator? Explain on the basis of atomic structure and bond energy.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Atomic Structure of Group 14 Elements:
Carbon ($Z = 6$), Silicon ($Z = 14$), and Germanium ($Z = 32$) all have four valence electrons in their outermost shell ($s^2 p^2$).

Comparison:

  1. Carbon ($n = 2$):
    The valence electrons are in the $n = 2$ shell, extremely close to the positively charged nucleus. The nuclear attraction is exceptionally strong. Consequently, the energy required to break a carbon-carbon covalent bond and liberate an electron into the conduction band is very high ($E_g \approx 5.4 \text{ eV}$). At room temperature, thermal energy ($k_B T \approx 0.026 \text{ eV}$) is completely incapable of exciting electrons into the conduction band. Hence, Carbon (diamond) is an insulator.
  2. Silicon ($n = 3$) and Germanium ($n = 4$):
    In Silicon and Germanium, the valence electrons occupy larger shells ($n = 3$ and $n = 4$). The valence electrons are screened by inner core shells and are located much further from the nucleus, resulting in much smaller band gaps: \[ E_g(\text{Si}) = 1.1 \text{ eV}, \quad E_g(\text{Ge}) = 0.72 \text{ eV} \] At room temperature, thermal agitation is sufficient to break a measurable fraction of covalent bonds, exciting electrons into the conduction band and creating holes in the valence band. Thus, they exhibit appreciable electrical conductivity and act as semiconductors.

Q 14.13
Exercise 14.13
Distinguish between metals, insulators, and semiconductors on the basis of energy band theory. Illustrate with neat schematic energy band diagrams.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Band Theory Classification:
The electrical conductivity of solids is determined by the width of the forbidden energy band gap ($E_g$) separating the completely or partially filled valence band (VB) and the empty conduction band (CB).

  1. Metals (Conductors):
    • Either the conduction band is partially filled, or the valence band and conduction band overlap ($E_g = 0$).
    • A large density of free electrons ($sim 10^{28} \text{ m}^{-3}$) is readily available to move under any applied electric field.
    • Electrical resistivity is extremely low ($\rho \sim 10^{-8} \ \Omega\text{ m}$).
  2. Insulators:
    • The valence band is completely full, the conduction band is completely empty, and the two are separated by a very large forbidden energy gap ($E_g > 3 \text{ eV}$, e.g. diamond $5.4 \text{ eV}$).
    • Room temperature thermal energy cannot bridge this massive gap, so no free electrons exist in the conduction band.
    • Resistivity is extremely high ($\rho \sim 10^{11} - 10^{19} \ \Omega\text{ m}$).
  3. Semiconductors:
    • The valence band is full and conduction band is empty at absolute zero ($0 \text{ K}$), where they behave as ideal insulators.
    • They possess a small forbidden energy gap ($E_g < 3 \text{ eV}$, e.g., $\text{Si} = 1.1 \text{ eV}, \text{Ge} = 0.7 \text{ eV}$).
    • At room temperature, thermal excitation promotes a modest number of electrons into the CB, leaving equal holes in the VB.
    • Resistivity is intermediate ($\rho \sim 10^{-5} - 10^6 \ \Omega\text{ m}$) and decreases exponentially with rising temperature ($d\rho/dT < 0$).

Q 14.14
Exercise 14.14
Explain the formation of the depletion region and barrier potential across an unbiased p-n junction with the help of a neat diagram.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Formation of P-N Junction:
When a p-type semiconductor is joined to an n-type semiconductor at atomic level:

  1. Diffusion Current:
    Due to the steep concentration gradient, majority holes from the p-side diffuse into the n-side, and majority electrons from the n-side diffuse into the p-side across the metallurgical junction.
  2. Formation of Depletion Layer:
    • When an electron diffuses into the p-side, it leaves behind an uncompensated, immobile positive donor ion ($\text{P}^+$ or $\text{As}^+$) on the n-side near the junction.
    • Similarly, when a hole diffuses into the n-side, it leaves behind an uncompensated, immobile negative acceptor ion ($\text{B}^-$ or $\text{In}^-$) on the p-side near the junction.
    • This layer of immobile space charges devoid of any free mobile charge carriers is called the depletion region (thickness $\sim 0.5 - 1 \ \mu\text{m}$).
  3. Barrier Potential ($V_0$):
    • The positive donor ions on the n-side and negative acceptor ions on the p-side set up an internal electric field $\mathbf{E}_{int}$ directed from the n-side to the p-side.
    • This internal electric field opposes further diffusion of majority carriers across the junction.
    • It creates a potential difference across the junction known as the barrier potential ($V_0 \approx 0.7 \text{ V}$ for Silicon, $0.3 \text{ V}$ for Germanium).
  4. Equilibrium Condition:
    The internal field sweeps minority carriers across the junction (drift current). Equilibrium is attained when: \[ I_{\text{diffusion}} = I_{\text{drift}} \] resulting in zero net current through an unbiased p-n junction.

Q 14.15
Exercise 14.15
With the help of a circuit diagram, explain the working of a full-wave rectifier using two p-n junction diodes. Draw the input and output waveforms and explain the role of a capacitor filter.
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Working of Full-Wave Centre-Tapped Rectifier:
The circuit comprises a step-down transformer whose secondary winding has a centre tap ($CT$) connected to the ground, two p-n junction diodes ($D_1$ and $D_2$), and a load resistance $R_L$.

  1. During Positive Half-Cycle of AC Input:
    • Upper terminal $A$ is positive with respect to centre tap $CT$, and lower terminal $B$ is negative.
    • Diode $D_1$ is forward biased and conducts, while diode $D_2$ is reverse biased and acts as an open circuit.
    • Current flows through $D_1$, through load $R_L$ from $X$ to $Y$, and returns to the centre tap.
  2. During Negative Half-Cycle of AC Input:
    • Terminal $A$ becomes negative with respect to $CT$, and terminal $B$ becomes positive.
    • Diode $D_1$ is reverse biased (cut off), while diode $D_2$ is forward biased and conducts.
    • Current flows through $D_2$, through load $R_L$ in the same direction (from $X$ to $Y$), and returns to $CT$.
  3. Result:
    Current flows through the load resistor $R_L$ in the same direction during both half-cycles of the AC input, achieving full-wave rectification. Output ripple frequency is $2f_{in}$ ($100 \text{ Hz}$ for $50 \text{ Hz}$ mains).

Role of Capacitor Filter:
• A large electrolytic capacitor $C$ is connected in parallel across the load resistor $R_L$.
• As the rectified voltage rises, the capacitor charges rapidly to the peak voltage $V_m$.
• When the diode output voltage begins to fall, the diode turns off, and the capacitor discharges slowly through $R_L$ (time constant $R_L C \gg T/2$).
• This prevents the voltage from dropping to zero between pulses, smoothing the pulsating DC into a steady DC output with minimal ripple factor.
