Q 13.1
Exercise 13.1
(a) Two stable isotopes of lithium $^{6}_3\text{Li}$ and $^{7}_3\text{Li}$ have respective abundances of $7.5\%$ and $92.5\%$. These isotopes have masses $6.01512 \text{ u}$ and $7.01600 \text{ u}$, respectively. Find the atomic mass of lithium.
(b) Boron has two stable isotopes, $^{10}_5\text{B}$ and $^{11}_5\text{B}$. Their respective masses are $10.01294 \text{ u}$ and $11.00931 \text{ u}$, and the atomic mass of boron is $10.811 \text{ u}$. Find the abundances of $^{10}_5\text{B}$ and $^{11}_5\text{B}$.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

(a) Atomic Mass of Lithium:
The weighted average atomic mass is: \[ m = \frac{\sum f_i m_i}{\sum f_i} = \frac{(7.5 \times 6.01512) + (92.5 \times 7.01600)}{100} \] \[ m = \frac{45.1134 + 648.9800}{100} = \frac{694.0934}{100} \approx 6.941 \text{ u} \]

(b) Abundances of Boron Isotopes:
Let the fractional abundance of $^{10}_5\text{B}$ be $x$. Then the abundance of $^{11}_5\text{B}$ is $(100 - x)$.
\[ 10.811 = \frac{x(10.01294) + (100 - x)(11.00931)}{100} \] \[ 1081.1 = 10.01294 x + 1100.931 - 11.00931 x \] \[ (11.00931 - 10.01294) x = 1100.931 - 1081.1 \] \[ 0.99637 x = 19.831 \implies x = \frac{19.831}{0.99637} \approx 19.90\% \] Abundance of $^{11}_5\text{B} = 100 - 19.90 = 80.10\%$.

Result: Abundance of $^{10}_5\text{B} = 19.9\%$ and $^{11}_5\text{B} = 80.1\%$.

Q 13.2
Exercise 13.2
The three stable isotopes of neon: $^{20}_{10}\text{Ne}, ^{21}_{10}\text{Ne}$ and $^{22}_{10}\text{Ne}$ have respective abundances of $90.51\%, 0.27\%$ and $9.22\%$. The atomic masses of the three isotopes are $19.99 \text{ u}, 20.99 \text{ u}$ and $21.99 \text{ u}$, respectively. Obtain the average atomic mass of neon.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Calculation:
\[ m_{avg} = \frac{(90.51 \times 19.99) + (0.27 \times 20.99) + (9.22 \times 21.99)}{100} \] \[ 90.51 \times 19.99 = 1809.2949 \] \[ 0.27 \times 20.99 = 5.6673 \] \[ 9.22 \times 21.99 = 202.7478 \] Sum: \[ \text{Total} = 1809.2949 + 5.6673 + 202.7478 = 2017.71 \text{ u} \] \[ m_{avg} = \frac{2017.71}{100} \approx 20.18 \text{ u} \]

Result: The average atomic mass of neon is $20.18 \text{ u}$.

Q 13.3
Exercise 13.3
Find the energy-equivalent of one atomic mass unit ($1 \text{ u}$) in Joules and in MeV.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Definition:
$1 \text{ u} = \frac{1}{12} \times \text{mass of a } ^{12}\text{C atom} = 1.660539 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg}$.
Speed of light $c = 2.997925 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$.

1. Energy in Joules ($J$):
By Einstein’s mass-energy equivalence $E = m c^2$: \[ E = (1.660539 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg}) \times (2.997925 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1})^2 \] \[ E = 1.660539 \times 10^{-27} \times 8.98755 \times 10^{16} \approx 1.4924 \times 10^{-10} \text{ J} \]

2. Energy in MeV:
Since $1 \text{ eV} = 1.602176 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J} \implies 1 \text{ MeV} = 1.602176 \times 10^{-13} \text{ J}$: \[ E = \frac{1.492418 \times 10^{-10} \text{ J}}{1.602176 \times 10^{-13} \text{ J MeV}^{-1}} \approx 931.49 \text{ MeV} \approx 931.5 \text{ MeV} \]

Result: $1 \text{ u} \equiv 1.492 \times 10^{-10} \text{ J} \equiv 931.5 \text{ MeV}$.

Q 13.4
Exercise 13.4
The nucleus $^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}$ has a mass of $55.934939 \text{ u}$. Find its binding energy and binding energy per nucleon.
(Given: $m_p = 1.007825 \text{ u}, m_n = 1.008665 \text{ u}$, $1 \text{ u} = 931.5 \text{ MeV}$).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Atomic number $Z = 26$, Mass number $A = 56$, Neutron number $N = A - Z = 30$
Nuclear mass $M(^{56}\text{Fe}) = 55.934939 \text{ u}$

Step 1: Total Mass of Constituent Nucleons:
Mass of 26 protons: \[ 26 m_p = 26 \times 1.007825 = 26.203450 \text{ u} \] Mass of 30 neutrons: \[ 30 m_n = 30 \times 1.008665 = 30.259950 \text{ u} \] Sum of nucleon masses: \[ 26 m_p + 30 m_n = 26.203450 + 30.259950 = 56.463400 \text{ u} \]

Step 2: Mass Defect $\Delta M$:
\[ \Delta M = (26 m_p + 30 m_n) - M = 56.463400 - 55.934939 = 0.528461 \text{ u} \]

Step 3: Binding Energy $E_b$:
\[ E_b = \Delta M \times 931.5 \text{ MeV} = 0.528461 \times 931.5 \approx 492.26 \text{ MeV} \]

Step 4: Binding Energy per Nucleon $E_{bn}$:
\[ E_{bn} = \frac{E_b}{A} = \frac{492.26 \text{ MeV}}{56} \approx 8.79 \text{ MeV/nucleon} \]

Q 13.5
Exercise 13.5
A given coin has a mass of $3.0 \text{ g}$. Calculate the nuclear energy that would be required to separate all the neutrons and protons from each other. For simplicity assume that the coin is entirely made of $^{63}_{29}\text{Cu}$ atoms (mass $= 62.92960 \text{ u}$).
($m_H = 1.007825 \text{ u}, m_n = 1.008665 \text{ u}$, Avogadro's number $N_A = 6.023 \times 10^{23} \text{ mol}^{-1}$).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Number of Atoms in $3.0 \text{ g}$ of $^{63}\text{Cu}$:
Molar mass $M \approx 63 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
\[ n = \frac{3.0 \text{ g}}{63 \text{ g mol}^{-1}} \times (6.023 \times 10^{23} \text{ mol}^{-1}) \approx 2.868 \times 10^{22} \text{ atoms} \]

Step 2: Binding Energy of One $^{63}_{29}\text{Cu}$ Nucleus:
$Z = 29$, $N = 63 - 29 = 34$.
Mass of constituents: \[ 29 m_H + 34 m_n = (29 \times 1.007825) + (34 \times 1.008665) = 29.226925 + 34.294610 = 63.521535 \text{ u} \] Mass defect: \[ \Delta m = 63.521535 - 62.92960 = 0.591935 \text{ u} \] Binding energy per nucleus: \[ E_b = 0.591935 \times 931.5 \approx 551.39 \text{ MeV} \]

Step 3: Total Energy Required:
\[ E_{total} = n \times E_b = (2.868 \times 10^{22}) \times 551.39 \text{ MeV} \approx 1.581 \times 10^{25} \text{ MeV} \] In Joules: \[ E_{total} = (1.581 \times 10^{25}) \times (1.602 \times 10^{-13} \text{ J}) \approx 2.53 \times 10^{12} \text{ J} = 2.53 \times 10^9 \text{ kJ} \]

Significance: An enormous energy of $\approx 2.53 \times 10^{12} \text{ J}$ is required, illustrating the immense strength of the strong nuclear force holding nucleons together.

Q 13.6
Exercise 13.6
Write nuclear reaction equations for:
(a) $\alpha$-decay of $^{226}_{88}\text{Ra}$
(b) $\alpha$-decay of $^{242}_{94}\text{Pu}$
(c) $\beta^-$-decay of $^{32}_{15}\text{P}$
(d) $\beta^-$-decay of $^{210}_{83}\text{Bi}$
(e) $\beta^+$-decay of $^{11}_{6}\text{C}$
(f) $\beta^+$-decay of $^{97}_{43}\text{Tc}$
(g) Electron capture of $^{120}_{54}\text{Xe}$.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Nuclear Decay Equations:

  1. (a) $\alpha$-decay of $^{226}_{88}\text{Ra}$ ($A-4, Z-2$): \[ ^{226}_{88}\text{Ra} \longrightarrow \, ^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} + \, ^4_2\text{He} \]

  3. (c) $\beta^-$-decay of $^{32}_{15}\text{P}$ ($Z+1$, antineutrino $\bar{\nu}_e$ emitted): \[ ^{32}_{15}\text{P} \longrightarrow \, ^{32}_{16}\text{S} + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e \]
  4. (d) $\beta^-$-decay of $^{210}_{83}\text{Bi}$: \[ ^{210}_{83}\text{Bi} \longrightarrow \, ^{210}_{84}\text{Po} + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e \]

Q 13.7
Exercise 13.7
A radioactive isotope has a half-life of $T$ years. How long will it take the activity to reduce to:
(a) $3.125\%$,
(b) $1\%$ of its original value?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Radioactive Decay Law:
\[ \frac{R}{R_0} = \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{t/T} = e^{-\lambda t} \]

(a) Reduction to $3.125\%$ ($0.03125$):
\[ \frac{R}{R_0} = 0.03125 = \frac{1}{32} = \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^5 \] Equating powers of $\frac{1}{2}$: \[ \frac{t}{T} = 5 \implies t = 5 T \text{ years} \]

(b) Reduction to $1\%$ ($0.01$):
\[ \frac{R}{R_0} = 0.01 = \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{t/T} \] Taking natural logarithms: \[ \ln(0.01) = \frac{t}{T} \ln(0.5) \implies -4.6052 = -0.69315 \left( \frac{t}{T} \right) \] \[ t = \frac{4.6052}{0.69315} T \approx 6.64 T \text{ years} \]

CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Solution for case (a): $t = 5T$1 Mark
Solution for case (b): $t = 6.64T$1 Mark
Q 13.8
Exercise 13.8
The normal activity of living carbon-containing matter is found to be about 15 decays per minute for each gram of carbon. An archaeological specimen found in Mohenjodaro gave an activity of 9 decays per minute per gram of carbon. Estimate the approximate age of the specimen. (Half-life of $^{14}\text{C} = 5730 \text{ years}$).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Initial activity $R_0 = 15 \text{ min}^{-1} \text{ g}^{-1}$
Present activity $R = 9 \text{ min}^{-1} \text{ g}^{-1}$
Half-life $T_{1/2} = 5730 \text{ years}$
Decay constant $\lambda = \frac{0.693}{5730} \approx 1.209 \times 10^{-4} \text{ yr}^{-1}$

Radioactive Dating Formula:
\[ R = R_0 e^{-\lambda t} \implies \frac{R_0}{R} = e^{\lambda t} \] \[ \lambda t = \ln\left( \frac{R_0}{R} \right) = \ln\left( \frac{15}{9} \right) = \ln\left( \frac{5}{3} \right) \approx \ln(1.667) \approx 0.5108 \]

Calculation of Age $t$:
\[ t = \frac{0.5108}{\lambda} = 0.5108 \times \frac{5730}{0.693} = \frac{2926.88}{0.693} \approx 4223.5 \text{ years} \approx 4224 \text{ years} \]

Result: The approximate age of the Mohenjodaro archaeological specimen is $4224 \text{ years}$.

Q 13.9
Exercise 13.9
Obtain the amount of $^{60}_{27}\text{Co}$ necessary to provide a radioactive source of $8.0 \text{ mCi}$ strength. The half-life of $^{60}\text{Co}$ is $5.3 \text{ years}$.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Activity $R = 8.0 \text{ mCi} = 8.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 3.7 \times 10^{10} \text{ Bq} = 2.96 \times 10^8 \text{ disintegrations s}^{-1}$
Half-life $T_{1/2} = 5.3 \text{ years} = 5.3 \times 365.25 \times 24 \times 3600 \approx 1.673 \times 10^8 \text{ s}$
Molar mass of $^{60}\text{Co} = 60 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$

Step 1: Decay Constant $\lambda$:
\[ \lambda = \frac{0.693}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{0.693}{1.673 \times 10^8 \text{ s}} \approx 4.142 \times 10^{-9} \text{ s}^{-1} \]

Step 2: Number of Nuclei Required ($N$):
\[ R = \lambda N \implies N = \frac{R}{\lambda} = \frac{2.96 \times 10^8 \text{ s}^{-1}}{4.142 \times 10^{-9} \text{ s}^{-1}} \approx 7.146 \times 10^{16} \text{ nuclei} \]

Step 3: Mass of $^{60}\text{Co}$ Required ($m$):
\[ m = \frac{N}{N_A} \times 60 \text{ g} = \frac{7.146 \times 10^{16}}{6.023 \times 10^{23}} \times 60 = 1.186 \times 10^{-7} \times 60 \approx 7.12 \times 10^{-6} \text{ g} = 7.12 \ \mu\text{g} \]

Q 13.10
Exercise 13.10
The half-life of $^{90}_{38}\text{Sr}$ is $28 \text{ years}$. What is the disintegration rate of $15 \text{ mg}$ of this isotope?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Mass $m = 15 \text{ mg} = 15 \times 10^{-3} \text{ g} = 1.5 \times 10^{-2} \text{ g}$
Atomic mass of $^{90}\text{Sr} = 90 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$
Half-life $T_{1/2} = 28 \text{ years} = 28 \times 3.156 \times 10^7 \text{ s} \approx 8.837 \times 10^8 \text{ s}$

Step 1: Number of Nuclei ($N$):
\[ N = \frac{m}{M} N_A = \frac{1.5 \times 10^{-2} \text{ g}}{90 \text{ g mol}^{-1}} \times (6.023 \times 10^{23}) \approx 1.004 \times 10^{20} \text{ nuclei} \]

Step 2: Decay Constant $\lambda$:
\[ \lambda = \frac{0.693}{8.837 \times 10^8 \text{ s}} \approx 7.842 \times 10^{-10} \text{ s}^{-1} \]

Step 3: Disintegration Rate (Activity $R$):
\[ R = \lambda N = (7.842 \times 10^{-10} \text{ s}^{-1}) \times (1.004 \times 10^{20}) \approx 7.873 \times 10^{10} \text{ Bq} \text{ (decays s}^{-1}\text{)} \] In Curies: \[ R = \frac{7.873 \times 10^{10}}{3.7 \times 10^{10}} \approx 2.13 \text{ Ci} \]

Q 13.11
Exercise 13.11
Obtain approximately the ratio of the nuclear radii of the gold isotope $^{197}_{79}\text{Au}$ and the silver isotope $^{107}_{47}\text{Ag}$. Show that nuclear matter density is independent of mass number $A$.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

1. Ratio of Nuclear Radii:
The radius of a nucleus of mass number $A$ is given by: \[ R = R_0 A^{1/3} \] Therefore: \[ \frac{R_{\text{Au}}}{R_{\text{Ag}}} = \left( \frac{A_{\text{Au}}}{A_{\text{Ag}}} \right)^{1/3} = \left( \frac{197}{107} \right)^{1/3} \approx (1.841)^{1/3} \approx 1.225 \approx 1.23 \]

2. Invariance of Nuclear Density:
Let $m$ be the average mass of a nucleon ($m \approx 1.66 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg}$).
Total mass of nucleus: $M = m A$.
Assuming spherical shape of radius $R = R_0 A^{1/3}$, the nuclear volume is: \[ V = \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 = \frac{4}{3} \pi (R_0 A^{1/3})^3 = \frac{4}{3} \pi R_0^3 A \] The density of nuclear matter is: \[ \rho = \frac{M}{V} = \frac{m A}{\frac{4}{3} \pi R_0^3 A} = \frac{3 m}{4 \pi R_0^3} \] Since $m$ and $R_0$ are universal nuclear constants, the mass number $A$ cancels out completely.
Taking $R_0 = 1.2 \times 10^{-15} \text{ m}$: \[ \rho = \frac{3 \times (1.66 \times 10^{-27})}{4\pi \times (1.2 \times 10^{-15})^3} \approx 2.3 \times 10^{17} \text{ kg m}^{-3} \] Hence, nuclear density is strictly constant and independent of the size or mass number of the nucleus.

Q 13.12
Exercise 13.12
Find the $Q$-value and the kinetic energy of the emitted $\alpha$-particle in the $\alpha$-decay of:
(a) $^{226}_{88}\text{Ra} \to \, ^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} + \, ^4_2\text{He}$
(Given: $m(^{226}\text{Ra}) = 226.02540 \text{ u}, m(^{222}\text{Rn}) = 222.01750 \text{ u}, m(^4\text{He}) = 4.002603 \text{ u}$).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Calculation of $Q$-Value:
Mass defect of the decay: \[ \Delta m = m(^{226}\text{Ra}) - [m(^{222}\text{Rn}) + m(^4\text{He})] \] \[ \Delta m = 226.02540 - (222.01750 + 4.002603) = 226.02540 - 226.020103 = 0.005297 \text{ u} \] $Q$-value: \[ Q = \Delta m \times 931.5 \text{ MeV} = 0.005297 \times 931.5 \approx 4.934 \text{ MeV} \]

Kinetic Energy of the $\alpha$-particle ($K_\alpha$):
By momentum conservation, the recoil energy of daughter nucleus gives: \[ K_\alpha = \left( \frac{A - 4}{A} \right) Q = \left( \frac{226 - 4}{226} \right) Q = \frac{222}{226} \times 4.934 \text{ MeV} \approx 4.847 \text{ MeV} \approx 4.85 \text{ MeV} \]

Q 13.13
Exercise 13.13
The radionuclide $^{11}\text{C}$ decays according to: \[ ^{11}_6\text{C} \longrightarrow \, ^{11}_5\text{B} + e^+ + \nu_e, \quad T_{1/2} = 20.3 \text{ min} \] The maximum energy of the emitted positron is $0.960 \text{ MeV}$. Given masses: $m(^{11}_6\text{C}) = 11.011434 \text{ u}, m(^{11}_5\text{B}) = 11.009305 \text{ u}, m_e = 0.000548 \text{ u}$. Calculate the $Q$-value and compare it with the maximum positron energy.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Nuclear Mass vs Atomic Mass in $\beta^+$-decay:
In positron emission, the decay equation in terms of atomic masses must account for the creation of positron and loss of an orbital electron: \[ Q = [m(^{11}_6\text{C}) - m(^{11}_5\text{B}) - 2 m_e] c^2 \]

Calculation:
\[ \Delta m = 11.011434 - 11.009305 - 2(0.000548) = 0.002129 - 0.001096 = 0.001033 \text{ u} \] \[ Q = 0.001033 \times 931.5 \text{ MeV} \approx 0.962 \text{ MeV} \]

Comparison: The calculated $Q$-value ($0.962 \text{ MeV}$) agrees exceptionally well with the experimental maximum kinetic energy of the emitted positron ($0.960 \text{ MeV}$), with the tiny negligible difference accounted for by the daughter recoil.

Q 13.14
Exercise 13.14
The nucleus $^{23}_{10}\text{Ne}$ decays by $\beta^-$ emission into $^{23}_{11}\text{Na}$. Write down the $\beta$-decay equation and determine the maximum kinetic energy of the electrons emitted.
(Given: $m(^{23}_{10}\text{Ne}) = 22.994466 \text{ u}, m(^{23}_{11}\text{Na}) = 22.989770 \text{ u}$).
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Decay Equation:
\[ ^{23}_{10}\text{Ne} \longrightarrow \, ^{23}_{11}\text{Na} + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e \]

$Q$-Value in $\beta^-$-decay:
In $\beta^-$ decay, the atomic masses automatically cancel the electron mass: \[ Q = [m(^{23}_{10}\text{Ne}) - m(^{23}_{11}\text{Na})] c^2 \] \[ \Delta m = 22.994466 - 22.989770 = 0.004696 \text{ u} \] \[ Q = 0.004696 \times 931.5 \text{ MeV} \approx 4.374 \text{ MeV} \] Neglecting the tiny recoil of the heavy daughter nucleus $^{23}\text{Na}$, the maximum kinetic energy carried by the emitted $\beta^-$ electron (when antineutrino energy $\to 0$) is: \[ K_{e, max} \approx Q \approx 4.37 \text{ MeV} \]

Q 13.15
Exercise 13.15
Calculate the $Q$-values of the following nuclear reactions:
(a) $^{1}_1\text{H} + \, ^3_1\text{H} \to \, ^2_1\text{H} + \, ^2_1\text{H}$
($m(^1\text{H}) = 1.007825 \text{ u}, m(^3\text{H}) = 3.016049 \text{ u}, m(^2\text{H}) = 2.014102 \text{ u}$)
(b) $^{12}_6\text{C} + \, ^{12}_6\text{C} \to \, ^{20}_{10}\text{Ne} + \, ^4_2\text{He}$
($m(^{12}\text{C}) = 12.000000 \text{ u}, m(^{20}\text{Ne}) = 19.992439 \text{ u}, m(^4\text{He}) = 4.002603 \text{ u}$).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

(a) Reaction $^{1}_1\text{H} + \, ^3_1\text{H} \to \, ^2_1\text{H} + \, ^2_1\text{H}$:
Initial mass: $m_i = 1.007825 + 3.016049 = 4.023874 \text{ u}$
Final mass: $m_f = 2 \times 2.014102 = 4.028204 \text{ u}$
Mass defect: \[ \Delta m = m_i - m_f = 4.023874 - 4.028204 = -0.004330 \text{ u} \] \[ Q = -0.004330 \times 931.5 \text{ MeV} \approx -4.033 \text{ MeV} \] (Negative $Q$ indicates an endoergic reaction requiring energy input).

(b) Reaction $^{12}_6\text{C} + \, ^{12}_6\text{C} \to \, ^{20}_{10}\text{Ne} + \, ^4_2\text{He}$:
Initial mass: $m_i = 2 \times 12.000000 = 24.000000 \text{ u}$
Final mass: $m_f = 19.992439 + 4.002603 = 23.995042 \text{ u}$
Mass defect: \[ \Delta m = 24.000000 - 23.995042 = +0.004958 \text{ u} \] \[ Q = +0.004958 \times 931.5 \text{ MeV} \approx +4.618 \text{ MeV} \] (Positive $Q$ indicates an exoergic energy-releasing reaction).

Q 13.16
Exercise 13.16
Suppose we consider the fission of a $^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}$ nucleus into two equal fragments, $^{28}_{13}\text{Al}$. Is this fission energetically possible? Argue by working out the $Q$-value of the process.
(Given: $m(^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}) = 55.93494 \text{ u}, m(^{28}_{13}\text{Al}) = 27.98191 \text{ u}$).
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Fission Reaction:
\[ ^{56}_{26}\text{Fe} \longrightarrow \, 2 \, ^{28}_{13}\text{Al} \]

Mass Defect:
\[ \Delta m = m(^{56}\text{Fe}) - 2 m(^{28}\text{Al}) = 55.93494 - 2 \times 27.98191 = 55.93494 - 55.96382 = -0.02888 \text{ u} \]

$Q$-Value:
\[ Q = \Delta m \times 931.5 \text{ MeV} = -0.02888 \times 931.5 \approx -26.90 \text{ MeV} \]

Conclusion:
Since $Q < 0$ (highly negative), this process is endothermic and requires an external supply of $26.9 \text{ MeV}$. Therefore, spontaneous fission of $^{56}\text{Fe}$ is energetically impossible. Indeed, $^{56}\text{Fe}$ is at the absolute peak of the binding energy curve and is the most tightly bound nucleus in nature.

Q 13.18
Exercise 13.18
A $1000 \text{ MW}$ fission reactor consumes half of its fuel in $5.00 \text{ years}$. How much $^{235}\text{U}$ did it contain initially? Assume that the reactor operates $80\%$ of the time, that all the energy generated comes from the fission of $^{235}\text{U}$ ($200 \text{ MeV}$ per fission).
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Thermal power output $P = 1000 \text{ MW} = 10^9 \text{ W}$
Total duration $t = 5.00 \text{ years} = 5 \times 365.25 \times 24 \times 3600 \approx 1.578 \times 10^8 \text{ s}$
Operating factor $= 80\% = 0.80$
Effective operating time $t_{eff} = 0.80 \times 1.578 \times 10^8 = 1.2624 \times 10^8 \text{ s}$
Energy per fission $E_f = 200 \text{ MeV} = 200 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-13} \text{ J} = 3.204 \times 10^{-11} \text{ J}$

Step 1: Total Energy Generated in 5 Years:
\[ E = P \times t_{eff} = 10^9 \text{ W} \times 1.2624 \times 10^8 \text{ s} = 1.2624 \times 10^{17} \text{ J} \]

Step 2: Number of Fissions:
\[ N_{fiss} = \frac{E}{E_f} = \frac{1.2624 \times 10^{17} \text{ J}}{3.204 \times 10^{-11} \text{ J}} \approx 3.940 \times 10^{27} \text{ nuclei} \]

Step 3: Mass of $^{235}\text{U}$ Consumed ($m_{consumed}$):
\[ m_{consumed} = \frac{N_{fiss}}{N_A} \times 235 \text{ g} = \frac{3.940 \times 10^{27}}{6.023 \times 10^{23}} \times 235 \text{ g} = 6.5415 \times 10^3 \times 235 \text{ g} \approx 1537.25 \text{ kg} \]

Step 4: Initial Mass of Uranium Fuel:
Since the reactor consumed half of its fuel in this duration: \[ m_{initial} = 2 \times m_{consumed} = 2 \times 1537.25 \text{ kg} \approx 3074.5 \text{ kg} \approx 3.07 \times 10^3 \text{ kg} \]

Q 13.19
Exercise 13.19
How long can an electric lamp of $100 \text{ W}$ be kept glowing by the complete fusion of $2.0 \text{ kg}$ of deuterium? Take the fusion reaction as: \[ ^2_1\text{H} + \, ^2_1\text{H} \longrightarrow \, ^3_2\text{He} + n + 3.27 \text{ MeV} \]
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Mass of deuterium $m = 2.0 \text{ kg} = 2000 \text{ g}$
Molar mass $= 2 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$
Power of lamp $P = 100 \text{ W}$
Energy per pair of deuterons $= 3.27 \text{ MeV}$

Step 1: Number of Deuteron Nuclei ($N$):
\[ N = \frac{2000 \text{ g}}{2 \text{ g mol}^{-1}} \times (6.023 \times 10^{23}) = 6.023 \times 10^{26} \text{ deuterons} \] Since each reaction involves 2 deuterons, the number of fusion reactions is: \[ N_{rxn} = \frac{N}{2} = 3.0115 \times 10^{26} \text{ reactions} \]

Step 2: Total Energy Released ($E$):
\[ E = N_{rxn} \times 3.27 \text{ MeV} = (3.0115 \times 10^{26}) \times 3.27 \text{ MeV} \approx 9.8476 \times 10^{26} \text{ MeV} \] In Joules: \[ E = (9.8476 \times 10^{26}) \times (1.602 \times 10^{-13} \text{ J}) \approx 1.5776 \times 10^{14} \text{ J} \]

Step 3: Duration $t$:
\[ t = \frac{E}{P} = \frac{1.5776 \times 10^{14} \text{ J}}{100 \text{ W}} = 1.5776 \times 10^{12} \text{ s} \] In years ($1 \text{ yr} \approx 3.156 \times 10^7 \text{ s}$): \[ t = \frac{1.5776 \times 10^{12}}{3.156 \times 10^7} \approx 4.998 \times 10^4 \text{ years} \approx 5.0 \times 10^4 \text{ years} \]

Result: The lamp can glow for roughly $50,000 \text{ years}$.

Q 13.20
Exercise 13.20
Calculate the height of the electrostatic potential barrier for a head-on collision of two deuterons. (Assume that they can be taken as hard spheres of radius $2.0 \text{ fm}$).
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Given:
Radius of each deuteron $R = 2.0 \text{ fm} = 2.0 \times 10^{-15} \text{ m}$
At the moment of touching during a head-on collision, the separation between their centres is: \[ d = 2 R = 2 \times (2.0 \times 10^{-15} \text{ m}) = 4.0 \times 10^{-15} \text{ m} \] Charge of each deuteron $q = +e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$

Electrostatic Potential Barrier Height ($U$):
\[ U = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{e^2}{d} = \frac{(9.0 \times 10^9 \text{ N m}^2 \text{ C}^{-2}) \times (1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C})^2}{4.0 \times 10^{-15} \text{ m}} \] \[ U = \frac{9.0 \times 10^9 \times 2.566 \times 10^{-38}}{4.0 \times 10^{-15}} = \frac{2.3094 \times 10^{-28}}{4.0 \times 10^{-15}} \approx 5.774 \times 10^{-14} \text{ J} \] In keV: \[ U = \frac{5.774 \times 10^{-14} \text{ J}}{1.602 \times 10^{-16} \text{ J keV}^{-1}} \approx 360 \text{ keV} \]

Result: The height of the Coulomb potential barrier is $360 \text{ keV}$.

Q 13.21
Exercise 13.21
Draw a plot of binding energy per nucleon ($E_{bn}$) as a function of mass number $A$. State the main conclusions drawn from this curve regarding:
(a) nuclear stability and saturation of nuclear forces,
(b) nuclear fission, and
(c) nuclear fusion.
[5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Solution

Features of the Binding Energy per Nucleon Curve:

  1. Plateau of High Stability: For intermediate mass numbers ($30 < A < 170$), $E_{bn}$ is practically constant at approximately $8.0 - 8.5 \text{ MeV/nucleon}$, reaching an absolute maximum of $8.75 \text{ MeV/nucleon}$ at $^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}$. These nuclei are the most stable in nature.
  2. Saturation of Nuclear Forces: The constancy of $E_{bn}$ over a broad range of $A$ proves that the strong nuclear force is extremely short-ranged ($sim 1 - 2 \text{ fm}$). A nucleon interacts only with its immediate nearest neighbours, so binding energy is proportional to $A$, leading to constant $E_{bn}$.
  3. Drop at Heavy Nuclei & Nuclear Fission: For very heavy nuclei ($A > 170$, e.g. $^{238}\text{U}$ where $E_{bn} \approx 7.6 \text{ MeV}$), cumulative Coulomb repulsion among many protons reduces $E_{bn}$. When a heavy nucleus undergoes fission into two medium-mass fragments ($A \sim 100 - 140$), $E_{bn}$ rises from $\sim 7.6$ to $\sim 8.5 \text{ MeV}$, releasing vast net nuclear energy ($Q \approx 200 \text{ MeV}$).
  4. Drop at Light Nuclei & Nuclear Fusion: For light nuclei ($A < 30$, e.g. $^2\text{H}, ^3\text{H}$), $E_{bn}$ is very low ($< 7 \text{ MeV}$). When two light nuclei undergo thermonuclear fusion to form a heavier, more tightly bound nucleus (such as $^4_2\text{He}$ with $E_{bn} \approx 7.1 \text{ MeV}$), the binding energy per nucleon jumps dramatically, liberating immense energy per unit mass.
