Q1
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Mention the advantages of selecting pea plant for experiment by Mendel.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Gregor Johann Mendel selected the garden pea (Pisum sativum) for his landmark hybridisation experiments due to several distinct morphological, reproductive, and practical advantages:

  1. Well-Defined Contrasting Traits: Pea plants exhibit conspicuous, easily identifiable contrasting characters (e.g., tall vs dwarf, yellow vs green seeds, round vs wrinkled seeds, purple vs white flowers).
  2. Naturally Self-Pollinating with Cleistogamous Tendency: The pea flower has a papilionaceous corolla (keel petals enclose the stamens and stigma), ensuring natural self-pollination and enabling the continuous generation of pure-breeding true lines.
  3. Feasibility of Easy Artificial Hybridisation (Cross-Pollination): Manual cross-pollination can be conveniently carried out by emasculation (removal of anthers before dehiscence) followed by controlled dusting of desired pollen and bagging.
  4. Short Life Cycle and Annual Habit: Peas complete their entire life cycle within 3–4 months, enabling Mendel to follow several successive generations within a few years.
  5. High Fecundity (Abundant Progeny): A single plant produces numerous seeds in each pod, providing large sample sizes that permit statistical validity and reliable mathematical probability ratios.
  6. Ease of Cultivation: Pea plants require simple cultivation conditions and small garden plots.

A diploid organism is heterozygous for 4 loci, how many types of gametes can be produced?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Formula and Calculation:

The number of genetically distinct gametes produced by an individual with independently assorting heterozygous gene loci is given by the formula:

Number of gamete types = 2n

Where n represents the number of heterozygous loci.

  • Given: Diploid organism is heterozygous for 4 loci, so n = 4.
  • Representative Genotype: AaBbCcDd
  • Calculation:
    Number of gametes = 24 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 types of gametes.

Conclusion: The organism can produce 16 genetically distinct types of gametes (each with a probability of 1/16).

Q4
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Explain the Law of Dominance using a monohybrid cross.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Statement of Mendel's Law of Dominance:

  1. Characters are controlled by discrete physical units called factors (now called genes).
  2. Factors occur in pairs in diploid organisms.
  3. In a dissimilar pair of factors (heterozygous condition), one member of the pair dominates (dominant factor) and expresses itself phenotypically, while the other member is masked/suppressed (recessive factor).

Demonstration Using Monohybrid Cross (Stem Height in Pisum sativum):

  • Parents (P): Pure-breeding Tall (TT) × Pure-breeding Dwarf (tt)
  • Gametes: All 'T' from tall parent; all 't' from dwarf parent.
  • F1 Generation: All offspring are heterozygous Tt. Phenotypically, 100% of the F1 plants are Tall. Despite carrying the allele for dwarfness ('t'), only the tall phenotype ('T') is expressed. This proves that 'T' is dominant over 't'.
  • F2 Generation (Selfing F1 × F1, Tt × Tt):
    Gametes T (½) t (½)
    T (½) TT (Tall) Tt (Tall)
    t (½) Tt (Tall) tt (Dwarf)

    In the F2 generation, the masked recessive trait reappears in the homozygous state (tt).
    Phenotypic Ratio: 3 Tall : 1 Dwarf (3 : 1)
    Genotypic Ratio: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt (1 : 2 : 1)

Define and design a test-cross.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Definition:

A test cross is a cross between an individual showing a dominant phenotype (whose genotype is unknown, i.e., whether homozygous dominant or heterozygous) and the homozygous recessive parent.

Purpose of Test Cross: To determine the unknown genotype of the dominant parent.

Design of Test Cross for Flower Colour in Pea (Violet vs White):

Dominant flower colour is Violet (alleles: W), recessive is White (ww). An individual plant with violet flowers may have genotype WW (homozygous) or Ww (heterozygous).

Case 1: If Unknown Plant is Homozygous (WW)

Cross: WW × ww

×WW

wWw (Violet)Ww (Violet)

Case 2: If Unknown Plant is Heterozygous (Ww)

Cross: Ww × ww

×Ww

Q8
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Two heterozygous parents are crossed. If the two loci are linked what would be the distribution of phenotypic features in F1 generation for a dihybrid cross?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

When two heterozygous parents (genotype: AaBb) are crossed and the two gene loci are linked (located on the same homologous chromosome pair):

  1. Absence of Independent Assortment: The genes do not assort independently. The classical Mendelian dihybrid F2 ratio of 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 will NOT be obtained.
  2. Case 1: Complete Linkage (No crossing over):
    • If genes are in cis-coupling phase (AB / ab), gametes formed are only parental types: AB (50%) and ab (50%).
    • Offspring produced upon crossing two heterozygous parents (AB/ab × AB/ab):
      Genotypes: 1 AB/AB : 2 AB/ab : 1 ab/ab
      Phenotypes: 3 Parental Dominant (A_B_) : 1 Parental Double Recessive (aabb) (Ratio = 3 : 1).
    • Non-parental (recombinant) phenotypes are 0%.
  3. Case 2: Incomplete Linkage (Crossing over occurs):
    • Parental phenotypes appear in a significantly higher proportion (> 50%) than expected by independent assortment.
    • Recombinant (non-parental) phenotypes appear in a lower frequency (< 50%), directly proportional to the physical distance between the linked loci on the chromosome.

Q9
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Briefly mention the contribution of T.H. Morgan in genetics.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Thomas Hunt Morgan (awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1933) is regarded as the Father of Experimental Genetics. His major contributions include:

  1. Selection of Drosophila melanogaster (Fruit Fly) as Model Organism: Established fruit flies for genetic research due to their short 2-week life cycle, ease of synthetic medium culture, high progeny count, clear morphological sexual dimorphism, and easily visible hereditary variations under low-power microscopes.
  2. Experimental Verification of Chromosomal Theory: Provided conclusive experimental proof for the Sutton-Boveri Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance.
  3. Discovery of Sex-Linkage: Discovered X-linked inheritance in Drosophila (e.g., white-eye mutation discovered in males and tracing criss-cross inheritance).
  4. Discovery of Linkage and Recombination: Demonstrated that genes located on the same chromosome do not assort independently, coining the term linkage to describe the physical association of genes on a chromosome, and recombination to describe generation of non-parental gene combinations due to crossing over.
  5. Foundation of Chromosome Mapping: His student, Alfred Sturtevant, utilized Morgan's recombination frequencies as a measure of genetic distance to construct the first genetic linkage maps.

Q10
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
What is pedigree analysis? Suggest how such an analysis, can be useful.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Definition of Pedigree Analysis:

Pedigree analysis is the systematic study and analysis of the inheritance of a specific trait, disease, or abnormality across multiple successive generations of a human family, represented in the form of a standardised genealogical family tree diagram.

Utility and Clinical Applications:

  1. Overcomes Limitations of Human Experimental Genetics: Controlled experimental breeding and hybridisation crosses are unethical and impossible in humans; furthermore, human families have small numbers of progeny. Pedigree charts allow geneticists to trace family history retrospectively.
  2. Identification of Inheritance Patterns: Determines whether an inherited trait is:
    • Autosomal dominant (e.g., Myotonic dystrophy)
    • Autosomal recessive (e.g., Sickle-cell anaemia, Cystic fibrosis)
    • Sex-linked (X-linked) recessive (e.g., Haemophilia, Red-green colour blindness)
  3. Genetic Counselling & Carrier Detection: Enables genetic counsellors to determine carrier status (heterozygous carriers) among prospective parents and assess the statistical probability/risk of conceiving an affected child.
  4. Prenatal Decision Making: Guides informed decisions on prenatal amniocentesis, IVF with preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD), or family planning.

Q11
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
How is sex determined in human beings?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Sex determination in human beings is based on the XX-XY chromosomal mechanism, characterized by male heterogamety.

  1. Karyotypic Constitution:
    • Human somatic cells contain 23 pairs (46 chromosomes): 22 pairs of autosomes and 1 pair of sex chromosomes (allosomes).
    • Females (Homogametic): Possess two identical X chromosomes (44 + XX). All ova produced during oogenesis carry an X chromosome (22 + X).
    • Males (Heterogametic): Possess one X and one significantly smaller Y chromosome (44 + XY). During spermatogenesis, males produce two distinct types of sperms in equal 50:50 proportion:
      • 50% Gynomperms carrying 22 + X
      • 50% Androsperms carrying 22 + Y
  2. Fertilisation & Sex Outcome:
    • If an ovum (22 + X) is fertilised by an X-bearing sperm (22 + X), the resulting zygote is 44 + XX, which develops into a Female child.
    • If an ovum (22 + X) is fertilised by a Y-bearing sperm (22 + Y), the resulting zygote is 44 + XY, which develops into a Male child (due to presence of SRY gene on Y chromosome).
  3. Statistical Probability: Since X-bearing and Y-bearing sperms are produced in equal numbers, there is an exact 50% (1 : 1) probability of having either a boy or a girl in each pregnancy.
  4. Biological Fact: It is biologically the father's sperm that determines the sex of the child, not the mother.

Q13
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Explain the following terms with one example each:
(a) Co-dominance
(b) Incomplete dominance
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution
(a) Co-dominance

Definition: Co-dominance is a phenomenon in which both alleles of a gene in a heterozygote express themselves equally and simultaneously in the phenotype. There is no dominant-recessive relationship, nor is there any blending of characters.

Example: ABO Blood Grouping in Humans:

  • The gene I controls ABO blood groups and has three alleles: IA, IB, and i.
  • Alleles IA and IB produce slightly different forms of surface sugar polymers on erythrocytes.
  • When both IA and IB are present together in an individual (genotype IAIB), both alleles express their respective sugars fully. As a consequence, the red blood cells have both A and B types of sugars, resulting in Blood Group AB.
(b) Incomplete Dominance

Definition: Incomplete dominance is an inheritance pattern in which neither allele of a gene is completely dominant over the other. The heterozygous F1 hybrid exhibits an intermediate, blending phenotype between the two homozygous parental phenotypes.

Example: Flower Colour in Snapdragon (Antirrhinum majus) or 4 O'clock Plant (Mirabilis jalapa):

  • A true-breeding red-flowered plant (RR) crossed with a true-breeding white-flowered plant (rr).
  • The F1 hybrid (Rr) produces Pink flowers (intermediate between red and white).
  • Selfing of F1 (Rr × Rr) produces F2 progeny with:
    Phenotypic ratio: 1 Red : 2 Pink : 1 White (1 : 2 : 1)
    Genotypic ratio: 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr (1 : 2 : 1)
  • Here, both phenotypic and genotypic ratios are identical (1:2:1), demonstrating that the recessive allele 'r' is not altered and segregates cleanly in F2.

Q14
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
What is point mutation? Give one example.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Definition:

A point mutation is a gene mutation that arises due to a change, substitution, insertion, or deletion of a single base pair in the DNA sequence of a gene.

Classic Example: Sickle-Cell Anaemia:

  1. Sickle-cell anaemia is an autosomal recessive genetic disease caused by a point mutation (base substitution) in the gene coding for the β-globin chain of haemoglobin located on chromosome 11.
  2. At the 6th codon of the β-globin gene, a transversion mutation converts the normal codon GAG to GUG (thymine substituted by adenine in the coding strand).
  3. This results in the substitution of Glutamic acid (Glu) by Valine (Val) at the sixth position of the β-globin polypeptide chain:
    Normal HbA gene: GAG (mRNA) → Glutamic Acid (Polar, hydrophilic)
    Sickle HbS gene: GUG (mRNA) → Valine (Non-polar, hydrophobic)
  4. Under low oxygen tension, the mutant haemoglobin (HbS) undergoes polymerisation, changing the shape of the erythrocyte from a flexible biconcave disc to an elongated, rigid sickle-like structure, leading to microvascular occlusion and haemolytic anaemia.

Q15
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Who had proposed the chromosomal theory of the inheritance?
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

The Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance was proposed independently in 1902 by:

Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri (1902)

Core Concept:

  • They noticed that the behaviour of chromosomes during meiosis (gamete formation) was parallel to the behaviour of Mendelian factors (genes).
  • They united chromosomal segregation during anaphase I & II with Mendelian principles and concluded that genes are linear physical entities carried upon chromosomes.
  • Note: Experimental verification of this theory was subsequently provided by Thomas Hunt Morgan and his colleagues using Drosophila melanogaster.

Q16
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Mention any two autosomal genetic disorders with their symptoms.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Two well-known autosomal genetic disorders are:

1. Down's Syndrome (Autosomal Aneuploidy / Trisomy 21)

Genetic Basis: Presence of an additional copy of chromosome number 21 (karyotype: 47, +21) due to non-disjunction of chromosomes during gametogenesis. First described by Langdon Down in 1866.

Key Clinical Symptoms:

  • Short stature with small round head.
  • Furrowed tongue and partially open mouth.
  • Broad palm with characteristic single transverse palmar crease (Simian crease).
  • Physical, psychomotor, and mental development is retarded.
2. Phenylketonuria (PKU) (Autosomal Recessive Inborn Error of Metabolism)

Genetic Basis: Mutation in the gene coding for the hepatic enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase located on chromosome 12.

Key Clinical Symptoms:

  • Inability to convert amino acid phenylalanine into tyrosine, causing accumulation of phenylalanine and its conversion to phenylpyruvic acid.
  • Accumulation of these neurotoxic derivatives in brain leads to severe mental retardation.
  • Excretion of phenyl derivatives in urine due to poor kidney tubular reabsorption.
  • Hypopigmentation of hair and skin.
