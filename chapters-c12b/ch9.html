✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Recombinant DNA technology has enabled the high-purity production of therapeutic human proteins in transgenic host cells (e.g., bacteria, yeast, CHO cells):

Q2
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Make a chart (with diagrammatic representation) showing a restriction enzyme, the substrate DNA on which it acts, the site at which it cuts DNA and the product it produces.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Action of Restriction Endonuclease EcoRI (from Escherichia coli RY 13):

EcoRI inspects the length of DNA and recognises a specific 6 base-pair palindromic recognition sequence:

1. Substrate DNA & Palindromic Recognition Site:

5' —— G ↓ A   A   T   T   C —— 3'
3' —— C   T   T   A   A ↑ G —— 5'

2. Site of Cleavage:

  • EcoRI cuts both strands of the double helix at the same specific point between G and A bases, just slightly away from the centre of the symmetry.
  • It cleaves the phosphodiester backbone between Guanine and Adenine on both complementary strands.

3. Resulting Products (Staggered Sticky Ends):

Strand 1:  5' —— G                             A   A   T   T   C —— 3'
Strand 2:  3' —— C   T   T   A   A                             G —— 5'

Significance of Sticky Ends: The single-stranded overhanging overhangs are termed sticky ends because they readily form complementary hydrogen bonds with matching sticky ends cut by the same enzyme on vector DNA, facilitating covalent sealing by DNA ligase to produce recombinant DNA.

Q3
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
From what you have learnt, can you tell whether enzymes are bigger or DNA is bigger in molecular size? How did you know?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

DNA is vastly bigger in molecular size, weight, and chain length than an enzyme.

Scientific Proof and Comparison:

  1. Chemical Constitution:
    • Enzymes: Enzymes are proteins, which are linear polymers of amino acids. A typical catalytic enzyme (e.g., DNA polymerase or lysozyme) contains roughly 100 to a few thousand amino acids. The average molecular weight of an enzyme ranges between 10,000 to a few hundred thousand Daltons (104 to 106 Da).
    • DNA: DNA is an enormous polynucleotide macromolecule. A single human chromosome consists of a continuous DNA duplex containing between 50 million to 250 million base pairs. The molecular mass of a single chromosome DNA reaches billions of Daltons (1010 to 1012 Da).
  2. Genetic Relationship (Gene codes for Enzyme):
    • DNA carries the instructions to synthesize thousands of different proteins. A single gene on a DNA strand transcribes an mRNA that translates into one enzyme.
    • Since an enzyme is merely the translated expression of a minute fraction (one gene) of a vast genomic DNA macromolecule containing tens of thousands of genes, the master blueprint (DNA) is infinitely larger than any single protein product it specifies.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Clear statement: DNA is vastly bigger than an enzyme0.5 Mark
Comparison of molecular weights (DNA ~10^10-10^12 Da vs Enzyme ~10^4-10^6 Da)1 Mark
Rationale that one DNA molecule encodes thousands of distinct protein/enzymes0.5 Mark
Q4
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
What would be the molar concentration of human DNA in a human cell? Consult your teacher.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Step-by-Step Biophysical Calculation:

  1. DNA Content per Human Diploid Cell:
    • A human somatic cell contains 46 chromosomes (23 pairs), corresponding to 1 single diploid genomic DNA set (or 46 separate DNA molecules) per cell.
  2. Volume of a Human Cell:
    • The average volume of a spherical mammalian cell (radius r ≈ 10 μm = 10-5 m) is:
      Volume V = (4/3)πr3 ≈ (4/3) × 3.14 × (10-5)3 ≈ 4.19 × 10-15 m3 = 4.19 × 10-12 Litres (or ~1–4 pL).
      If considering nuclear volume specifically (radius ~3 μm): Vnucleus ≈ 1.13 × 10-13 Litres.
  3. Calculation of Molar Concentration (M = Moles / Volume):
    • Number of moles of diploid genome in 1 cell = 1 / Avogadro's Number:
      Moles = 1 / (6.023 × 1023) ≈ 1.66 × 10-24 moles.
    • Molar Concentration in whole cell:
      M = (1.66 × 10-24 moles) / (4.19 × 10-12 L) ≈ 4 × 10-13 M (0.4 picomolar).
    • Alternatively, if calculated in terms of total individual base pairs (6.6 × 109 bp per diploid cell):
      Moles of base pairs = (6.6 × 109) / (6.023 × 1023) ≈ 1.1 × 10-14 moles.
      Molar concentration of base pairs in cell ≈ (1.1 × 10-14) / (4.19 × 10-12 L) ≈ 2.6 × 10-3 M = 2.6 mM (millimolar).

Conclusion: The molar concentration of the diploid human genome is approximately 0.4 pM (or in terms of nucleotide base pairs, roughly 2.6 mM).

Calculation of moles per cell using Avogadro's number (1 / 6.023 x 10^23)1 Mark
Estimation of cell/nuclear volume and deriving molarity (~0.4 pM for genome or ~2-3 mM for base pairs)1 Mark
Q5
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Do eukaryotic cells have restriction endonucleases? Justify your answer.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

No, eukaryotic cells naturally do NOT have restriction endonucleases.

Scientific Justification:

  1. Evolutionary Purpose in Prokaryotes:
    • Restriction endonucleases evolved exclusively in bacteria and archaea as a primitive, adaptive intracellular immune defense mechanism against bacteriophage (viral) infection.
    • Bacteria synthesize restriction enzymes to cleave incoming foreign viral DNA, while protecting their own genomic DNA from self-digestion through site-specific DNA methylases (the Restriction-Modification or R-M system).
  2. Absence and Incompatibility in Eukaryotes:
    • Eukaryotic genomic DNA is organized inside a membrane-bound nucleus and intricately packaged with histone proteins into chromatin fibers.
    • Eukaryotic cells have evolved entirely different, sophisticated defense systems against viral and transposable element invaders, such as RNA interference (RNAi), APOBEC cytidine deaminases, and adaptive cell-mediated immunity.
    • If restriction endonucleases were present within a eukaryotic nucleus, they would cleave endogenous genomic DNA at every occurrence of their recognition sequence, leading to fatal chromosome fragmentation and cellular death.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Definitive negative answer: No, eukaryotic cells lack restriction endonucleases0.5 Mark
Biological role of restriction enzymes as prokaryotic bacterial defense against bacteriophages0.5 Mark
Justification: Eukaryotes utilize alternative antiviral systems (RNAi, immune cells) and would suffer lethal auto-genomic digestion1 Mark
Q6
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Besides better stirring and oxygen availability, what other advantages have stirred tank bioreactors over shake flasks?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

A stirred-tank bioreactor provides an engineered, automated, large-scale (100–1,000 litres) closed culture vessel with multiple decisive bioprocess engineering advantages over small laboratory shake flasks:

  1. Continuous Real-Time Process Monitoring & Control:
    • Equipped with automated control systems to continuously monitor and maintain optimal physiological parameters:
      • Temperature Control: Steam jacket / cooling coils maintain constant temperature despite metabolic heat generation.
      • pH Automation: Automatic addition of acid or base to maintain strict pH setpoints.
      • Dissolved Oxygen (DO) Sensors: Monitors oxygen saturation levels automatically.
  2. Efficient Foam Management: Includes an automated foam control / foam breaker system to prevent excessive foaming caused by aeration and protein denaturation, which overflows and plugs filters in shake flasks.
  3. Aseptic Sampling Ports: Equipped with sterile sampling ports that permit drawing small culture samples periodically to test optical density, cell viability, and product concentration without breaching sterility or interrupting the fermentation run.
  4. Continuous vs Batch Operation: Can be operated in fed-batch or continuous culture modes (chemostat), maintaining microorganisms in their physiologically active exponential / log phase for maximum biomass and product yield.
  5. Massive Scalability: Allows scaling biological processes from millilitre flask volumes to thousands of litres required for commercial pharmaceutical production.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Precise temperature, pH, and dissolved oxygen automation systems1 Mark
Foam control system and sterile sampling ports for real-time monitoring1 Mark
Ability to run continuous/fed-batch culture and massive industrial scalability (100-1000 L)1 Mark
Q7
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Collect 5 examples of palindromic DNA sequences by consulting your teacher. Better try to create a palindromic sequence by following base-pair rules.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

A palindromic sequence in DNA is a sequence of base pairs that reads identical on both complementary strands when reading in the same direction (5' → 3' or 3' → 5').

5 Verified Examples of Restriction Enzyme Palindromes:

1. EcoRI Recognition Palindrome (6 bp):
5' — G ↓ A   A   T   T   C — 3'
3' — C   T   T   A   A ↑ G — 5'
2. HindIII Recognition Palindrome (6 bp):
5' — A ↓ A   G   C   T   T — 3'
3' — T   T   C   G   A ↑ A — 5'
3. BamHI Recognition Palindrome (6 bp):
5' — G ↓ G   A   T   C   C — 3'
3' — C   C   T   A   G ↑ G — 5'
4. SmaI Recognition Palindrome (Blunt cutter, 6 bp):
5' — C   C   C ↓ G   G   G — 3'
3' — G   G   G ↑ C   C   C — 5'
5. HaeIII Recognition Palindrome (4 bp):
5' — G   G ↓ C   C — 3'
3' — C   C ↑ G   G — 5'

Rule for Designing Custom Palindromes:

To create an 8-base-pair palindrome: Choose 4 bases at random for 5'-end (e.g., 5'-TCGA...). Write the inverted Watson-Crick complement for the next 4 bases (...TCGA-3').

Custom 8 bp Palindrome:
5' — T   C   G   A   T   C   G   A — 3'  → Inverted: 5' — A   G   C   T   A   G   C   T — 3'

Q8
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Can you recall meiosis and indicate at what stage a recombinant DNA is made?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Biological Context:

In natural sexual reproduction, recombinant DNA is formed during Meiosis I, specifically at the Pachytene stage of Prophase I.

Detailed Meiotic Mechanism:

  1. During the preceding Zygotene stage, homologous maternal and paternal chromosomes pair lengthwise to form bivalents (synaptonemal complex).
  2. At the Pachytene stage, non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes undergo physical exchange of genetic segments called Crossing Over.
  3. This process is an enzyme-mediated breakage and reciprocal reunion catalyzed by the multi-enzyme complex Recombinase (endonucleases and ligases).
  4. The breaking, swapping, and re-joining of non-sister chromatid DNA strands creates brand new chimeric combinations of maternal and paternal alleles, producing natural recombinant DNA (visualised later at Diplotene as X-shaped chiasmata).

Q9
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Can you think and answer how a reporter enzyme can be used to monitor transformation of host cells by genetically engineered DNA?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

A reporter enzyme (encoded by a reporter gene) provides an easily assayable phenotypic signal (such as a colour change, fluorescence, or luminescence) that allows researchers to visually distinguish transformed recombinant host cells from non-recombinants without lethal selection:

Classic Example: β-Galactosidase Reporter (Insertional Inactivation / Blue-White Screening):

  1. Vector Construction: The plasmid vector (e.g., pUC19) carries the reporter gene lacZ, which codes for the enzyme β-galactosidase. The multiple cloning site (MCS) is engineered directly inside the coding sequence of lacZ.
  2. Non-Recombinant Vector (No foreign insert):
    • The lacZ gene remains intact and fully functional.
    • The translated active β-galactosidase hydrolyses a chromogenic substrate (X-gal) present in the agar medium, producing a bright blue indole precipitate.
    • Result: Colonies appear Blue.
  3. Recombinant Vector (Foreign gene successfully inserted):
    • Ligation of foreign DNA into the MCS causes insertional inactivation of the lacZ gene.
    • No functional β-galactosidase is produced. The chromogenic substrate X-gal is not hydrolysed.
    • Result: Recombinant transformant colonies appear White.

Conclusion: White colonies can be directly picked from the plate as confirmed recombinants, eliminating tedious replica plating.

CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Definition of reporter enzyme: provides detectable phenotypic signal (colour/luminescence)1 Mark
Mechanism of insertional inactivation of reporter gene (e.g. lacZ encoding beta-galactosidase)1 Mark
Differentiating outcomes on X-gal: Blue colonies (non-recombinants) vs White colonies (successful recombinants)1 Mark
Q10
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Describe briefly the following:
(a) Origin of replication
(b) Bioreactors
(c) Downstream processing
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution
(a) Origin of Replication (ori)

A specific conserved nucleotide sequence in a DNA molecule or plasmid vector where replication is initiated. Any piece of foreign DNA linked to the ori sequence is replicated along with the host genome. The ori sequence also controls the copy number of the linked foreign gene; cloning into a vector whose ori supports high copy numbers ensures massive yield of the recombinant gene.

(b) Bioreactors

Large cylindrical vessels (100–1,000 litres) engineered to biologically convert raw substrate materials into specific recombinant proteins or bioactive products using living microbial, plant, or animal cells. They provide optimal growth parameters (temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, nutrients, and vitamins) through automated agitation impellers, spargers, and monitoring probes.

(c) Downstream Processing

The collective series of post-fermentation separation, isolation, and purification processes that convert raw fermented culture broth into a finished, clinically safe, marketable commercial product. It involves cell disruption, centrifugation, membrane ultrafiltration, chromatography, formulation with preservatives, strict quality control testing, and clinical trials for pharmaceutical drugs.

CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
(a) Origin of replication: Sequence initiating replication and controlling vector copy number1 Mark
(b) Bioreactors: Large-scale vessels providing automated optimal growth conditions (pH, temp, aeration)1 Mark
(c) Downstream processing: Post-fermentation recovery, separation, purification, and quality control1 Mark
Q11
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Explain briefly:
(a) PCR
(b) Restriction enzymes and DNA
(c) Chitinase
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution
(a) Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

An in vitro molecular technique invented by Kary Mullis (1983) to amplify a specific target segment of DNA into billions of copies in a matter of hours. It proceeds through repeated automated thermal cycles consisting of three steps: Denaturation of dsDNA at 94°C, Annealing of oligonucleotide primers at 50–60°C, and Extension at 72°C catalyzed by a thermostable DNA polymerase (Taq Polymerase isolated from Thermus aquaticus) using dNTP substrates.

(b) Restriction Enzymes and DNA

Bacterial enzymes acting as "molecular scissors" that recognize specific short palindromic base sequences (4–8 bp) in double-stranded DNA and hydrolyse the phosphodiester bonds at specific points on both strands. They generate either sticky overhanging ends (e.g., EcoRI, HindIII) or blunt ends (e.g., SmaI), allowing foreign genes and cloning vectors cut with the same enzyme to be seamlessly joined by DNA ligase.

(c) Chitinase

A hydrolytic enzyme used during DNA isolation to enzymatically digest the tough, rigid chitinaceous cell wall of fungal cells (e.g., yeast or mold), releasing the intact cellular contents and pure genomic DNA for recombinant DNA experiments.

CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
(a) PCR: In vitro DNA amplification via Denaturation, Annealing, and Taq polymerase Extension1 Mark
(b) Restriction enzymes: Molecular scissors cutting palindromic sequences to yield sticky/blunt ends1 Mark
(c) Chitinase: Hydrolytic enzyme digesting fungal chitin cell walls to release pure genomic DNA1 Mark
Q12
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Discuss with your teacher and find out how to distinguish between:
(a) Plasmid DNA and Chromosomal DNA
(b) RNA and DNA
(c) Exonuclease and Endonuclease
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution
(a) Plasmid DNA vs Chromosomal DNA
Plasmid DNA Chromosomal DNA
Extra-chromosomal, small, circular double-stranded DNA capable of autonomous replication. Primary genomic, massive linear (eukaryotes) or large circular (prokaryotes) master DNA.
Dispersion is dispensable for routine survival; carries accessory traits (e.g., antibiotic resistance). Extensively used as cloning vector. Essential for basic metabolism, cell survival, growth, and reproduction.
(b) RNA vs DNA
RNA (Ribonucleic Acid) DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid)
Contains ribose sugar (possesses reactive 2'-OH group). Contains 2'-deoxyribose sugar (lacks reactive oxygen at 2' carbon).
Contains the pyrimidine base Uracil (U). Usually single-stranded and chemically labile. Contains the pyrimidine base Thymine (5-methyl uracil). Double-stranded and exceptionally stable.
(c) Exonuclease vs Endonuclease
Exonuclease Endonuclease
Cleaves nucleotides sequentially one-by-one from the terminal free ends (5'-end or 3'-end) of a DNA strand. Cleaves internal phosphodiester bonds at specific positions within the DNA strand.
Cannot generate internal fragments or specific sticky ends for molecular cloning. Restriction endonucleases recognize specific palindromes and cut internally, serving as indispensable tools for creating recombinant DNA.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
(a) Plasmid vs Chromosomal DNA (extra-chromosomal autonomous vector vs master genomic blueprint)1 Mark
(b) RNA vs DNA (ribose with 2'-OH & Uracil vs deoxyribose & Thymine)1 Mark
(c) Exonuclease vs Endonuclease (terminal cleavage vs internal cleavage at palindromic sites)1 Mark
