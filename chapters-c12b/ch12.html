NCERT Exercises (14 Verbatim Questions)
Q1
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Fill in the blanks:
(a) Plants are called as_________because they fix carbon dioxide.
(b) In an ecosystem dominated by trees, the pyramid (of numbers) is_________type.
(c) In aquatic ecosystems, the limiting factor for the productivity is_________.
(d) Common detritivores in our ecosystem are_________.
(e) The major reservoir of carbon on earth is_________.
[2.5 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution
(a) Plants are called as autotrophs (producers) because they fix carbon dioxide through photosynthesis.
(b) In an ecosystem dominated by trees, the pyramid (of numbers) is inverted type (a single tree supports hundreds of frugivorous birds and thousands of parasites).
(c) In aquatic ecosystems, the limiting factor for the productivity is light (and nutrients like nitrogen/phosphorus).
(d) Common detritivores in our ecosystem are earthworms (and termites, woodlice).
(e) The major reservoir of carbon on earth is the oceans (holding roughly 71% of all global dissolved carbon).
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
(a) Autotrophs / Producers0.5 Mark
(b) Inverted (spindle-shaped/inverted)0.5 Mark
(c) Light / Sunlight (or nutrients)0.5 Mark
(d) Earthworm0.5 Mark
(e) Oceans0.5 Mark
Q2
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Which one of the following has the largest population in a food chain?
(a) Producers
(b) Primary consumers
(c) Secondary consumers
(d) Decomposers
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Correct Option: (d) Decomposers

Detailed Scientific Explanation:

  • In any natural ecological food web, decomposers (saprotrophic bacteria, actinomycetes, and microscopic fungi) inhabit the soil and sediment litter in trillions of individuals per square meter.
  • While producers (phytoplankton or grass blades) occupy the base of grazing pyramids, the sheer numerical density of microscopic decomposers breaking down organic matter vastly outstrips all macroscopic autotrophs and consumers combined.
  • Note on NCERT alternative: If restricted strictly to a classical Grazing Food Chain (GFC) excluding saprotrophs, (a) Producers have the largest population. However, ecologically across the entire ecosystem, (d) Decomposers represent the largest population.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Correct choice: (d) Decomposers (or (a) Producers in strict GFC context)0.5 Mark
Ecological justification: Microscopic saprophytic bacteria/fungi exist in trillions per unit area0.5 Mark
Q3
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
The second trophic level in a lake is —
(a) Phytoplankton
(b) Zooplankton
(c) Benthos
(d) Fishes
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Correct Option: (b) Zooplankton

Explanation:

  • First Trophic Level (T1 - Primary Producers): Microscopic photosynthetic floating autotrophs, i.e., Phytoplankton (diatoms, green algae, cyanobacteria).
  • Second Trophic Level (T2 - Primary Consumers / Herbivores): Microscopic floating herbivores that feed directly upon phytoplankton, i.e., Zooplankton (rotifers, copepods, cladocerans).
  • Third Trophic Level (T3 - Secondary Consumers): Small planktivorous fishes.
  • Fourth Trophic Level (T4 - Tertiary Consumers): Large predatory fishes.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Correct choice: (b) Zooplankton0.5 Mark
Trophic position: Zooplankton are primary consumers (T2) grazing on phytoplankton (T1)0.5 Mark
Q4
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Secondary producers are —
(a) Herbivores
(b) Producers
(c) Carnivores
(d) None of the above
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Correct Option: (a) Herbivores

Explanation:

  • Green plants and photosynthetic autotrophs synthesize organic matter from inorganic CO2 and sunlight, and are termed Primary Producers.
  • Herbivores (Primary Consumers) consume plant biomass and assimilate it into new animal biomass (flesh, muscle tissue). This newly produced organic animal matter is then made available as food for the next higher trophic level (carnivores).
  • Therefore, herbivores are formally designated as Secondary Producers.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Correct choice: (a) Herbivores0.5 Mark
Rationale: Herbivores assimilate plant food into animal biomass available for higher trophic levels0.5 Mark
Q5
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
What is the percentage of photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) in the incident solar radiation?
(a) 100%
(b) 50%
(c) 1-5%
(d) 2-10%
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Correct Option: (b) 50% (less than 50%)

Scientific Explanation:

  • Of the total incident solar radiation reaching the earth's atmosphere, less than 50% constitutes Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR) (wavelength range between 400 nm to 700 nm). The rest consists of ultraviolet (UV) and infrared (heat) radiation.
  • Plants and photosynthetic autotrophs capture only 2–10% of this PAR (equivalent to 1–5% of total incident solar radiation) to sustain the entire biosphere.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Correct choice: (b) 50% (or less than 50%)0.5 Mark
Clarification: PAR constitutes <50% of incident sunlight, of which plants capture 2-10%0.5 Mark
Q6
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Distinguish between:
(a) Grazing food chain and detritus food chain
(b) Production and decomposition
(c) Upright and inverted pyramid
(d) Food chain and Food web
(e) Top consumer and Primary consumer
(f) Primary and secondary productivity
[6 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution
(a) Grazing Food Chain (GFC) vs Detritus Food Chain (DFC)
Grazing Food Chain (GFC) Detritus Food Chain (DFC)
Starts with living green photosynthetic plants (primary producers) as the primary energy source. Starts with dead organic matter (detritus / leaf litter) as the primary energy source.
Major conduit of energy flow in aquatic ecosystems. Much larger fraction of energy flows through DFC in terrestrial forest ecosystems.
(b) Production vs Decomposition
Production Decomposition
Anabolic process where organic biomass is synthesised by autotrophs using solar energy and CO2. Catabolic process where complex dead organic matter is broken down into simple inorganic nutrients.
Requires solar energy input; increases standing biomass. Releases energy and recycles essential mineral nutrients back to soil/water.
(c) Upright Pyramid vs Inverted Pyramid
Upright Pyramid Inverted Pyramid
Base (producers) is broad; apex (carnivores) narrows progressively (e.g., Grassland number/biomass). Base (producers) is narrow and small; apex (consumers) is broad and heavy.
Energy pyramid is always upright. Biomass pyramid in sea/oceans is inverted (tiny phytoplankton standing crop supports large fishes).
(d) Food Chain vs Food Web
Food Chain Food Web
A single, linear, unbranched pathway of feeding relationships and energy transfer. A complex, interconnected, multidirectional network of multiple intersecting food chains.
Vulnerable to collapse if one species is eliminated. Provides alternative food pathways, ensuring high ecosystem stability and resilience.
(e) Top Consumer vs Primary Consumer
Top Consumer (Apex Carnivore) Primary Consumer (Herbivore)
Occupies the highest trophic level (T4 or T5); is not preyed upon by any other animal in that ecosystem (e.g., Lion, Eagle, Shark). Occupies the second trophic level (T2); feeds directly on living plant matter (e.g., Deer, Cow, Zooplankton).
Receives the lowest fraction of original energy (Lindeman's law). Receives the highest fraction of energy directly from primary producers.
(f) Primary Productivity vs Secondary Productivity
Primary Productivity Secondary Productivity
Rate at which biomass or organic matter is produced per unit area by green photosynthetic autotrophs (producers). Rate of formation of new organic matter by heterotrophic consumers (herbivores/carnivores).
Divided into Gross Primary (GPP) and Net Primary (NPP). Depends entirely upon the available net primary productivity of the preceding trophic level.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
(a) GFC vs DFC: Living producer base vs dead organic detritus base1 Mark
(b) Production vs Decomposition: Anabolic synthesis vs catabolic mineral breakdown1 Mark
(c) Upright vs Inverted: Broad base vs broad apex (aquatic biomass)1 Mark
(d) Food chain vs Food web: Linear pathway vs interconnected network1 Mark
(e) Top consumer vs Primary consumer: Apex carnivore vs herbivore1 Mark
(f) Primary vs Secondary productivity: Rate of synthesis by autotrophs vs consumers1 Mark
Q7
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Describe the components of an ecosystem.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

An ecosystem is a functional, self-sustaining biological unit of nature where living organisms interact among themselves and with the surrounding physical environment. It consists of two major structural components:

1. Abiotic Components (Physical & Chemical Factors):

  • Climatic Factors: Sunlight (solar radiation drives photosynthesis), Temperature, Humidity, Precipitation, and Wind.
  • Edaphic & Topographic Factors: Soil mineral composition, soil pH, moisture, topography, and organic humus content.
  • Inorganic & Organic Substances: Essential biogeochemical elements (C, N, P, S, Ca) and macromolecules (proteins, carbohydrates, lipids) cycling through the system.

2. Biotic Components (Living Organisms):

  • Producers (Autotrophs): Photosynthetic plants, phytoplankton, and photosynthetic bacteria that convert solar energy into chemical energy (biomass) via photosynthesis.
  • Consumers (Heterotrophs / Phagotrophs): Animals that depend on producers for food:
    • Primary Consumers (Herbivores): Feed directly on plants (e.g., deer, cattle, zooplankton).
    • Secondary Consumers (Primary Carnivores): Feed on herbivores (e.g., frogs, small fishes).
    • Tertiary Consumers (Top Carnivores): Apex predators (e.g., lions, tigers, eagles).
  • Decomposers (Saprotrophs / Microconsumers): Heterotrophic bacteria and fungi that secrete extracellular digestive enzymes to break down complex dead organic detritus, releasing mineral nutrients back into the abiotic reservoir.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Abiotic components: Climatic (light, temperature) and edaphic/chemical factors (soil, nutrients)1 Mark
Biotic producers: Autotrophs fixing solar energy into organic matter1 Mark
Biotic consumers (herbivores, carnivores) and saprotrophic decomposers (bacteria/fungi)1 Mark
Q8
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Define ecological pyramids and describe with examples, pyramids of number and biomass.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Definition:

An ecological pyramid is a graphic representation of the trophic structure and function of an ecosystem, where successive trophic levels are plotted in steps starting with producers at the base and apex carnivores at the top.

1. Pyramid of Numbers:

  • Represents the total number of individual organisms inhabiting each trophic level per unit area.
  • Upright in Grassland: Millions of grass plants support thousands of grasshoppers, which support hundreds of frogs, which support a few snakes and top birds of prey.
  • Inverted in Tree Ecosystem: A single large oak/banyan tree (1 producer) supports hundreds of herbivorous birds, which in turn support thousands of parasitic lice, ticks, and fleas (hyperparasites).

2. Pyramid of Biomass:

  • Represents the total dry weight / organic biomass of standing crop present at each trophic level.
  • Upright in Terrestrial Forest: Massive biomass of trees and shrubs > biomass of herbivores > biomass of carnivores.
  • Inverted in Aquatic (Sea / Lake) Ecosystem: Phytoplankton have a very small standing biomass at any given moment because of their microscopic size and rapid turnover (lifespan of hours/days). This small biomass supports a significantly larger standing biomass of zooplankton and predatory fishes.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Definition of ecological pyramid as graphical representation of trophic parameters1 Mark
Pyramid of number with grassland (upright) and single tree (inverted) examples1 Mark
Pyramid of biomass with terrestrial forest (upright) and ocean/sea (inverted) examples1 Mark
Q9
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
What is primary productivity? Give brief description of factors that affect primary productivity.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Definition of Primary Productivity:

Primary productivity is defined as the rate at which organic biomass is synthesized per unit area over a specified time period by photosynthetic autotrophs (plants and phytoplankton) through photosynthesis, expressed in units of g m-2 yr-1 or kcal m-2 yr-1.

  • Gross Primary Productivity (GPP): The total rate of production of organic matter during photosynthesis.
  • Net Primary Productivity (NPP): The biomass remaining after meeting the plant's own respiratory losses (R):
    NPP = GPP - R

Key Factors Regulating Primary Productivity:

  1. Plant Species & Photosynthetic Capacity: The genetic architecture and physiological efficiency of plant species inhabiting a particular area (e.g., C4 plants like sugarcane and maize have higher productivity than C3 plants).
  2. Availability of Solar Radiation (Light): Intensity, quality, and photoperiod of sunlight; light is a critical limiting factor in aquatic deep zones and dense forest understories.
  3. Temperature: Governs the enzymatic kinetics of photosynthetic enzymes (e.g., RuBisCO). Tropical ecosystems exhibit peak productivity due to optimal warm temperatures year-round.
  4. Soil Moisture & Water Availability: Water is an essential electron donor in photolysis and prevents stomatal closure. Deserts have exceptionally low productivity due to severe drought.
  5. Nutrient Availability: Availability of essential minerals (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium). Marine open oceans have low productivity due to nitrogen/phosphorus limitation.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Definition of primary productivity and equation NPP = GPP - R1 Mark
Measurement units: g m^-2 yr^-1 or kcal m^-2 yr^-10.5 Mark
Factors: Plant species capacity, solar radiation, temperature, water availability, and nutrients1.5 Marks
Q10
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Define decomposition and describe the processes and products of decomposition.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Definition:

Decomposition is the oxygen-requiring physical and biochemical breakdown of complex, insoluble dead organic matter (detritus such as fallen leaves, dead plant debris, animal carcasses, and fecal matter) into simple, soluble inorganic substances (carbon dioxide, water, and mineral nutrients).

Five Sequential Biological Processes of Decomposition:

  1. 1. Fragmentation: Detritivores (such as earthworms) break down large pieces of detritus into fine particulate matter, greatly increasing the surface area for microbial enzyme action.
  2. 2. Leaching: Water-soluble inorganic nutrients percolate downward through the soil profile along with gravitational water and become precipitated as unavailable subterranean salts.
  3. 3. Catabolism: Bacterial and fungal extracellular saprotrophic enzymes degrade detritus into simple inorganic compounds.
  4. 4. Humification: Leads to the accumulation of humus, a dark-coloured, amorphous colloidal organic substance that is highly resistant to microbial decomposition, undergoes extremely slow degradation, acts as a reservoir of nutrients, and improves soil water retention.
  5. 5. Mineralisation: The slow, continuous degradation of humus by specialized microbes, releasing inorganic mineral ions (NH4+, PO43-, K+) back into the soil solution for root absorption.

Final Products: Humus, CO2, H2O, and plant-available mineral ions.

Definition of decomposition (oxygen-requiring breakdown of detritus into minerals)1 Mark

Q11
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Give an account of energy flow in an ecosystem.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Energy flow through an ecosystem obeys the fundamental laws of thermodynamics and is strictly unidirectional (one-way):

  1. Incident Solar Radiation & Capture:
    • The Sun is the ultimate source of energy for all terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems on Earth (except deep-sea hydrothermal ecosystems).
    • Less than 50% of incident sunlight is Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR). Plants and photosynthetic autotrophs capture only 2–10% of this PAR to convert it into chemical bond energy (organic biomass).
  2. Obedience to Laws of Thermodynamics:
    • First Law: Energy is neither created nor destroyed; radiant solar energy is transformed into chemical bond energy in glucose.
    • Second Law: Ecosystems require a constant influx of energy to counteract entropy and maintain structural order; at each trophic transfer, substantial energy is dissipated as non-recoverable metabolic heat.
  3. Lindeman's 10% Law of Energy Transfer:
    • Formulated by Raymond Lindeman (1942). Only about 10% of the energy stored as biomass at one trophic level is transferred to and assimilated into biomass by the next higher trophic level.
    • The remaining 90% is expended during cellular respiration (maintenance metabolism) or lost as heat and unassimilated waste.
    • Example: 10,000 J in Plants → 1,000 J in Herbivores → 100 J in Carnivores → 10 J in Top Carnivores.
  4. Limitation on Food Chain Length: Because of this steep 90% energy loss at each step, food chains rarely exceed 4 to 5 trophic levels, beyond which the remaining energy is insufficient to support another viable predator population.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Solar energy capture (Sun as ultimate source, <50% PAR, 2-10% captured by plants)1 Mark
Thermodynamic laws and unidirectional energy flow without recycling1 Mark

Q12
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Write important features of a sedimentary cycle in an ecosystem.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Biogeochemical nutrient cycles are broadly divided into gaseous and sedimentary cycles. Phosphorus and sulphur cycles exemplify sedimentary cycles:

  1. Lithospheric Reservoir:
    • Unlike gaseous cycles (where the main reservoir is the atmosphere or ocean), the primary reservoir of a sedimentary cycle is located in the Earth's crust (lithosphere / sedimentary rocks) in the form of mineral deposits.
  2. Slow Weathering Input:
    • Nutrients enter the biological cycle through the extremely slow mechanical, chemical, and biological weathering and erosion of phosphate-containing rocks, dissolving minute quantities into the soil solution.
  3. Biological Assimilation & Food Chain Circulation:
    • Plant roots absorb dissolved inorganic phosphates (PO43-) from soil moisture. The nutrient is incorporated into nucleic acids (DNA, RNA), ATP, GTP, and cellular phospholipids.
    • Herbivores and carnivores obtain phosphorus sequentially by consuming lower trophic biomass.
  4. Return via Decomposition:
    • Excreta and dead organic detritus are decomposed by phosphate-solubilising bacteria, returning inorganic phosphate back to the soil.
  5. Absence of Significant Respiratory/Atmospheric Gaseous Phase:
    • In contrast to the carbon cycle, sedimentary cycles have no gaseous atmospheric exchange or respiratory release into the air. Phosphorus inputs from rainfall are negligible.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Primary reservoir in Earth's crust (sedimentary rocks)1 Mark
Entry via slow rock weathering into soil solution and uptake by roots1 Mark
Contrast with gaseous cycles: Negligible atmospheric/rainfall component and no respiratory release1 Mark
Q13
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Outline salient features of carbon cycling in an ecosystem.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Carbon constitutes 49% of the dry weight of living organisms (second only to water) and cycles continuously through a global gaseous biogeochemical cycle:

  1. Global Carbon Reservoirs:
    • Oceanic Reservoir (71%): Oceans hold the massive majority of the biosphere's dissolved carbon (~71%), acting as the planetary carbon buffer and regulating atmospheric carbon dioxide levels.
    • Fossil Fuel Reservoir: Immense quantities of carbon remain locked in underground sedimentary strata as coal, petroleum, and natural gas.
    • Atmospheric Reservoir: The atmosphere holds only about 1% of total global carbon (approx 0.04% CO2 by volume).
  2. Assimilation via Photosynthesis:
    • An astounding 4 × 1013 kg (40 billion tonnes) of carbon is fixed annually in the biosphere through terrestrial and marine photosynthesis.
  3. Return Pathways of Carbon into Atmosphere:
    • Cellular Respiration: Living organisms (plants, animals, decomposers) continuously oxidize glucose, releasing CO2 gas back into the air.
    • Decomposition: Saprophytes catabolize detritus, releasing immobilized carbon as CO2.
    • Combustion of Fossil Fuels & Biomass: Forest fires, burning of firewood, and combustion of fossil fuels (coal, diesel, petrol) in transport and power plants rapidly inject CO2 into the atmosphere.
    • Volcanic Degassing: Volcanic eruptions emit juvenile carbon dioxide.

Carbon reservoirs: Oceans (71% dissolved carbon), fossil fuels, and atmosphere (1%)1 Mark

Q14
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Explain the 10 percent law of energy transfer with an example.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Statement of Lindeman's 10% Law (1942):

During the transfer of organic energy from one trophic level to the next higher trophic level, only about 10% of the energy is conserved and stored as new biomass; the remaining 90% is consumed in cellular respiration (metabolic maintenance) or dissipated into the environment as unrecoverable heat.

Demonstrative Example in a 4-Tier Terrestrial Food Chain:

Assume an incident solar radiation yields 10,000 Joules of Net Primary Productivity at the plant producer level:

  • Trophic Level 1 (T1 - Primary Producers / Grass):
    Stored Chemical Energy = 10,000 J
  • Trophic Level 2 (T2 - Primary Consumers / Deer):
    Herbivores consume the grass, but 90% is lost to maintenance, movement, and heat.
    Energy transferred = 10% of 10,000 J = 1,000 J
  • Trophic Level 3 (T3 - Secondary Consumers / Wolves):
    Carnivores hunt and consume herbivores.
    Energy transferred = 10% of 1,000 J = 100 J
  • Trophic Level 4 (T4 - Tertiary Consumers / Lions):
    Top predators consume secondary consumers.
    Energy transferred = 10% of 100 J = 10 J

Ecological Implication: Because energy diminishes exponentially by a factor of 10 at each successive tier, higher carnivores require expansive hunting ranges to harvest sufficient calories, and food chains are strictly constrained to 4 or 5 trophic levels.
