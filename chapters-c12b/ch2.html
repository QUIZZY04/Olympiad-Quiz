✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Key Functional Components of Female Reproductive System:

  1. Ovaries: Primary female sex organs producing the female gamete (ovum) and steroid hormones (estrogen and progesterone).
  2. Fallopian Tubes (Oviducts): Each $10-12 \text{ cm}$ long, comprising three parts:

  4. Cervix and Vagina: The cervical canal along with vagina forms the birth canal.

Q 2.5
Exercise 2.5
Describe the microscopic structure of a seminiferous tubule.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Microscopic Structure of Seminiferous Tubule:
Each testicular lobule contains 1 to 3 highly coiled seminiferous tubules lined internally by a specialised germinal epithelium containing two types of cells:

  1. Male Germ Cells (Spermatogonia): Diploid stem cells that undergo meiotic divisions to successively produce primary spermatocytes, secondary spermatocytes, spermatids, and finally mature spermatozoa.
  2. Sertoli Cells (Nurse Cells): Large, elongated pyramid-shaped somatic cells that provide physical support, nutritional nourishment, and chemical factors to developing spermatogenic cells. They also secrete androgen-binding protein (ABP) and inhibin, and form the blood-testis barrier.
  3. Interstitial Space: The connective tissue regions outside the seminiferous tubules contain small blood vessels and clusters of polyhedral Leydig cells (interstitial cells), which synthesise and secrete androgens (testosterone) under LH stimulation, along with immunocompetent cells.

Q 2.6
Exercise 2.6
What is spermatogenesis? Briefly describe the process of spermatogenesis with a schematic representation.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

1. Definition:
Spermatogenesis is the biological process of formation of mature, motile haploid spermatozoa from diploid immature male germ cells (spermatogonia) within the seminiferous tubules of testes, initiating at puberty.

2. Stages of Spermatogenesis:

  1. Multiplication Phase: Spermatogonia ($2n = 46$) situated on the inner wall of seminiferous tubules multiply by repeated mitosis.
  2. Growth Phase: Some spermatogonia enlarge to form diploid primary spermatocytes ($2n$).
  3. Maturation Phase (Meiosis):
    • Each primary spermatocyte undergoes Meiosis I (reductional division) to form two equal haploid secondary spermatocytes ($n = 23$).
    • Secondary spermatocytes undergo Meiosis II (equational division) to produce four equal haploid spermatids ($n = 23$).
  4. Spermiogenesis: The non-motile, spherical spermatids are transformed into specialised, motile, flagellated spermatozoa (sperms).

Q 2.7
Exercise 2.7
Name the hormones involved in regulation of spermatogenesis and explain their physiological roles.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Hormonal Regulation of Spermatogenesis:
Spermatogenesis is initiated at puberty due to the significant increase in the secretion of GnRH (Gonadotropin Releasing Hormone) from the hypothalamus.

  1. GnRH: Acts on the anterior pituitary gland and stimulates the secretion of two gonadotropins: LH and FSH.
  2. LH (Luteinising Hormone / ICSH): Acts directly on the Leydig cells and stimulates them to synthesise and secrete androgens (testosterone). Testosterone in turn triggers and maintains spermatogenesis.
  3. FSH (Follicle Stimulating Hormone): Acts on Sertoli cells and stimulates secretion of Androgen Binding Protein (ABP) (which concentrates testosterone in seminiferous tubules) and factors essential for spermiogenesis.
  4. Inhibin: Secreted by Sertoli cells, exerts negative feedback on anterior pituitary to suppress excess FSH release when sperm count is high.

Q 2.8
Exercise 2.8
Define spermiogenesis and spermiation.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

1. Spermiogenesis:
The physiological and morphological transformation of non-motile, round, haploid spermatids into mature, motile spermatozoa (sperms). During this process, the Golgi complex condenses into the acrosome, the nucleus condenses, mitochondria spiral around the axial filament in the middle piece, and a long flagellar tail is formed.

2. Spermiation:
The final detachment and release of fully formed mature spermatozoa from the Sertoli cells into the central lumen of the seminiferous tubule.

Q 2.9
Exercise 2.9
Draw a labelled diagram of human sperm. Describe the structure and function of each region.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Structural Regions of Human Spermatozoon:

  1. Head: Contains an elongated haploid nucleus ($n = 23$) carrying paternal genetic material. Its anterior two-thirds is capped by the acrosome, a lysosome-derived vesicle filled with proteolytic hydrolytic enzymes (hyaluronidase, corona penetrating enzyme, acrosin) essential for penetrating the outer coats of the ovum.
  2. Neck: Very short segment containing a proximal centriole (initiates cleavage divisions in the fertilised egg) and a distal centriole (gives rise to the axial filament of the tail).
  3. Middle Piece: Contains numerous densely packed mitochondria spiralling around the axial filament (nebenkern), providing metabolic energy (ATP) to power the tail for sperm motility.
  4. Tail: A long flagellum surrounded by a plasma membrane, executing vigorous undulating lashing movements that propel the sperm through the female reproductive tract towards the ampulla.

Q 2.10
Exercise 2.10
What are the major components of seminal plasma?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Components of Seminal Plasma:
Seminal plasma is the collective alkaline fluid secretion contributed by the male accessory glands (seminal vesicles $\sim 60-70\%$, prostate gland $\sim 20-30\%$, and bulbourethral glands $\sim 5\%$):

  1. Fructose: Unique monosaccharide secreted by seminal vesicles serving as the primary energetic nutrient for sperm motility (also used as forensic marker for rape confirmation).
  2. Calcium Ions ($Ca^{2+}$) and Enzymes: Required for sperm motility and activation.
  3. Prostaglandins: Stimulate reverse peristaltic contractions in the female reproductive tract to help propel sperms towards the fallopian tubes.
  4. Alkaline Buffers / Bicarbonates: Neutralise the acidic vaginal environment ($ ext{pH } 3.5 - 4.0$), safeguarding sperm survival.
  5. Mucus: Secreted by bulbourethral glands, facilitating lubrication of the penis.
  6. Clotting Enzymes & Fibrinolysin: Help in coagulation and subsequent liquefaction of semen.

Q 2.12
Exercise 2.12
What is oogenesis? Give a brief account of oogenesis.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

1. Definition:
Oogenesis is the biological process of formation of a mature haploid female gamete (ovum) within the ovary. Unlike spermatogenesis, it initiates during embryonic development.

2. Stages of Oogenesis:

  1. Prenatal Phase (Fetal Life): A couple of million gamete mother cells (oogonia, $2n$) are formed in each fetal ovary; no more are formed after birth. Oogonia enter Prophase-I of Meiosis-I and get temporarily arrested as primary oocytes ($2n$).
  2. Follicular Atresia: From birth to puberty, a large number of primary follicles degenerate, leaving only $60,000 - 80,000$ in each ovary at puberty.
  3. First Meiotic Division (At Puberty): Prior to ovulation, the primary oocyte completes its unequal Meiosis-I, producing a large haploid secondary oocyte ($n$) and a tiny first polar body ($n$).
  4. Second Meiotic Division (At Fertilisation): The secondary oocyte is arrested at Metaphase-II and ovulated. Meiosis-II is completed only upon sperm entry (fertilisation), producing a mature ovum (ootid, $n$) and a second polar body ($n$).

Q 2.13
Exercise 2.13
Draw a labelled diagram of a section through human ovary showing different stages of follicular development.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Sequential Stages Visible in Ovarian Section:

  1. Primary Follicle: Primary oocyte enclosed by a single layer of granulosa cells.
  2. Secondary Follicle: More granulosa layers surrounded by a connective tissue theca.
  3. Tertiary Follicle: Characterised by an inner fluid-filled cavity called the antrum, with theca differentiated into theca interna (vascular, estrogen-secreting) and theca externa.
  4. Mature Graafian Follicle: Large follicle bulging at ovarian surface, containing secondary oocyte surrounded by the zona pellucida and corona radiata.
  5. Ovulation: Rupture of Graafian follicle releasing the secondary oocyte into peritoneal cavity.
  6. Corpus Luteum: Remnants of ruptured follicle luteinised into an endocrine body secreting progesterone.
  7. Corpus Albicans: Degenerated white fibrous scar of corpus luteum when pregnancy fails.

Structure of Mature Graafian Follicle:

  7. Corona Radiata: Radially elongated granulosa cells adhering firmly to the exterior of the zona pellucida.

Q 2.17
Exercise 2.17
What is the menstrual cycle? Which hormones regulate the menstrual cycle?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

1. Definition:
The menstrual cycle is the rhythmic series of cyclic natural physiological changes occurring in the ovaries and the uterine endometrium of female primates (human, apes, monkeys) roughly every $28-29 \text{ days}$, from menarche (puberty) to menopause, preparing the uterus for potential pregnancy.

2. Hormones Regulating the Menstrual Cycle:

  1. Pituitary Gonadotropins:
    • FSH (Follicle Stimulating Hormone): Stimulates development of primary ovarian follicles into mature Graafian follicles and stimulates estrogen secretion.
    • LH (Luteinising Hormone): A rapid mid-cycle surge of LH (LH Surge on Day 14) induces the rupture of the Graafian follicle and release of the secondary oocyte (ovulation), and converts the ruptured follicle into the corpus luteum.
  2. Ovarian Steroid Hormones:
    • Estrogen: Secreted by growing follicles, promotes repair and proliferation of the uterine endometrium.
    • Progesterone: Secreted in large amounts by the corpus luteum during the secretory phase, maintaining the vascularised endometrium essential for implantation.

Q 2.18
Exercise 2.18
What is parturition? Which hormones are involved in the induction of parturition?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

1. Definition:
Parturition (childbirth) is the biological process of vigorous uterine contractions resulting in the expulsion or delivery of the mature fetus from the uterus through the birth canal at the end of gestation.

2. Neuroendocrine Mechanism & Hormones Involved:
Parturition is triggered by a complex neuroendocrine mechanism initiated by the fully developed fetus and placenta, generating mild uterine contractions termed the foetal ejection reflex.

  1. Oxytocin: The foetal ejection reflex triggers the release of oxytocin from the maternal posterior pituitary. Oxytocin acts on the uterine myometrium, causing stronger contractions, which in turn stimulates further oxytocin secretion (positive feedback loop) until the baby is expelled.
  2. Relaxin: Secreted by the ovary/placenta towards term to relax the pubic symphysis ligament and dilate the cervix.
  3. Estrogen and Progesterone: The estrogen-to-progesterone ratio increases, increasing oxytocin receptors on myometrial cells.
  4. Prostaglandins: Produced locally in the uterine wall, amplifying the intensity of contractions.

Q 2.19
Exercise 2.19
In our society women are often blamed for giving birth to daughters. Can you explain why this is scientifically completely incorrect?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Scientific Basis of Sex Determination in Humans:
Sex in human beings is determined by the chromosomal constitution of the fertilising sperm from the father, not the mother (XY sex-determination system).

  1. Female Homogamety: Females have two identical $X$ sex chromosomes ($44 + XX$). During oogenesis, all ova produced are genetically identical, each carrying an $X$ chromosome ($22 + X$). The mother is completely homogametic.
  2. Male Heterogamety: Males have two different sex chromosomes ($44 + XY$). During spermatogenesis, the father produces two equal types of sperms in a $50:50$ ratio:
    • $50\%$ gynosperms carrying an $X$ chromosome ($22 + X$).
    • $50\%$ androsperms carrying a $Y$ chromosome ($22 + Y$).
  3. Fertilisation Outcome:
    • If an $X$-carrying sperm fertilises the ovum: $(22 + X) + (22 + X) = 44 + XX implies$ Female child (daughter).
    • If a $Y$-carrying sperm fertilises the ovum: $(22 + Y) + (22 + X) = 44 + XY implies$ Male child (son).

Conclusion: The genetic sex of the baby depends strictly on whether an $X$ or $Y$ sperm from the father fertilises the ovum. Blaming the mother is scientifically unfounded and socially unjust.

Q 2.20
Exercise 2.20
How many eggs are released by a human ovary in a month? How many eggs do you think would have been released if the mother gave birth to identical twins? Are fraternal twins different?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

1. Eggs Released in a Month:
Normally, only one single egg (secondary oocyte) is released by one of the ovaries during each menstrual cycle (roughly every 28 days), with the ovaries typically alternating each month.

2. In Case of Identical (Monozygotic) Twins:
Only one egg was released. A single ovum is fertilised by a single sperm to form one diploid zygote. During early cleavage stages (blastocyst), the inner cell mass splits into two separate embryonic clusters, producing two genetically identical individuals of the same sex.

3. Fraternal (Dizygotic) Twins:
Yes, fraternal twins are fundamentally different.
• They arise when two separate eggs are released simultaneously during the same cycle (hyperovulation) and fertilised by two different sperms.
• They are no more genetically alike than ordinary siblings born at different times, and can be of the same sex or different sexes.
