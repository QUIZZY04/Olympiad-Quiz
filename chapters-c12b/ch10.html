Q1
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Crystals of Bt toxin produced by some bacteria do not kill the bacteria themselves because —
(a) bacteria are resistant to the toxin
(b) toxin is immature;
(c) toxin is inactive;
(d) bacteria enclose toxin in a special sac.
[1 Mark]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Correct Option: (c) toxin is inactive

Detailed Scientific Explanation:

  • The bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis produces insecticidal crystalline proteins during sporulation known as Bt toxin crystals.
  • Inside the bacterial cell, this toxin exists harmlessly as an inactive protoxin (prototoxin). It requires an alkaline environment and specific endoproteases to undergo activation.
  • Because the bacterial intracellular cytoplasm has a neutral pH, the toxin remains completely inert and crystalline, causing zero harm to the bacterium.
  • When a susceptible insect (e.g., lepidopteran bollworm) ingests the crop tissue, the alkaline pH of the insect midgut solubilises the crystals, and gut proteases cleave the protoxin into its active, lethal toxic conformation. The active toxin binds to surface receptors of midgut epithelial cells, creating pores that cause cell swelling, lysis, and death of the insect pest.

Q2
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
What are transgenic bacteria? Illustrate using any one example.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Definition:

Transgenic bacteria (genetically modified bacteria) are bacterial cells whose genome has been stably altered by the introduction and functional integration of a foreign gene (transgene) using recombinant DNA technology, enabling them to synthesize a specific heterologous protein.

Classic Example: Transgenic Escherichia coli for Human Insulin (Humulin) Production:

  1. In 1983, the American pharmaceutical company Eli Lilly prepared two synthetic DNA oligonucleotide sequences corresponding to the A chain (21 amino acids) and B chain (30 amino acids) of human insulin.
  2. These synthetic DNA sequences were linked to the β-galactosidase gene and separately introduced into plasmid vectors of Escherichia coli.
  3. The transgenic E. coli bacteria were cultured in large bioreactors to express individual insulin chains A and B.
  4. The chains were extracted, purified, and joined together chemically by creating synthetic disulphide (-S-S-) bonds to produce active human insulin (Humulin).

Q3
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Compare and contrast the advantages and disadvantages of production of genetically modified crops.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Genetically Modified (GM) crops carry beneficial foreign genes to confer targeted agronomic traits, but also pose potential ecological and health concerns:

1. Major Advantages of GM Crops
  • Enhanced Tolerance to Abiotic Stresses: Engineered to withstand harsh environmental extremes (drought, cold, frost, heat, and soil salinity).
  • Built-In Pest Resistance: Transgenic crops like Bt Cotton produce their own insecticidal Cry proteins, dramatically reducing reliance on harmful chemical pesticides.
  • Reduction in Post-Harvest Losses: Delayed fruit softening (e.g., Flavr Savr tomato via polygalacturonase antisense inhibition) extends shelf-life and reduces transport wastage.
  • Enhanced Nutritional Value: Biofortified crops, such as Golden Rice enriched with provitamin A (β-carotene), directly combat nutritional blindness in developing countries.
  • Increased Mineral Usage Efficiency: Prevents early exhaustion of soil fertility by enabling crops to utilize micronutrients more efficiently.
2. Potential Disadvantages & Concerns of GM Crops
  • Emergence of "Superweeds": Risk of transgene escape via accidental cross-pollination between herbicide-resistant GM crops and wild relatives.
  • Threat to Non-Target Biodiversity: Secretion of insecticidal proteins into soil or nectar could potentially harm beneficial pollinators (honeybees) or soil organisms.
  • Risk of Allergenicity in Humans: Introducing novel viral or bacterial genes may express uncharacterised proteins that provoke food allergies.
  • Corporate Monopolisation & Terminator Seeds: Multinationals patenting seed technologies and using Genetic Use Restriction Technology (terminator genes) forces farmers to repurchase seeds every season.

Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
What are Cry proteins? Name an organism that produce it. How has man exploited this protein to his benefit?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

1. Definition & Producing Organism:

  • Cry proteins (Crystal endotoxins) are toxic crystalline proteinaceous parasporal inclusions encoded by cry genes.
  • They are naturally produced by the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) during its sporulation phase.

2. Exploitation in Agricultural Biotechnology:

  1. Specific cry genes were isolated from Bacillus thuringiensis and cloned into crop plants (e.g., cotton, corn, brinjal, potato) using Agrobacterium tumefaciens plasmid vectors:
    • cryIAc and cryIIAb: Effectively control cotton bollworms.
    • cryIAb: Specifically controls corn borers.
  2. Transgenic Resistance Mechanism:
    • When insects chew on the transgenic crop tissues, they ingest the protoxin crystals.
    • The protoxin is solubilised in the alkaline insect midgut, where gut proteases cleave it into its active toxic form.
    • The activated toxin binds to receptors on midgut epithelial cells, forms lytic trans-membrane pores, causing osmotic swelling, cell lysis, and starvation-induced death of the pest.
  3. Human Benefit: Created high-yielding, pest-resistant crops (e.g., Bt Cotton), saving billions of dollars in crop losses, eliminating toxic synthetic pesticide spraying, and protecting farmworkers.

Q5
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
What is gene therapy? Illustrate using the example of adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency.
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Definition:

Gene therapy is a revolutionary collection of clinical methods that allows the correction of a hereditary genetic defect by inserting a normal, functional copy of a gene into the cells and tissues of an individual to take over the function of and compensate for the non-functional mutant gene.

Illustration: Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) Deficiency (SCID):

  1. Etiology: ADA deficiency is caused by the deletion or mutation of the gene coding for the enzyme adenosine deaminase. The lack of ADA leads to accumulation of toxic deoxyadenosine, destroying mature T and B lymphocytes and causing Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (SCID) ("bubble boy" disease).
  2. Historic Gene Therapy Procedure (1990):
    • In 1990, the first clinical gene therapy was performed by French Anderson and colleagues on a 4-year-old girl suffering from ADA deficiency.
    • Lymphocytes were harvested from the peripheral blood of the patient and cultured in vitro in the laboratory.
    • A functional human ADA cDNA was introduced into these cultured lymphocytes using a disarmed retroviral vector.
    • The genetically engineered lymphocytes expressing functional ADA were re-infused into the young patient's bloodstream.
  3. Limitation & Permanent Cure:
    • Because mature lymphocytes have a finite lifespan and are not immortal, the patient requires periodic, lifelong re-infusions of genetically engineered cells.
    • Permanent Cure: If the functional ADA gene isolated from healthy bone marrow is introduced into recipient cells at early embryonic stages, it provides a complete, lifelong permanent cure.

Q6
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Diagrammatically represent the experimental steps in cloning and expressing an human gene (say for growth hormone) into a bacterium like E. coli?
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Step-by-Step Experimental Protocol for Expressing Human Growth Hormone (hGH) in E. coli:

  1. 1. Isolation of Human Gene (cDNA): Human pituitary mRNA is reverse-transcribed to yield pure hGH cDNA (devoid of eukaryotic non-coding introns).
  2. 2. Cleavage of Plasmid Vector & Gene: The cloning vector (plasmid pBR322 / pUC) and the human hGH cDNA are digested with the same restriction endonuclease (e.g., EcoRI) to generate matching cohesive sticky ends.
  3. 3. Formation of Recombinant DNA: The cleaved vector and human cDNA fragment are incubated together in the presence of DNA ligase to covalently seal the phosphodiester bonds, yielding the recombinant plasmid.
  4. 4. Transformation into Host: Recombinant plasmids are introduced into competent E. coli bacterial cells made permeable by ice-cold CaCl2 treatment followed by a brief heat-shock at 42°C.
  5. 5. Selection of Transformants: Transformed bacterial colonies are selected using selectable antibiotic markers (e.g., ampicillin/tetracycline resistance or blue-white screening).
  6. 6. Mass Expression in Bioreactor: Selected recombinant E. coli clones are cultured in large-scale stirred-tank bioreactors under controlled induction, expressing high yields of recombinant Human Growth Hormone.
  7. 7. Downstream Processing: Lysis of bacterial cells, chromatographic purification, and formulation into injectable clinical somatotropin.

Q7
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Can you suggest a method to remove oil (hydrocarbon) from seeds based on your understanding of rDNA technology and chemistry of oil?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Biochemical & Genetic Engineering Strategy:

  1. Chemistry of Seed Oil:
    • Seed oils are triacylglycerols (triglycerides) synthesized through the glycerol-3-phosphate / Kennedy pathway.
    • Glycerol is esterified with three fatty acid chains, catalyzed sequentially by key rate-limiting enzymes such as Glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (GPAT) and Diacylglycerol acyltransferase (DGAT).
  2. Targeted Gene Silencing via RNA Interference (RNAi):
    • Isolate the cDNA sequence coding for the critical rate-limiting enzyme Diacylglycerol acyltransferase (DGAT).
    • Clone both the sense and antisense fragments of this gene into a plant expression vector under the control of a seed-specific promoter (such as the napin or phaseolin promoter).
    • Transform oil-seed plants (e.g., soybean, canola, or sunflower) using Agrobacterium tumefaciens.
    • During seed maturation, transcription of both strands forms a hairpin double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) in the developing embryonic cotyledons.
    • Dicer cleaves dsRNA into small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), which program the RISC complex to cleave and degrade the endogenous mRNA of DGAT.
  3. Result: Silencing DGAT eliminates triglyceride assembly specifically within the seed tissues without affecting vegetative plant growth. The carbon flux is redirected towards starch or protein accumulation, yielding oil-free / low-fat seeds.

Q8
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Find out on the internet what is golden rice.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Golden Rice is a genetically biofortified variety of rice (Oryza sativa) engineered to synthesize and accumulate β-carotene (provitamin A) in the edible starchy endosperm of the grain, giving the milled grain a characteristic golden-yellow colour.

Key Scientific Highlights:

  1. Inventors & Genetic Architecture:
    • Developed by Ingo Potrykus and Peter Beyer in 1999.
    • Wild rice naturally synthesizes β-carotene in its green photosynthetic leaves, but the biosynthetic pathway is switched off in the endosperm.
    • Two exogenous genes encoding the enzymes of the carotenoid biosynthetic pathway were introduced into the rice genome:
      • psy (Phytoene Synthase): Sourced from daffodil (Narcissus pseudonarcissus) or maize.
      • crtI (Phytoene Desaturase): Sourced from the soil bacterium Pantoea ananatis (formerly Erwinia uredovora).
  2. Humanitarian Mission:
    • Developed to combat Vitamin A Deficiency (VAD) in poverty-stricken populations across Southeast Asia and Africa where rice is the primary staple food.
    • VAD is the leading cause of preventable childhood blindness (xerophthalmia) and increases mortality from childhood diarrhoeal infections.
    • Inside the human body, consumed β-carotene is cleaved by intestinal mucosal enzymes into active Vitamin A (retinol).

Q9
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Does our blood have proteases and nucleases? If yes, why don't they digest our proteins and nucleic acids?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Yes, human blood contains proteases and nucleases.

1. Presence of Enzymes in Blood:

  • Proteases in Blood: The blood plasma contains numerous serine proteases that drive the blood coagulation cascade (e.g., Prothrombin, Factor X, Factor IX, Factor VII), fibrinolytic enzymes (Plasmin), and the complement system cascade (C1r, C1s).
  • Nucleases in Blood: Circulating blood contains trace amounts of secretory deoxyribonucleases (e.g., Serum DNase I) and ribonucleases (RNase 1) responsible for clearing extracellular cell-free DNA and RNA fragments released from apoptotic cells.

2. Why They Do Not Digest Our Own Body Proteins and Nucleic Acids:

  1. Synthesis as Inactive Zymogens (Pro-enzymes): Coagulation and complement proteases circulate in the bloodstream strictly as inactive pro-enzymes (zymogens). They are activated exclusively at localized sites of vascular injury through a highly regulated cascade of cofactor triggers.
  2. High Concentration of Endogenous Protease Inhibitors: Normal blood plasma contains massive concentrations of circulating antiproteases (such as α1-antitrypsin, α2-macroglobulin, Antithrombin III) that instantly bind and neutralize any free active proteases.
  3. Membrane Compartmentalisation & Protection of Nucleic Acids: Cellular DNA and RNA are strictly sequestered inside intact lipid bilayer plasma membranes and nuclear envelopes, physically shielded from extracellular nucleases. Extracellular serum nucleases only degrade foreign viral nucleic acids and debris from ruptured apoptotic cells.

Q10
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Consult internet and find out how to make orally active protein pharmaceutical. What is the major problem to be encountered?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

The Major Problem Encountered with Oral Protein Pharmaceuticals:

  1. Enzymatic Gastrointestinal Proteolysis: Proteins are biological polymers of amino acids. When ingested orally, powerful gastric proteases (pepsin in stomach at pH 1.5–2) and pancreatic proteases (trypsin, chymotrypsin, carboxypeptidases at alkaline pH in small intestine) rapidly hydrolyse peptide bonds, completely denaturing and degrading the therapeutic protein into inactive fragments.
  2. Poor Epithelial Permeability & Bioavailability: Proteins are large, hydrophilic macromolecules unable to cross the lipophilic intestinal epithelial cell membrane or mucosal tight junctions to reach the bloodstream intact.
  3. Hepatic First-Pass Metabolism: Any minor amount absorbed into mesenteric capillaries is routed via portal circulation directly to the liver, where it undergoes extensive degradation.

Modern Biotechnology Approaches to Engineer Orally Active Proteins:

  • Enteric Polymer Coating: Encapsulating the protein inside pH-sensitive acid-resistant polymers (e.g., methacrylic acid co-polymers) that remain insoluble in acidic gastric juice (pH < 3) but dissolve selectively in the neutral/alkaline small intestine (pH > 6.5).
  • Liposomal & Nanoparticle Encapsulation: Packaging the therapeutic protein inside biocompatible lipid nanocarriers (liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles) or biodegradable polymer matrices (PLGA) that shield the protein from digestive enzymes and enhance mucosal endocytosis.
  • Co-administration with Protease Inhibitors & Permeation Enhancers: Formulating the drug alongside transient intestinal permeability enhancers (e.g., sodium caprate, bile salts) and safe enzyme inhibitors that reversibly loosen tight junctions for systemic absorption.

Q11
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Re-combinant DNA technology has simplified the process of finding out the structure of a gene and its function. How?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Recombinant DNA (rDNA) technology has revolutionized molecular genetics by providing powerful analytical tools to dissect gene structure and assign cellular functions:

  1. Molecular Cloning and Gene Amplification:
    • Before rDNA technology, isolating a single gene from a eukaryotic genome containing billions of base pairs was practically impossible.
    • rDNA technology allows a target DNA segment to be cleaved with restriction enzymes, ligated into a plasmid vector, and cloned in bacteria, or exponentially amplified using Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR), yielding milligram quantities of ultra-pure gene fragments.
  2. High-Speed Sanger & Next-Generation DNA Sequencing:
    • Pure cloned gene fragments can be readily sequenced using dideoxy chain termination methods, directly revealing the complete primary nucleotide sequence (exons, introns, promoter TATA boxes, and termination signals).
  3. Site-Directed Mutagenesis:
    • Geneticists can introduce precise, premeditated point mutations into specific base pairs of the cloned gene in vitro and express it in host cells to observe functional alterations in the translated protein (e.g., identifying catalytic active-site amino acids).
  4. Gene Knockout & RNA Interference (RNAi):
    • Specific genes can be selectively disrupted ("knocked out") in model transgenic organisms (e.g., knockout mice) or silenced post-transcriptionally via RNAi. The resulting phenotypic deficiencies directly reveal the natural physiological role and function of the gene.

Q12
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Why are transgenic animals produced? Mention any four areas where they are used.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Transgenic animals are animals that have had their DNA manipulated to carry and stably express a foreign gene (transgene) across successive generations. Over 95% of all existing transgenic animals are mice.

Four Major Areas of Production & Utilisation:

  1. Normal Physiology and Development:
    • Engineered to study how specific genes are regulated and how they affect normal bodily development and growth factors (e.g., studying the biological role of Insulin-like Growth Factor - IGF).
  2. Study of Human Disease Pathology:
    • Transgenic models serve as living animal models for investigating the onset and progression of incurable human diseases and testing new gene therapies (e.g., transgenic animal models for cancer, cystic fibrosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and Alzheimer's disease).
  3. Production of Biological Products (Molecular Pharming):
    • Transgenic animals are engineered to produce valuable human therapeutic proteins in their milk:
      • Transgenic sheep producing α-1-antitrypsin used for treating emphysema.
      • In 1997, the first transgenic cow, Rosie, produced human protein-enriched milk (2.4 grams per litre) containing human α-lactalbumin, providing a more nutritionally balanced milk for human infants than natural cow milk.
  4. Vaccine Safety & Chemical Toxicity Testing:
    • Transgenic mice are developed for testing the safety of vaccines (e.g., Polio vaccine) before human trials. Transgenic animals carrying genes making them more sensitive to toxins are used for rapid toxicity safety testing.

Q13
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
What are the ethical issues associated with biotechnology?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Genetic engineering manipulates living organisms and ecosystems across species boundaries, raising significant bioethical, safety, and socioeconomic challenges:

  1. Disruption of Natural Order & Animal Welfare:
    • Altering the genetic makeup of animals and creating chimera models crosses natural species barriers. Subjecting animals to genetic deformities, painful chronic diseases, or commercial exploitation raises serious ethical concerns regarding animal suffering and intrinsic rights.
  2. Unforeseen Ecological Consequences:
    • The environmental release of genetically modified organisms (GMOs) may trigger unpredictable ecological consequences (e.g., gene flow to wild weeds creating uncontrollable "superweeds", toxicity to non-target pollinators, and loss of indigenous landrace biodiversity).
  3. Biopiracy & Corporate Monopolisation:
    • Biopiracy is the unauthorized commercial exploitation and patenting of bioresources, indigenous crops, and traditional ethnic knowledge of developing nations by wealthy multinational corporations without authentic consent or fair benefit sharing (e.g., foreign patents attempted on Basmati rice (Texas company RiceTec), Neem, and Turmeric).
  4. Regulatory Governance (Role of GEAC):
    • To address these concerns, the Government of India established the Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (GEAC) under the Ministry of Environment and Forests, responsible for evaluating the ethical validity of GMO research and deciding the biosafety clearance of introducing GM organisms into the public domain.
