Q1
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Group the following as nitrogenous bases and nucleosides: Adenine, Cytidine, Thymine, Guanosine, Uracil and Cytosine.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

In nucleic acids, a nitrogenous base is a heterocyclic nitrogen-containing aromatic compound (purine or pyrimidine). A nucleoside is formed when a nitrogenous base is covalently linked to a pentose sugar (ribose or 2'-deoxyribose) through an N-glycosidic bond.

1. Nitrogenous Bases

Heterocyclic aromatic bases without sugar linkage:

  • Adenine (Purine)
  • Thymine (Pyrimidine - in DNA)
  • Uracil (Pyrimidine - in RNA)
  • Cytosine (Pyrimidine)
2. Nucleosides

Base + Pentose Sugar (via N-glycosidic linkage):

  • Cytidine (Cytosine + Ribose / Deoxyribose)
  • Guanosine (Guanine + Ribose / Deoxyribose)

Q2
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
If a double stranded DNA has 20 per cent of cytosine, calculate the per cent of adenine in the DNA.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Application of Chargaff's Equimolar Rules:

Erwin Chargaff demonstrated that in any double-stranded DNA (dsDNA):

  1. The total purine content is always equal to total pyrimidine content: [A + G] = [T + C].
  2. Adenine pairs exclusively with Thymine by two hydrogen bonds: % A = % T.
  3. Guanine pairs exclusively with Cytosine by three hydrogen bonds: % G = % C.

Step-by-Step Calculation:

  • Given: Cytosine (C) = 20%
  • Since % G = % C, therefore: Guanine (G) = 20%
  • Total percentage of (G + C) = 20% + 20% = 40%
  • Total percentage of (A + T) = 100% - 40% = 60%
  • Since % A = % T, therefore:
    % Adenine (A) = 60% / 2 = 30%

Conclusion: The percentage of Adenine in this double-stranded DNA is 30%.

CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Statement of Chargaff's rule: %G = %C and %A = %T; %A + %T + %G + %C = 100%1 Mark
Calculation showing %G = 20%, %(A+T) = 60%, arriving at %Adenine = 30%1 Mark

Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
If the sequence of one strand of DNA is written as follows:
5' -ATGCATGCATGCATGCATGCATGCATGC-3'
Write down the sequence of complementary strand in 5' → 3' direction.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Rule of Complementary Base Pairing & Antiparallel Polarity:

  1. DNA strands are antiparallel (if one strand is 5' → 3', the complementary strand is 3' → 5').
  2. Base-pairing rules state that Adenine (A) pairs with Thymine (T) and Guanine (G) pairs with Cytosine (C).

Step 1: Write the complementary strand in the 3' → 5' direction:

Given strand:             5' -ATGCATGCATGCATGCATGCATGCATGC- 3'
Complementary (3' → 5'): 3' -TACGTACGTACGTACGTACGTACGTACG- 5'

Step 2: Invert the complementary strand into the requested 5' → 3' direction:

5' -GCATGCATGCATGCATGCATGCATGCAT- 3'

Q4
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
If the sequence of the coding strand in a transcription unit is written as follows:
5' -ATGCATGCATGCATGCATGCATGCATGC-3'
Write down the sequence of mRNA.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Transcription Polarity and mRNA Rule:

  • In a transcription unit, the template strand (3' → 5') is transcribed into mRNA.
  • The coding strand (5' → 3') is not transcribed, but by scientific convention, the sequence of mRNA is identical to the coding strand, with only one chemical difference: Thymine (T) in DNA is replaced by Uracil (U) in RNA.
  • The polarity of both the coding strand and the resulting mRNA is 5' → 3'.

Deduction:

Coding strand (DNA):     5' -ATGCATGCATGCATGCATGCATGCATGC- 3'
Template strand (DNA):   3' -TACGTACGTACGTACGTACGTACGTACG- 5'
mRNA transcribed:       5' -AUGCAUGCAUGCAUGCAUGCAUGCAUGC- 3'
Sequence of mRNA: 5' -AUGCAUGCAUGCAUGCAUGCAUGCAUGC- 3'

Q5
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Which property of DNA double helix led Watson and Crick to hypothesise semi-conservative mode of DNA replication? Explain.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

The Core Property:

The property of complementary base pairing between the two antiparallel polynucleotide strands led James Watson and Francis Crick (1953) to immediately propose the semi-conservative mode of replication.

Explanation:

  1. Watson and Crick famously noted in their 1953 Nature paper: "It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material."
  2. Due to strict complementary base pairing (Adenine specifically pairing with Thymine via 2 H-bonds, and Guanine specifically pairing with Cytosine via 3 H-bonds), the sequence of bases in one strand automatically and precisely dictates the sequence of bases in the opposite strand.
  3. During replication:
    • The hydrogen bonds holding the two parental strands together uncoil and separate.
    • Each separated parental strand acts as a template for the synthesis of a new complementary strand.
    • Substrate deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates (dNTPs) align opposite the template according to base pairing rules and are joined by DNA polymerase.
  4. Upon completion of replication, each daughter DNA duplex contains one conserved parental strand and one newly synthesised strand. Hence, the replication process is termed semi-conservative (experimentally verified later by Meselson and Stahl in 1958).

Q7
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
How did Hershey and Chase differentiate between DNA and protein in their experiment while proving that DNA is the genetic material?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase (1952) worked with bacteriophage T2 that infects Escherichia coli to provide unequivocal proof that DNA is the genetic material.

Chemical Differentiation Strategy Using Radioisotopes:

  • Radioactive Phosphorus (32P) for DNA: DNA contains phosphorus as an integral constituent of its sugar-phosphate backbone, but contains no sulphur. Bacteriophages grown in a medium containing radioactive 32P incorporated radioactivity exclusively into their DNA.
  • Radioactive Sulphur (35S) for Protein: Proteins contain sulphur in amino acids (cysteine and methionine), but contain no phosphorus. Bacteriophages grown in a medium containing radioactive 35S incorporated radioactivity exclusively into their protein capsid coats.

Experimental Protocol & Results:

  1. Infection: Both batches of labeled phages were allowed to attach to separate cultures of E. coli.
  2. Blending: The bacterial cultures were agitated in a kitchen blender to dislodge the empty viral coats (ghosts) from the surface of the bacterial cells.
  3. Centrifugation: The viral ghosts (lighter supernatant) were separated from the heavier bacterial cells (pellet).
  4. Observation:
    • Bacteria infected with 32P-labeled phages showed radioactivity in the cell pellet, and subsequent progeny phages were radioactive.
    • Bacteria infected with 35S-labeled phages showed radioactivity in the supernatant; no radioactivity was detected in the bacterial cell pellet.

Conclusion: This decisively proved that DNA (and not protein) enters the bacteria from the virus, confirming that DNA is the hereditary genetic material.

CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Radioactive labelling strategy: 32P labels DNA (contains phosphorus, no sulfur); 35S labels protein coat (contains sulfur, no phosphorus)1.5 Marks
Three-step methodology: Infection, Blending, and Centrifugation0.5 Mark
Experimental result: 32P in pellet (inside bacteria) and 35S in supernatant proving DNA is the genetic material1 Mark
Q8
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Differentiate between the followings:
(a) Repetitive DNA and Satellite DNA
(b) mRNA and tRNA
(c) Template strand and Coding strand
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution
(a) Repetitive DNA vs Satellite DNA
Repetitive DNA Satellite DNA
Broad category of DNA sequences that are repeated many times (from hundreds to millions of times) in the genome. A specific sub-fraction of repetitive DNA that separates as distinct minor peaks during CsCl density gradient centrifugation.
Can be interspersed or tandem; includes transposons, retrotransposons, and satellite sequences. Highly tandemly repeated non-coding sequences (micro-satellites, mini-satellites/VNTRs) showing high polymorphism.
(b) mRNA vs tRNA
mRNA (Messenger RNA) tRNA (Transfer / Adapter RNA)
Linear, single-stranded molecule without secondary base-paired loops; accounts for ~5% of cellular RNA. Clover-leaf shaped 2D (inverted L-shaped 3D) molecule with folded hydrogen-bonded arms; accounts for ~15% of cellular RNA.
Carries genetic information in triplet codons from DNA to ribosomes for polypeptide synthesis. Acts as an adapter molecule: reads mRNA codons via its anticodon loop and carries the cognate amino acid at its 3'-CCA end.
(c) Template Strand vs Coding Strand
Template Strand (Antisense Strand) Coding Strand (Sense Strand)
Has 3' → 5' polarity. Has 5' → 3' polarity.
Actively transcribed by RNA polymerase; base sequence is complementary to the synthesised mRNA. Not transcribed into mRNA; its sequence is identical to the synthesised mRNA (with T instead of U).

Q9
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
List two essential roles of ribosome during translation.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

During protein synthesis (translation), the ribosome acts as a complex cellular macromolecular machine performing the following two essential roles:

  1. Structural Platform & Positioning of mRNA and Aminoacyl-tRNAs:
    • The smaller ribosomal subunit binds to the mRNA transcript, ensuring proper reading-frame alignment at the initiation codon (AUG).
    • The larger ribosomal subunit contains two functional binding sites (A site for aminoacyl-tRNA and P site for peptidyl-tRNA), positioning adjacent aminoacyl-tRNAs close enough for peptide bond formation.
  2. Enzymatic Catalysis (Peptidyl Transferase Activity):
    • The ribosome acts as a ribozyme. In prokaryotes, the 23S rRNA of the larger ribosomal subunit catalyses the formation of peptide bonds between the amino group of the incoming amino acid at the A site and the carboxyl group of the growing peptidyl chain at the P site.
CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Session 2026–27)
Providing structural framework/binding sites (A, P, E sites) and reading mRNA codons1 Mark
Acting as a catalyst/ribozyme (23S rRNA peptidyl transferase) for peptide bond synthesis1 Mark
Q10
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
In the medium where E. coli was growing, lactose was added, which induced the lac operon. Then, why does lac operon shut down some time after addition of lactose in the medium?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

The shut-down of the lac operon occurs through an intrinsic biochemical feedback loop:

  1. Induction Phase: When lactose (allolactose) is added to the growth medium, it binds to the active repressor protein synthesised by the regulatory i gene. This alters the repressor's allosteric conformation, preventing it from binding to the operator region.
  2. Transcription of Structural Genes: RNA polymerase accesses the promoter and transcribes the polycistronic mRNA, translating into:
    • β-galactosidase (z gene): Hydrolyses lactose into glucose and galactose.
    • Permease (y gene): Increases cell membrane permeability to β-galactosides.
    • Transacetylase (a gene): Transfers an acetyl group to β-galactosides.
  3. Metabolic Depletion of Inducer: As β-galactosidase hydrolyses lactose, the intracellular concentration of lactose/allolactose declines steadily until all of it is metabolised into glucose and galactose.
  4. Repressor Reactivation and Shut-Down: In the absence of lactose/inducer, the newly synthesized repressor protein remains free and in its active conformation. It binds tightly once again to the operator gene (o), physically blocking RNA polymerase from transcribing the structural genes. Consequently, the lac operon is switched OFF.

Action of beta-galactosidase hydrolysing lactose into glucose and galactose1 Mark

Q11
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Explain (in one or two lines) the function of the followings:
(a) Promoter
(b) tRNA
(c) Exons
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution
(a) Promoter:
A DNA sequence located towards the 5'-end (upstream) of the structural gene that provides the specific binding site for RNA polymerase and determines which DNA strand serves as the template for transcription.
(b) tRNA (Transfer RNA):
Acts as an adapter molecule that reads the genetic code on mRNA through its complementary anticodon triplet and transfers the corresponding cognate amino acid to the growing polypeptide chain during translation.
(c) Exons:
The coding/expressed coding nucleotide sequences in eukaryotic split genes that appear in mature, spliced functional RNA and are translated into the final polypeptide sequence.

Q12
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Why is the Human Genome Project called a mega project?
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

The Human Genome Project (HGP), initiated in 1990 and completed in 2003, is termed a "Mega Project" because of its unprecedented scale, budget, data volume, and international logistical collaboration:

  1. Enormous Scale and Target Size: The human haploid genome comprises approximately 3 × 109 base pairs (3 billion bp). Sequencing every individual nucleotide across all 24 chromosomes (22 autosomes + X + Y) was a monumental biological undertaking.
  2. Colossal Financial Cost: The projected cost of sequencing was roughly US $3 per base pair, making the total estimated budget of the project nearly US $9 billion.
  3. Immense Data Volume & Storage Requirements: If the sequence was printed in books containing 1,000 pages each, with 1,000 letters per page, sequencing one human cell would require 3,300 books! This necessitated the development of novel computer algorithms, high-speed computational databases, and bioinformatics tools for storage, retrieval, and analysis.
  4. Massive Global Collaboration: Coordinated by the US Department of Energy (DOE) and the National Institutes of Health (NIH), with key participation from the Wellcome Trust (UK), Japan, France, Germany, China, and thousands of international scientists over a 13-year timeline.
  5. Associated Ethical, Legal, and Social Issues (ELSI): Required dedicated funding to address the societal, legal, and privacy implications of human genomic data.

Q13
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
What is DNA fingerprinting? Mention its application.
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution

Definition:

DNA fingerprinting (DNA profiling), developed by Sir Alec Jeffreys in 1984, is a molecular diagnostic technique used to identify individuals based on unique variations and patterns in their DNA sequence, specifically targeting Variable Number of Tandem Repeats (VNTRs) in satellite DNA.

Underlying Scientific Principle:

  • 99.9% of DNA sequence is identical among all human beings. Individual uniqueness resides in 0.1% polymorphic non-coding DNA.
  • VNTRs (minisatellites) exhibit a very high degree of inheritable DNA polymorphism. The size and repeat number of VNTRs vary from person to person (ranging from 0.1 to 20 kb), yielding a unique banding pattern upon Southern blot hybridisation with a radio-labelled VNTR probe (except in monozygotic identical twins).

Major Practical Applications:

  1. Forensic Criminology: Identifying suspects or victims in criminal investigations (homicides, rapes, burglaries) by matching DNA extracted from trace biological evidence (blood drops, semen stains, hair roots, saliva).
  2. Paternity and Maternity Dispute Resolution: Solving disputed lineage or custody cases, as an individual's VNTR bands are strictly inherited from biological parents (50% from mother, 50% from father).
  3. Immigration Verification: Scientifically verifying biological kinship claims for immigration authorities.
  4. Pedigree and Wildlife Conservation: Monitoring genetic diversity and parentage in captive breeding programmes of endangered species.
  5. Evolutionary and Anthropological Studies: Tracing prehistoric human migrations and population genetics.

Q14
Exercises (NCERT Verbatim)
Briefly describe the following:
(a) Transcription
(b) Polymorphism
(c) Translation
(d) Bioinformatics
[4 Marks]
✅ CBSE Step-by-Step Biological Solution
(a) Transcription

The enzymatic process of copying genetic information from the template strand of DNA into a complementary single-stranded RNA molecule (mRNA), catalyzed by DNA-dependent RNA polymerase. It proceeds in three distinct phases: initiation (at promoter via σ-factor), elongation, and termination (via ρ-factor). In eukaryotes, primary transcripts (hnRNA) undergo post-transcriptional processing: 5'-capping, 3'-polyadenylation (tailing), and splicing to remove introns.

(b) Polymorphism (DNA Polymorphism)

Heritable genetic variation occurring at a frequency greater than 0.01 (1%) in a human population. It arises due to mutations that accumulate over generations in non-coding DNA sequences. DNA polymorphism ranges from single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) to variable numbers of tandem repeats (VNTRs), and forms the scientific foundation for genetic linkage mapping and DNA fingerprinting.

(c) Translation

The cellular process wherein the sequence of nucleotide triplets (codons) in mRNA is decoded to synthesize a specific sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain. It requires ribosomes, aminoacyl-tRNAs, GTP, and initiation/elongation factors, proceeding through amino acid activation, charging of tRNA, initiation at AUG codon, peptide chain elongation (peptidyl transferase), and termination at stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA).

(d) Bioinformatics

An interdisciplinary branch of science that combines computer science, mathematics, statistics, and molecular biology to acquire, store, organise, analyse, and model biological data, especially massive macromolecular datasets generated by high-throughput DNA sequencing (e.g., Human Genome Project).
