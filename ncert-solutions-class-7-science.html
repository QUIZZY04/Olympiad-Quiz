Chapter 1: Nutrition in Plants

NCERT Exercise Solutions — Complete Step-by-Step Answers with CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

📌 Key Scientific Concepts & Chemical Equations
  • Modes of Nutrition:
    • Autotrophic Nutrition: Organisms synthesise their own food from simple inorganic raw materials (CO₂, H₂O, minerals) using sunlight and chlorophyll. E.g., all green plants and blue-green algae.
    • Heterotrophic Nutrition: Organisms depend directly or indirectly on autotrophs for food. E.g., animals, non-green plants, fungi.
  • Photosynthesis: The biochemical process by which green leaves prepare food using chlorophyll, sunlight, carbon dioxide, and water:
    Carbon dioxide (6CO₂) + Water (6H₂O) ⎯⎯⎯[Sunlight / Chlorophyll]⎯⎯→ Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) + Oxygen (6O₂)
  • Specialised Modes of Nutrition in Plants:
    • Parasitic Plants: Non-green plants that obtain readymade food from a host plant using specialised absorbing roots (haustoria). E.g., Cuscuta (Amarbel).
    • Insectivorous Plants: Partially autotrophic plants growing in nitrogen-deficient bogs that trap and digest insects to obtain nitrogenous compounds. E.g., Pitcher plant (Nepenthes), Venus flytrap.
    • Saprotrophic Nutrition: Organisms secrete digestive juices onto dead and decaying matter and absorb dissolved nutrients. E.g., Fungi, Mushrooms, bread mould (Rhizopus).
    • Symbiosis: Mutually beneficial association between two organisms sharing shelter and nutrients. E.g., Lichens (Alga + Fungus), Rhizobium bacteria in root nodules of leguminous plants.
  • Stomata: Tiny microscopic pores present predominantly on the underside of leaves, flanked by kidney-shaped guard cells, responsible for gaseous exchange (CO₂ and O₂) and transpiration.

Why do all living organisms require food?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

All living organisms require food for the following fundamental life processes:

1. Energy Source: Food serves as fuel; through cellular respiration, nutrients are broken down to release ATP energy needed for locomotion, digestion, circulation, and maintenance.

3. Repair of Tissues: Worn-out, injured, or damaged cells are repaired and replaced using nutrients absorbed from food.
4. Immune Resistance: Vitamins and essential minerals protect the body against pathogens and infectious diseases.

Page 2 — Intext Q2 (Paheli's Query)
Paheli wants to know why our body cannot make food from carbon dioxide, water and minerals like plants do.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Scientific Explanation:
1. Green plants possess a unique green pigment called chlorophyll enclosed inside specialized cellular organelles called chloroplasts.
2. Chlorophyll has the unique biochemical capability of trapping and absorbing solar radiation (photons) from sunlight to drive the endothermic synthesis of glucose from CO₂ and H₂O.
3. Human and animal cells completely lack chlorophyll and chloroplasts. Therefore, even though carbon dioxide, water, and sunlight are abundantly available in our environment, our cells cannot trap solar energy to synthesize organic food molecules. Consequently, humans must rely on heterotrophic nutrition.
CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27
Presence of chlorophyll/chloroplasts in plants to trap solar energy1 Mark

Page 2 — Intext Q3 (Boojho's Query)
Boojho wants to know how water and minerals absorbed by roots reach the leaves of a plant.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Mechanism of Water & Mineral Transport:
1. Water and dissolved soil minerals are absorbed by microscopic root hairs through osmosis and active transport.
2. Plants possess specialized vascular plumbing tissues called xylem.
3. Xylem vessels form a continuous, unbroken pipeline of microscopic hollow tubes running from the root tips, up through the stem, into the branches, and throughout the petiole and veins of every leaf.
4. Evaporation of water from leaf stomata creates a powerful upward physical pull called transpirational pull (suction pull), drawing water and dissolved minerals upwards against gravity right into the food-making cells (mesophyll) of the leaves.

Page 3 — Intext Q4 (Paheli's Query)
Paheli wants to know what is so special about the leaves that they can synthesise food but other parts of the plant cannot.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Structural Adaptations of Leaves:
1. Concentration of Chlorophyll: Leaves possess millions of specialized photosynthetic mesophyll cells containing abundant green pigment chlorophyll, which captures solar radiation.
2. Presence of Stomata: The leaf epidermis features thousands of microscopic pores called stomata flanked by guard cells, allowing atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO₂) to enter the interior leaf tissues efficiently.
3. Broad Flat Surface Area: Leaves are thin, broad, and laminar, maximizing light interception and minimizing internal diffusion distance.
Note: Non-green parts like roots or thick woody stems lack chlorophyll and cannot capture solar energy. (However, in desert plants like cactus, modified green fleshy stems take over photosynthesis).

Page 3 — Intext Q5 (Boojho's Query)
Boojho has seen some plants with deep red, violet or brown leaves. He wants to know whether these leaves also carry out photosynthesis.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Answer: Yes, leaves with deep red, violet, or brown colors DO carry out photosynthesis.

Scientific Justification:
1. Chlorophyll is present in these colorful leaves in normal quantities.
2. However, these leaves also produce large quantities of other non-green pigments (such as anthocyanins, carotenoids, and xanthophylls) which impart deep red, purple, or dark brown hues.
3. These intense dark pigments physically mask (hide) the green color of chlorophyll from human eyes.
4. The underlying chlorophyll continues to trap solar energy, and photosynthesis proceeds normally. This can be verified experimentally by boiling the leaf in alcohol to extract the masking pigments and performing the iodine starch test, which turns blue-black.

Page 5 — Intext Q6 (Paheli's Query)
Paheli wants to know whether mosquitoes, bed bugs, lice and leeches that suck our blood are also parasites.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Answer: Yes, bed bugs, lice, and leeches are ectoparasites, and female mosquitoes behave as temporary parasites.

Biological Classification:
1. Parasite Definition: An organism that lives on or inside another living organism (the host) and derives nutrition directly from the host, usually causing harm or discomfort.
2. Head Lice & Bed Bugs: Live directly on the host's body/habitat, feeding on blood meals for survival and reproduction (obligate ectoparasites).
3. Leeches: Attach to the host's skin using suckers, secrete hirudin (anticoagulant), and feed on blood.
4. Female Mosquitoes: Siphon blood protein from warm-blooded hosts to nourish developing eggs (often classified as temporary micropredators or hematophagous parasites).

Page 6 — Intext Q7 (Boojho's Query)
Boojho wants to know how fungi appear suddenly during the rainy season on bread, leather shoes, and rotting wood.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Scientific Explanation:
1. Microscopic Spores in Air: Fungal spores are extremely light and are naturally floating suspended in the atmosphere around us at all times in a dormant state.
2. Protective Spore Coat: Each spore is enclosed in a tough, hard protective coat that allows it to survive long dry spells and unfavorable temperatures.
3. Monsoon Warmth & Moisture: During the rainy season, the ambient air becomes highly humid and warm. When these microscopic airborne spores land on wet, organic substrates (moist bread, damp leather, decaying wood), the moisture triggers instant germination.
4. Rapid Mycelial Growth: The spores sprout hyphae, secrete digestive enzymes onto the substrate, and rapidly multiply into visible cottony fungal colonies within 48 to 72 hours.

Q3
How would you test the presence of starch in leaves?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

The presence of starch in green leaves is tested using the Iodine Test following a standard laboratory procedure:

Step 1 (Killing the cells): Pluck a green leaf from a healthy plant exposed to sunlight for a few hours. Boil the leaf in a beaker of water for 3 to 5 minutes to break down cell membranes and make it permeable.
Step 2 (Decolourisation): Place the boiled leaf in a test tube containing alcohol (ethanol). Place this test tube inside a water bath (beaker filled with boiling water) and heat gently until all the green pigment (chlorophyll) dissolves out into the alcohol and the leaf turns pale white/colourless.
Caution: Alcohol is highly flammable; never heat it directly over a flame!
Step 3 (Washing & Softening): Remove the brittle bleached leaf and rinse it gently in warm water to soften it and remove trace alcohol.
Step 4 (Iodine Addition): Spread the decolourised leaf flat on a white ceramic tile and add a few drops of dilute iodine solution onto its surface.
Observation & Conclusion: The leaf turns blue-black in colour. This confirms the presence of starch, proving that photosynthesis has taken place.

Q4
Give a brief description of the process of synthesis of food in green plants.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Green plants synthesise their food through the physiological process of Photosynthesis. Leaves act as the "food factories" of the plant.

1. Absorption of Water & Minerals: Roots absorb water and dissolved minerals from the soil. These are transported upwards through specialised vascular tissues called xylem vessels to the leaves.
2. Intake of Carbon Dioxide: Carbon dioxide from atmospheric air enters the leaves through microscopic pores called stomata, controlled by guard cells.
3. Trapping Solar Energy: Green leaves contain a special pigment called chlorophyll, which captures radiant solar energy.
4. Chemical Conversion: Solar energy is converted into chemical energy to combine CO₂ and H₂O into carbohydrate (glucose). Oxygen is released as a vital byproduct into the air:
6CO₂ + 6H₂O ⎯⎯⎯[Sunlight / Chlorophyll]⎯⎯→ C₆H₁₂O₆ (Glucose) + 6O₂ (Oxygen)

The synthesised glucose is converted and stored in plant organs as starch.

Q13
Why do farmers grow many fruits and vegetable crops inside large green houses? What are the advantages to the farmers?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

A greenhouse (glasshouse or polyhouse) provides a controlled microenvironment for plant cultivation. Farmers grow crops inside greenhouses due to the following specific advantages:

1. Optimal Temperature & Light Control: Glass walls trap solar heat (greenhouse effect), maintaining a warmer, optimum temperature for photosynthesis even during cold winter seasons.
2. Protection from Harsh Weather & Pests: Enclosed structures shield delicate crops from high winds, hailstorms, heavy rainfall, frost, birds, insects, and air-borne pests.
3. Controlled Carbon Dioxide & Humidity: Farmers can enrich the internal air with higher concentrations of CO₂ and maintain ideal humidity, leading to faster growth rates, higher yields, and better-quality produce.
4. Year-Round Farming: It enables farmers to grow off-season vegetables and fruits, fetching premium market prices.

Case Study: Nitrogen Fixation in Agriculture
Scenario: Farmer Ramesh noticed that after harvesting wheat, the soil becomes depleted of nitrogen. However, his agricultural advisor recommended rotating the crop by planting gram (chickpea) or peas before sowing wheat again, without applying synthetic nitrogen fertilisers.

(i) Name the bacterium that lives in the roots of gram and pea plants.
(ii) What type of relationship exists between this bacterium and the leguminous plant?
(iii) Why is crop rotation with legumes economically and ecologically beneficial?

(i) Bacterium Name: Rhizobium.

(ii) Biological Relationship: Symbiotic relationship (Symbiosis). The bacterium cannot synthesise its own food; it receives shelter, carbohydrates, and organic nutrients from the host plant roots. In exchange, Rhizobium converts atmospheric gaseous nitrogen (N₂) into water-soluble nitrogenous compounds (nitrates) that the plant easily absorbs.

(iii) Benefits: (a) Economic: Saves farmers significant money on expensive chemical fertilisers. (b) Ecological: Prevents soil degradation, preserves soil texture, and stops chemical runoff from polluting nearby water bodies (eutrophication).

Assertion (A): Pitcher plant performs photosynthesis but still traps and digests insects.
Reason (R): Pitcher plants grow in nitrogen-deficient boggy soils and feed on insects to fulfil their nitrogen requirement.

Choose the correct option:
(a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
(b) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
(c) A is true, but R is false.
(d) A is false, but R is true.

Answer: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Scientific Explanation: Pitcher plants possess green chlorophyll and synthesise their own carbohydrates through photosynthesis. However, the waterlogged, marshy soils they inhabit are severely deficient in available nitrogen minerals. To synthesise essential proteins, the leaf lamina modifies into a pitcher trap with digestive enzymes to digest trapped insects and absorb amino acids.

Chapter 2: Nutrition in Animals

NCERT Exercise Solutions — Complete Step-by-Step Answers with CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

📌 Key Scientific Concepts & Digestive System Summary
  • Five Major Steps of Holozoic Nutrition:
    1. Ingestion: Taking food into the body via the mouth (buccal cavity).
    2. Digestion: Breakdown of complex, insoluble food molecules into simple, soluble forms by digestive enzymes.
    3. Absorption: Movement of digested nutrients through intestinal walls (villi) into the bloodstream.
    4. Assimilation: Utilization of absorbed nutrients by body cells for energy, growth, and tissue repair.
    5. Egestion: Removal of undigested, unabsorbed waste residue from the body through the anus.
  • Human Digestive Canal & Associated Glands:
    • Mouth & Teeth: Incisors (cutting/biting), Canines (tearing), Premolars & Molars (chewing/grinding). Salivary amylase breaks starch into maltose.
    • Stomach: Secretes mucous (protects inner wall), hydrochloric acid (kills bacteria, creates acidic medium pH ~1.5–2), and pepsin (digests proteins into peptides).
    • Liver: Largest gland in human body; secretes bile stored in gall bladder; emulsifies fats (breaks large fat globules into tiny droplets).
    • Pancreas: Secretes pancreatic juice containing amylase, trypsin, and lipase.
    • Small Intestine: Longest part (~7.5 m); site of complete digestion and nutrient absorption through finger-like villi.
    • Large Intestine: ~1.5 m; absorbs water and essential salts from undigested residue.
  • Nutrition in Ruminants: Four-chambered stomach (Rumen, Reticulum, Omasum, Abomasum). Swallowed food is stored in rumen as cud, brought back to mouth and chewed (rumination). Symbiotic bacteria in caecum digest complex cellulose.
  • Nutrition in Amoeba: Unicellular organism that pushes out finger-like projections called pseudopodia (false feet) to engulf food into a food vacuole where digestive enzymes break it down.

Page 15 — Intext Q2 (Paheli's Query)
Paheli wants to know how food moves in the opposite direction during vomiting.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Mechanism of Vomiting (Anti-peristalsis):
1. Normally, food moves downward through the alimentary canal by rhythmic wave-like contractions of the muscular gut wall called peristalsis.
2. When the stomach detects toxic, contaminated, or irritating food, or when it is excessively overloaded, nerve signals trigger the vomiting reflex in the brainstem.
3. The normal downward peristalsis reverses into anti-peristalsis (retro-peristalsis).
4. The stomach and abdominal muscles violently contract simultaneously, the cardiac sphincter at the esophageal entrance relaxes, and the stomach contents are forcefully propelled backwards up the esophagus and expelled through the mouth.

Page 17 — Intext Q3 (Boojho's Query)
Boojho wants to know why ruminants (like cows and buffaloes) cannot chew their food properly when they take it in, but chew cud later while resting.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Evolutionary Adaptation of Ruminants:
1. Predator Avoidance: In the wild, grazing herbivores are vulnerable to carnivores in open pastures. To minimize time exposed, they swallow huge quantities of grass as quickly as possible with minimal chewing.
2. Storage in Rumen: The swallowed grass is stored in the rumen (first stomach chamber), where symbiotic anaerobic bacteria and protozoa begin breaking down tough cellulose, forming soft partially digested food called cud.
3. Rumination in Safety: Once the animal retreats to a sheltered, safe spot to rest, small lumps of cud are regurgitated back into the mouth in batches and thoroughly chewed (chewing the cud / rumination). This fine mastication enables complete microbial fermentation later in the digestive tract.

Page 18 — Intext Q4 (Boojho's Query)
Boojho wants to know why humans cannot digest cellulose like cattle and horses do.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Why Humans Cannot Digest Cellulose:
1. Lack of Cellulase Enzyme: Cellulose is a tough, complex structural carbohydrate with strong β-glycosidic linkages. Humans do not produce the enzyme cellulase required to cleave these bonds.
2. Absence of Cellulolytic Symbionts: Herbivorous ruminants have a rumen and animals like horses and rabbits have an enlarged caecum hosting millions of specialized symbiotic anaerobic bacteria and protozoa that ferment and digest cellulose.
3. Vestigial Human Caecum: In humans, the caecum is reduced, and the appendix is a tiny vestigial organ with no cellulose-digesting bacteria. Hence, cellulose passes through the human gut undigested as dietary fiber (roughage).

📌 Key Scientific Concepts & Thermodynamics Summary
  • Heat vs Temperature:
    • Heat: A form of energy that flows spontaneously from a body at a higher temperature to a body at a lower temperature until thermal equilibrium is reached. Unit: Joule (J) or Calorie (cal).
    • Temperature: A reliable quantitative measure of the degree of hotness or coldness of an object. Measured using thermometers in Degree Celsius (°C), Fahrenheit (°F), or Kelvin (K).
  • Thermometers:
    • Clinical Thermometer: Designed specifically for human body temperature. Range: 35°C to 42°C (or 94°F to 108°F). Contains a narrow constriction (kink) just above the bulb to prevent mercury from falling back automatically before the reading is recorded.
    • Laboratory Thermometer: Used to measure temperature of chemical solutions and substances in labs. Range: -10°C to 110°C. Has no kink; reading must be taken while still in contact with the substance.
  • Three Modes of Heat Transfer:
    1. Conduction: Heat transfer in solids from the hotter end to the colder end through molecular vibrations without the actual movement of particles. Metals are good conductors; wood, plastic, glass, and air are insulators.
    2. Convection: Heat transfer in fluids (liquids and gases) where warmer, less dense fluid expands, rises, and cooler denser fluid sinks to replace it, setting up convection currents. Drives sea breeze (day) and land breeze (night) in coastal areas.
    3. Radiation: Heat transfer through electromagnetic infrared waves without requiring any physical medium. Can travel through vacuum (how the Sun's heat warms Earth). Dark, dull surfaces absorb and radiate heat best; light, polished surfaces reflect heat.

    • Litmus (from Lichens): Mauve/purple in neutral; Red in acid; Blue in base.
    • Turmeric: Yellow in acid/neutral; turns Reddish-brown in base.
    • China Rose Petal Solution: Turns dark pink (magenta) in acid; green in base.
    • Phenolphthalein: Colourless in acid; turns pink in base.
  • Neutralisation Reaction: The reaction between an acid and a base producing a salt and water with the evolution of heat:
    Acid + Base ⎯⎯→ Salt + Water + Heat
    HCl (Hydrochloric acid) + NaOH (Sodium hydroxide) ⎯⎯→ NaCl (Sodium chloride) + H₂O + Heat

📌 Key Scientific Concepts & Chemical Reactions
  • Physical Changes: Changes in which only the physical properties (shape, size, state, appearance) of a substance undergo transformation, with no new chemical substance formed. Generally temporary and easily reversible. E.g., melting of ice, boiling of water, tearing of paper, dissolution of sugar in water.
  • Chemical Changes (Chemical Reactions): Changes in which one or more entirely new substances with distinct chemical compositions and properties are formed. Irreversible and accompanied by energy changes (heat, light, sound), evolution of gas, change in colour, or precipitate formation. E.g., burning of fuels, rusting of iron, curdling of milk, digestion of food.
  • Classic Laboratory Chemical Reactions:
    • Burning of Magnesium Ribbon: Burns with a dazzling white flame:
      2Mg (Magnesium) + O₂ (Oxygen) ⎯⎯→ 2MgO (Magnesium oxide - basic ash)
      MgO + H₂O ⎯⎯→ Mg(OH)₂ (Magnesium hydroxide — turns red litmus blue)
    • Copper Sulphate and Iron Nail Reaction (Displacement):
      CuSO₄ (Blue solution) + Fe (Iron nail) ⎯⎯→ FeSO₄ (Light green solution) + Cu (Brown deposit on nail)
    • Baking Soda and Vinegar Reaction:
      CH₃COOH (Vinegar / Acetic acid) + NaHCO₃ (Baking soda) ⎯⎯→ CO₂ (Carbon dioxide) + H₂O + Sodium acetate
      CO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ (Lime water) ⎯⎯→ CaCO₃ (Insoluble Calcium carbonate, milky) + H₂O
  • Rusting of Iron: Corrosion of iron in the simultaneous presence of both oxygen (air) and moisture (water):
    4Fe + 3O₂ + 2xH₂O ⎯⎯→ 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O (Hydrated iron(III) oxide / Rust)
    Prevention: Barrier protection (painting, greasing, oiling), alloying (stainless steel), and galvanisation (coating iron with a sacrificial protective layer of molten zinc metal).
  • Crystallisation: A physical purification technique in which pure, well-defined solid crystals of a substance are separated from its hot, saturated solution upon cooling.

📌 Key Scientific Concepts & Respiratory Mechanisms
  • Cellular Respiration: The intracellular biochemical breakdown of glucose in the presence or absence of oxygen to release usable biological energy (stored as ATP):
    • Aerobic Respiration: Occurs in the presence of oxygen; completely oxidises glucose into carbon dioxide, water, and 36–38 ATP molecules:
      Glucose + Oxygen ⎯⎯→ Carbon dioxide (6CO₂) + Water (6H₂O) + Energy (38 ATP)
    • Anaerobic Respiration in Yeast (Fermentation): In the absence of oxygen, glucose is partially broken down into ethyl alcohol, carbon dioxide, and 2 ATP molecules:
      Glucose ⎯⎯[In absence of O₂]⎯⎯→ Ethyl alcohol (2C₂H₅OH) + Carbon dioxide (2CO₂) + Energy (2 ATP)
    • Anaerobic Respiration in Human Skeletal Muscles: Occurs during intense exercise when oxygen supply falls short of muscular demand; produces lactic acid:
      Glucose ⎯⎯[In absence of O₂]⎯⎯→ Lactic acid (2C₃H₆O₃) + Energy (2 ATP)
      Accumulation of lactic acid causes painful muscle cramps. Relief is obtained through a hot bath or massage, which improves blood circulation and oxygen supply to oxidise lactic acid into CO₂ and H₂O.
  • Breathing Mechanism in Humans:
    • Inhalation (Inspiration): Diaphragm contracts and flattens downwards; ribcage moves upwards and outwards. Chest cavity volume increases, lowering air pressure in lungs. Atmospheric air rushes in through nostrils → pharynx → trachea → bronchi → lungs.
    • Exhalation (Expiration): Diaphragm relaxes and curves upwards into dome shape; ribcage moves downwards and inwards. Chest cavity volume decreases, increasing lung air pressure, forcing air out.
  • Respiration in Diverse Organisms:
    • Insects (Cockroach): Network of air tubes called tracheae opening through small exterior holes called spiracles.
    • Earthworm: Breathes through moist, slimy, vascular skin (cutaneous respiration).
    • Fish: Breathes dissolved oxygen in water through comb-like, highly vascularised gills.
    • Frogs: Dual breathing: through moist skin in water and through a pair of lungs on land.
    • Plants: Stomata on leaves and lenticels on bark for gaseous exchange; root hairs absorb oxygen from air spaces between soil particles.

📌 Key Scientific Concepts & Circulatory / Vascular Systems
  • Human Circulatory System:
    • Blood Composition: Liquid matrix called plasma containing Red Blood Cells (RBCs) with iron-rich haemoglobin for oxygen transport; White Blood Cells (WBCs) that destroy invading pathogens; and Platelets essential for blood clotting to prevent haemorrhage.
    • Blood Vessels: Arteries carry oxygenated blood away from the heart at high pressure (thick, elastic walls, no valves; exception: pulmonary artery); Veins carry deoxygenated blood towards the heart at low pressure (thin walls, possess internal pocket valves preventing backflow; exception: pulmonary vein); Capillaries are microscopic single-cell-thick vessels facilitating nutrient and gas exchange.

  • Human Excretory System: Consists of a pair of bean-shaped Kidneys (filter blood), tube-like Ureters, a muscular Urinary Bladder (stores urine), and a terminal Urethra with an opening. Normal human urine composition: ~95% water, 2.5% urea, 2.5% other toxic metabolic salts. In kidney failure, blood is cleansed periodically using an artificial kidney through dialysis.
  • Vascular Transport in Plants:
    • Xylem: Continuous tubular network conducting water and dissolved mineral salts unidirectionally from roots upwards to the leaves.
    • Phloem: Living vascular tissue translocating food (sugars synthesized by leaves) bidirectionally to all parts of the plant.
    • Transpiration: Evaporation of water vapour from leaf stomata. Generates a powerful suction pull (transpirational pull) capable of lifting water columns over 100 metres high in giant trees, while cooling the plant.

Clinical Case: 55-year-old Mohan suffered from chronic uncontrolled hypertension, resulting in complete renal failure (both kidneys stopped functioning). (iii) What permanent medical solution exists if long-term dialysis is no longer viable?

(i) Medical Term: Haemodialysis (Artificial Kidney).

(ii) Mechanism: Blood from an artery in the patient's arm is pumped through a dialysis machine containing thousands of semi-permeable cellophane tubes bathed in a temperature-controlled dialysing fluid (which has the exact same ionic composition as normal blood plasma but zero urea). 📌 Key Scientific Concepts & Plant Reproduction Modes
  • Modes of Plant Reproduction:
    • Asexual Reproduction: Multiplication without involving the fusion of gametes or production of seeds. Offspring are genetically identical clones of the single parent. Major types include:
      • Vegetative Propagation: New plants arise from vegetative units like roots (sweet potato, dahlia), stems (runners, rhizomes in ginger/turmeric, tubers with "eyes" in potato, stem cuttings in rose), and leaves (notches of leaves in Bryophyllum).
      • Budding: Formation of a small daughter bulb-like projection (bud) that detaches and matures (e.g., Yeast).
      • Fragmentation: Filamentous organisms break into two or more fragments when mature, each growing into a new individual (e.g., Spirogyra alga).
      • Spore Formation: Production of asexual microscopic spores protected by a tough, thick wall to withstand unfavourable conditions (heat, low humidity) which germinate under moisture (e.g., Bread mould / Rhizopus, ferns, mosses).
    • Sexual Reproduction: Involves fusion of male and female gametes produced by the reproductive organ of angiosperms — the flower.
  • Anatomy of a Flower:
    • Bisexual Flowers: Contain both stamens (male) and pistil (female) — e.g., mustard, rose, petunia.
    • Unisexual Flowers: Possess either male stamens (staminate) or female pistil (pistillate) — e.g., corn, papaya, cucumber.
    • Stamen (Male organ): Comprises a slender filament and a terminal two-lobed anther containing pollen grains that produce male gametes.
    • Pistil / Carpel (Female organ): Comprises the receptive stigma, elongated style, and a basal swollen ovary containing one or more ovules enclosing female gametes (egg cells).
  • Pollination & Fertilisation:
    • Pollination: Transfer of pollen grains from anther to stigma. Can be Self-pollination (same flower or another flower on same plant) or Cross-pollination (flower of another plant of same species via wind, water, insects).
    • Fertilisation: Fusion of male gamete (travelling down a pollen tube) with the female egg cell inside the ovule to produce a diploid zygote. The zygote develops into an embryo; ovule matures into a seed, and the ovary ripens into a fruit.
  • Seed Dispersal Mechanisms: Prevents overcrowding, competition for sunlight, water, and minerals. Dispersed by: Wind (winged seeds of drumstick, maple; hairy seeds of aak, dandelion), Water (spongy, fibrous coats in coconut, lotus), Animals (hooked/spiny seeds of Xanthium, Urena), and Explosion/dehiscence (balsam, castor).

Q7
How does the process of fertilisation take place in flowers?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

Fertilisation is the process in which the male gamete fuses with the female gamete to form a single diploid cell called a zygote. In flowering plants, it occurs in the following sequential stages:

Step 3: Entry into the Ovule: The pollen tube enters the ovary and penetrates an ovule through a microscopic aperture called the micropyle.

  • The zygote divides repeatedly to develop into an embryo.
  • The ovule develops a tough seed coat and matures into a seed.
  • The ovary enlarges, ripens, and transforms into a succulent or dry fruit.
  • Petals, sepals, and stamens wither and fall off.

Seed dispersal is the natural scattering or transport of seeds away from parent plants. It is ecologically vital because it avoids overcrowding, minimizes intense competition for water, sunlight, and soil minerals, and enables species to invade new geographical habitats. The four major mechanisms are:

1. Dispersal by Wind: Seeds are lightweight, small, and equipped with special aerodynamic structures:

📌 Key Scientific Concepts & Kinematic Equations
  • Motion & Speed:
    • Speed: The total distance covered by an object divided by the total time taken to cover that distance.
      Speed = Total Distance Covered / Total Time Taken
    • SI Units: Basic unit of distance is metre (m); basic unit of time is second (s). Hence, the SI basic unit of speed is metre per second (m/s). Larger commercial unit is kilometre per hour (km/h).
      Conversion:
      1 km/h = (1000 m) / (3600 s) = 5/18 m/s
    • Uniform vs Non-uniform Motion: An object moving along a straight line with a constant speed is in uniform motion. If the speed keeps changing with time, the motion is non-uniform.
  • Measurement of Time & Periodic Motion:
    • Clocks and watches rely on regular, repeating periodic events.
    • Simple Pendulum: Consists of a small metallic sphere (bob) suspended from a rigid stand by a light thread.
    • Oscillation: One complete to-and-fro motion of the bob from one extreme position to another and back.
    • Time Period (T): The time taken by the pendulum bob to complete one full oscillation.
      Time Period (T) = Total Time Taken / Number of Oscillations
      Discovery: Galileo Galilei observed that the time period of a simple pendulum depends only on the length of the suspension string, and remains constant regardless of the amplitude of swing.
  • Distance-Time Graphs:
    • A graphical representation plotting time on the horizontal X-axis and distance on the vertical Y-axis.
    • A straight line sloping upwards indicates uniform speed (constant speed).
    • A horizontal line parallel to the time axis indicates an object at rest (speed = 0).
    • A curved line indicates non-uniform speed (accelerating or decelerating motion).
    • The steeper the slope (gradient) of the distance-time graph, the greater is the speed of the object.

📌 Key Scientific Concepts & Circuit Fundamentals
  • Symbols of Circuit Components:
    • Electric Cell: Represented by two parallel lines — one long thin line (+ positive terminal) and one shorter thick line (- negative terminal).
    • Battery: A combination of two or more cells connected such that the positive terminal of one cell connects to the negative terminal of the next.
    • Switch: Shown open (OFF - incomplete circuit) or closed (ON - complete closed circuit).
    • Electric Bulb: A circle enclosing an incandescent filament loop.
  • Heating Effect of Electric Current (Joule Heating):
    • When an electric current flows through a high-resistance wire, electrical energy is converted into heat energy. The wire becomes hot.
    • Amount of heat produced depends on the wire's material, length, thickness, and the magnitude of current.
    • Heating Appliances: Electric iron, room heater, geyser, toaster use coils of high-resistance wire called elements (usually made of Nichrome alloy).
    • Electric Fuse: A safety device made of a special wire with a low melting point. If excessive current surges through the circuit due to overloading or short-circuiting, the fuse wire melts and breaks the circuit, preventing fires and device damage.
    • MCB (Miniature Circuit Breaker): Modern electromagnetic/bimetallic switch that automatically trips OFF when current exceeds safe limits; easily reset without wire replacement.
  • Magnetic Effect of Electric Current (Electromagnetism):
    • Discovered in 1820 by Danish physicist Hans Christian Oersted, who noticed that a magnetic compass needle deflects whenever current passes through a nearby wire.
    • Electromagnet: An insulated wire coil wound tightly around a soft iron core. It behaves like a magnet only when electric current flows through it. Magnetism disappears immediately when current is switched off.
    • Applications: Scrap-metal cranes, electric bells, telephone receivers, MRI machines, maglev trains.
    • Electric Bell: Operates on an electromagnet, an armature with a striker hammer, an adjustment contact screw, and a metallic gong in a self-breaking circuit loop.

📌 Key Scientific Concepts & Optics Fundamentals
  • Rectilinear Propagation & Reflection of Light:
    • Light travels in straight lines (rectilinear propagation). When light falls on a polished, shiny surface, its direction changes — a phenomenon known as reflection of light.
    • Plane Mirror Image Characteristics:
      1. The image is erect (upright) and virtual (cannot be caught on a screen).
      2. The size of the image is identical to the object.
      3. The distance of the image behind the mirror is equal to the distance of the object in front of the mirror.
      4. The image exhibits lateral inversion: the left side of the object appears as the right side of the image, and vice versa.
  • Spherical Mirrors:
    • Concave Mirror (Converging): Inner curved surface is reflecting. Can form real, inverted images (diminished, same size, or magnified) when the object is beyond the focus; forms an erect, enlarged, and virtual image when placed very close (between pole and focus). Used by dentists, in solar furnaces, torch reflectors, and shaving mirrors.
    • Convex Mirror (Diverging): Outer bulging surface is reflecting. Always forms an erect, virtual, and diminished (smaller) image over a very wide field of view. Used as rear-view mirrors in automobiles.
  • Spherical Lenses:
    • Convex Lens (Converging): Thicker at the middle than at edges. Converges incident parallel light rays. Can form real and inverted images, or a virtual, erect, and magnified image when held close to an object (used as a magnifying glass and in spectacles for hypermetropia).
    • Concave Lens (Diverging): Thinner at the middle than at edges. Diverges incident parallel light rays. Always forms an erect, virtual, and diminished image regardless of object distance.
  • Dispersion of Light & Sunlight:
    • White sunlight consists of seven distinct constituent colours: Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, Red (VIBGYOR).
    • A glass prism splits white light into its seven constituent colours (dispersion of light), forming a rainbow spectrum.
    • Newton's Disc: A circular disc painted with the seven rainbow segments rotated rapidly blends into a uniform white appearance, demonstrating that white light is a composite of the seven colours.

Q4
Describe the steps involved in getting clarified water from wastewater.
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer

At a Wastewater Treatment Plant (WWTP), raw sewage is transformed into clarified water through the following sequential physical and biological stages:

Stage 1: Filtration through Bar Screens:
Wastewater first flows through large vertical bar screens. These screens intercept and strain out large floating physical debris such as plastic rags, polythene bags, sticks, cans, sanitary napkins, and pebbles.
Stage 2: Grit and Sand Removal Chamber:
The water then passes through a grit and sand removal tank where the incoming flow velocity is deliberately slowed down. Heavy inorganic particles like coarse sand, gravel, and grit settle to the tank bottom by gravity and are mechanically removed.
Stage 3: Primary Sedimentation (Clarifier Tank):
The water is channeled into a large sedimentation tank sloped downwards towards the centre. The water remains undisturbed for several hours:
  • Heavy solid organic wastes (human faeces) settle at the bottom as sludge and are removed by a bottom mechanical scraper.
  • Light, floatable impurities such as oils and grease rise to the surface and are removed by a surface skimmer.
The remaining liquid separated from sludge and scum is called clarified water.
Stage 4: Aeration & Biological Purification:
Air is continuously pumped into the clarified water inside a massive aeration tank. The abundant oxygen triggers explosive growth of harmless aerobic bacteria, which consume residual dissolved organic impurities, food remnants, and soaps. After aeration, the microbes settle down as activated sludge, leaving clear, purified water ready for chemical disinfection.

  2. Reporting Open Sewer Hazards: If an open sewer, choked manhole, or broken municipal water pipe is spotted in the neighbourhood, immediately alert the local municipal corporation or Gram Panchayat to avert disease outbreaks.
  3. Eradicating Open Defecation: Promote and use sanitary closed toilets; advocate against open defecation and urination in open public spaces.
  4. Community Cleanliness Campaigns: Actively participate in neighbourhood sanitation drives (like the Swachh Bharat Abhiyan), ensuring stagnant rainwater puddles are sprayed or drained to curb mosquito breeding.
  5. Spreading Awareness: Educate family, neighbours, and community workers about proper hand hygiene, water purification, and the health benefits of covered sewage networks.

Case Study: Vermi-Processing Toilets & Low-Cost On-Site Sanitation
Biotechnology in Sanitation: In a remote rural village without underground sewerage infrastructure, an NGO introduced vermi-processing toilets. Human excreta is collected in a pit populated by red earthworms (tiger worms).

(i) What do the earthworms convert human excreta into?
(ii) Why are vermi-processing toilets considered ideal for rural communities with low water availability?
(iii) Name another traditional on-site sewage disposal system commonly used for isolated rural homes.

(i) Bio-conversion: Red worms ingest and thoroughly decompose human faeces, transforming pathogenic excreta into high-grade, pathogen-free vermicompost (vermi-cakes), which serves as nutrient-dense organic fertilizer for crops.

(ii) Advantages:
• Low Water Requirement: Requires minimal flushing water compared to standard cistern toilets.
• Simple & Zero Odour: Operates naturally without noxious odours, complex machinery, or expensive electrical connections.
• Resource Recovery: Directly converts hazardous waste into economic fertilizer right at the site.

(iii) Other On-site Systems: Septic tanks, biogas composting pits, and chemical toilets.

Assertion (A): Cooking oils and solid food wastes should never be flushed down kitchen sinks into domestic drainage pipes.
Reason (R): Fats and oils solidify inside cold plumbing conduits, trapping food scraps and causing stubborn drain blockages, while choking soil filtration pores in open drains.

Choose the correct option:
(a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
(b) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
(c) A is true, but R is false.
(d) A is false, but R is true.

Answer: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Scientific Explanation: Greasy hydrophobic lipids adhere to the walls of drainage pipes and harden as temperature drops, drastically constricting water flow. Discarding oils into solid waste receptacles preserves sewer line integrity.
