Poem 8: A Slumber Did My Spirit Seal

William Wordsworth — Grief, Immortality & Fusion with Nature — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

A slumber did my spirit seal; I had no human fears: She seemed a thing that could not feel The touch of earthly years. No motion has she now, no force; She neither hears nor sees; Rolled round in earth's diurnal course, With rocks, and stones, and trees.
— William Wordsworth
I. Thinking about the Poem — Short Questions (30–40 Words / 2–3 Marks)
Q1
What does 'a slumber did my spirit seal' mean? What had made the poet oblivious to human fears?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

'A slumber did my spirit seal' means that a state of deep, dream-like sleep or illusion had locked the poet's mind. The poet had loved Lucy so deeply that he fell into a complacency, believing she was immune to aging, disease, and the touch of earthly years, making him entirely oblivious to the mortal reality of death.

What does 'earth's diurnal course' mean? How does the poet console his grief?
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

'Diurnal course' refers to the earth's daily, regular 24-hour rotation on its axis. The poet consoles his profound grief through pantheistic acceptance. Although Lucy has lost individual human consciousness, she has attained cosmic immortality by fusing with the eternal rhythm of mother earth. She is now an immortal part of the planetary fabric—revolving perpetually with nature's timeless elements.

II. Analytical & Value-Based Questions (100–120 Words / 6 Marks)
Q4
Analyze the profound contrast between the two stanzas of Wordsworth's eight-line masterpiece. (Max Word Count)
6 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. First Stanza (Past Illusion of Immortality):
In the opening quatrain, Wordsworth portrays a psychological state of romantic delusion. He was in a spiritual 'slumber' where human mortality seemed irrelevant. His beloved appeared so pure, radiant, and ethereal that he assumed she was beyond the reach of time, decay, and earthly years.

2. Second Stanza (Stark Reality and Cosmic Transformation):
The second stanza presents a devastating shock of reality: she is dead, devoid of motion, hearing, and breath. Yet Wordsworth does not sink into despair. Instead, he elevates her death into pantheistic transcendence. She becomes integrated with 'earth's diurnal course', revolving rhythmically with rocks, stones, and trees.

3. Conclusion:
In just eight minimalist lines, Wordsworth captures the shock of mortality and transmutes painful human grief into sublime cosmic serenity.

Pantheism & Romanticism
Literary Insight: How does Wordsworth's philosophy of Pantheism shine through this Lucy poem?

Wordsworth's philosophy of Pantheism views God and nature as synonymous. When Lucy dies, the poet does not imagine her in a conventional religious heaven; instead, her body returns to the earth's womb. By asserting that she rolls with rocks, stones, and trees, Wordsworth makes her an eternal participant in the cosmos. Death is not the end of existence, but a return to the infinite, cyclical energy of the natural world.

Poetic Structure
Form and Rhyme: What makes this eight-line poem extraordinarily powerful?

The poem's power lies in its extreme poetic restraint and brevity. Written in two simple elegiac stanzas with an ABAB CDCD rhyme scheme and alternating iambic tetrameter and trimeter (ballad meter), Wordsworth uses monosyllabic, everyday words ('motion', 'force', 'rocks', 'stones'). The total absence of melodrama or adjectives gives the poem a solemn, monumental, and haunting dignity.
