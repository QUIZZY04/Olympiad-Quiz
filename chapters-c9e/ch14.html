Poem 5: A Legend of the Northland

Phoebe Cary — Ballad on Greed, Charity & Saint Peter — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

Away, away in the Northland, Where the hours of the day are few, And the nights are so long in winter That they cannot sleep them through... He came to the door of a cottage, In traveling round the earth, Where a little woman was making cakes, And baking them on the hearth; And being faint with fasting, For the day was almost done, He asked her, from her store of cakes, To give him a single one.
— Phoebe Cary

Q2
What did Saint Peter ask the little woman for? What was her selfish reaction?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Saint Peter, famished and exhausted from wandering and preaching all day, asked the little woman for a single cake from her store of hearth-baked cakes. The selfish woman kneaded smaller and smaller dough pieces, but each time she baked one, it appeared too large to give away for free. Finally, she rolled out a dough scrap thin as a wafer, but even that she refused to part with, storing it greedily on the shelf.

Q3
How did Saint Peter punish the greedy woman? What bird did she become?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Saint Peter grew righteous in anger. He told her she was far too selfish to dwell in human form, blessed with food, shelter, and a warm hearth. He cursed her to become a bird that must build its nest by boring holes into dry, hard wood all day long for scarce food. She flew up the chimney without speaking a word and emerged as a woodpecker, retaining only her scarlet cap while her clothes burned black as coal.

Q4
What is a ballad? What moral lesson does 'A Legend of the Northland' impart? (Max Word Count)
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Form of a Ballad:
A ballad is a traditional folk poem or song that narrates a story through short, rhythmic four-line stanzas (quatrains), typically featuring a regular rhyme scheme (ABCB). Passed down orally through generations, ballads celebrate folklore, heroic deeds, or moral tales.

2. Moral Lesson:
Phoebe Cary's ballad conveys an enduring ethical warning against greed, selfishness, and lack of empathy. When human beings are blessed with abundance (shelter, warm hearth, food), they are morally obligated to share with the hungry and destitute. Greed degrades human dignity; the selfish woman's refusal to surrender even a wafer-thin scrap of bread strips her of humanity, condemning her to the hard, solitary labor of a woodpecker boring wood. The poem urges unconditional kindness and generosity.

Moral & Folk Tradition
Folklore Insight: Why do old legends like this continue to be narrated to children?

Although the poet admits 'I don't believe 'tis true; and yet you may learn a lesson if I tell the tale to you', legends serve as timeless educational instruments. They instill basic moral codes—such as charity, hospitability, and compassion—in young minds through memorable, vivid symbolism. By turning a greedy baker into a woodpecker tapping endlessly for food, the fable visualizes the spiritual poverty that greed causes, teaching children that hoarding brings misery while sharing brings grace.

Symbolism
Literary Analysis: What does the woman's scarlet cap symbolize?

The scarlet cap is the only surviving human relic of the woman after her transformation. It symbolizes her persistent vanity and arrogance. While all her functional clothing burns away into black soot in the chimney—representing the loss of domestic comforts—the proud scarlet cap remains fixed on the woodpecker's head, serving as a permanent badge of her shame and a warning sign to all who encounter her in the forest.
