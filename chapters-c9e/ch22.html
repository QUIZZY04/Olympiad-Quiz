Chapter 5: The Happy Prince

Oscar Wilde — Compassion, Sacrifice & True Spiritual Beauty — CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27

I. Thinking about the Text — Short Questions (30–40 Words / 2–3 Marks)
Q1
Why do the courtiers call the prince 'the Happy Prince'? Is he truly happy? What does he see all around him?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

When alive, the prince lived in the Palace of Sans-Souci where sorrow was forbidden to enter; he danced and played in gardens, so courtiers called him 'the Happy Prince'. In his statue form, high above the city, he is deeply sorrowful because his eyes can now see all the misery, destitution, and ugly suffering of his people.

Q2
Why does the Happy Prince send a ruby for the seamstress? What does the swallow do in her room?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Why Ruby was Sent: The prince saw a poor seamstress embroidering passion-flowers on a queen's maid's satin gown. Her little boy lay sick with fever in the corner, crying for oranges, but she had nothing to give him but river water. Her hands were pricked and red.

2. Swallow's Action: The swallow flew into the room, laid the great ruby on the table beside the woman's thimble, and gently fanned the sick boy's forehead with his wings, cooling his fever into sweet slumber.

Q3
For whom does the Prince send the sapphires from his eyes? What happens when both are gone?
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. The First Sapphire: Sent to a starving young playwright in a cold garret who is too frozen to finish his play for the theatre director.

2. The Second Sapphire: Sent to a poor little matchgirl whose matches had fallen into the gutter and spoiled; her father would beat her if she brought home no money.

3. The Consequence: Stripped of both sapphires, the Happy Prince becomes completely blind. Moved to tears by such boundless selflessness, the little swallow vows never to leave the prince and stays beside him forever, despite the deadly winter approaching.

Q4
How does the swallow die? What happens to the Prince's leaden heart?
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

As winter intensifies, frost and snow coat the city. The swallow grows colder and colder, yet refuses to desert the blind Prince, pecking crumbs outside the baker's door. Knowing death is near, he kisses the Prince on the lips and falls dead at his feet. At that exact moment, a curious cracking sound echoes inside the statue—the Prince's leaden heart breaks cleanly in two from profound grief and love. Even the town foundry furnace cannot melt that broken lead heart.

II. Long Answer Questions — Spiritual Nobility (100–120 Words / 6 Marks)
Q5
What are the 'two most precious things in the city' chosen by the Angel? Why did God praise this choice? (Max Word Count)
6 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. The City Council's Superficiality:
When the Mayor and Town Councillors see the statue stripped of gold leaf and jewels, they decree it is 'little better than a beggar' and tear it down, squabbling over who should have the next statue erected in their honor. The broken lead heart is discarded onto a dust heap alongside the dead swallow.

2. God's Divine Verdict:
God commands His Angel to bring Him the two most precious things in the entire city. The Angel descends and selects the leaden heart of the Happy Prince and the dead body of the little swallow.

3. Eternal Reward in Paradise:
God commends the Angel's supreme spiritual wisdom, proclaiming: 'You have rightly chosen: for in my garden of Paradise this little bird shall sing for evermore, and in my city of gold the Happy Prince shall praise me.'

Conclusion:
Oscar Wilde illustrates that superficial material beauty (gold, rubies, sapphires) decays into dust, whereas selfless love, sacrifice, and empathy are the only eternal treasures recognized in heaven.

🎯 Competency-Based & Value-Based Questions — Unit 22CBSE 2026-27
Moral & Spiritual Beauty
Christian Allegory: How does 'The Happy Prince' reflect the Christian ethic of self-sacrifice and charity?

Oscar Wilde frames the story as an allegory of Christ-like self-renunciation. The Prince, who was once secluded in earthly luxury, voluntarily gives away his beauty, his sight, and his gold skin to relieve human misery. The swallow acts as a devoted disciple, sacrificing his life and biological migratory instinct out of love for the Prince. Their physical death is not defeat, but spiritual resurrection, proving that giving oneself entirely for the redemption of others transforms mortality into divine grace.

Satire on Vanity
Social Satire: How does Wilde satirize the Victorian ruling elite through the Mayor and Councillors?

Wilde exposes the hollow vanity and hypocrisy of the ruling class. The Mayor and Councillors value the Prince only when he glitters with gold, using him as a status symbol to flatter civic vanity. When the Prince sacrifices his ornaments to feed starving children, the elite declare him 'shabby' and melt him down. Their squabble over who should have the next statue highlights political self-aggrandizement, contrasting their spiritual ugliness with the silent nobility of the Prince and the bird.
