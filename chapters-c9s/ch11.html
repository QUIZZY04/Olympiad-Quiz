Page 162 — Q1
How does the sound produced by a vibrating object in a medium reach your ear?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Propagation of Sound through Medium Particles:
1. When an object vibrates (such as a tuning fork prong or vocal cord), it exerts a periodic push on the air molecules in its immediate vicinity.
2. As the vibrating surface moves forward, it compresses and pushes the adjacent air particles forward, creating a localized high-pressure, high-density region called a Compression (C).
3. When the surface moves backward, it creates a localized low-pressure, low-density region called a Rarefaction (R).
4. Individual air particles do not travel all the way from the source to our ear; instead, they oscillate back and forth about their equilibrium mean positions, transferring momentum and energy to neighboring particles.
5. This disturbance travels forward as an acoustic pressure wave and impinges on the tympanic membrane (eardrum) of our ear, making it vibrate and producing the sensation of hearing.

Page 163 — Q2
Why are sound waves called mechanical waves?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Definition of Mechanical Waves:
Sound waves are termed mechanical waves (or elastic waves) because:
  1. They are produced by the physical mechanical oscillation of particles of matter.
  2. They strictly require a material medium (solid, liquid, or gas) with inertial and elastic properties for their propagation.
  3. They cannot propagate through a vacuum, unlike electromagnetic waves (such as light or radio waves) which can travel through empty space.

What are wavelength, frequency, time period and amplitude of a sound wave?
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Fundamental Wave Parameters:
  1. Wavelength (λ): The linear distance between two consecutive compressions or two consecutive rarefactions of a sound wave. SI unit: metre (m).
  2. Frequency ( u): The number of complete wave oscillations or cycles passing through a given point per second. SI unit: Hertz (Hz = s⁻¹).
  3. Time Period (T): The time taken by the wave to complete one full oscillation or cycle. T = 1 / u. SI unit: second (s).
  4. Amplitude (A): The magnitude of maximum displacement or disturbance of the medium particles from their mean equilibrium position. SI unit: metre (m) (or Pascal for density/pressure waves).

Exercise — Q2
Describe with the help of a diagram, how compressions and rarefactions are produced in air near a source of sound.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Mechanism of Compressions and Rarefactions:
Consider a vibrating tuning fork prong in air:
  1. Forward Stroke (Compression): When the prong moves rapidly forward, it compresses the air directly in front of it. A localized zone of high particle density and high pressure is created, called a Compression (C).
  2. Backward Stroke (Rarefaction): When the prong moves rapidly backward, a localized partial vacuum/decompression is created where particles spread apart, forming a zone of low particle density and low pressure called a Rarefaction (R).
  3. As the prong continues vibrating, a continuous periodic succession of compressions and rarefactions travels through the air as a longitudinal acoustic wave.

Cite an experiment to show that sound needs a material medium for its propagation.

The Electric Bell Jar Experiment:
  1. Experimental Setup: Suspend an electric bell inside an airtight glass bell jar connected to a vacuum suction pump via a bottom stopcock. Connect the bell to an external battery through a switch.
  2. Initial Observation: Press the switch. With air inside the bell jar, the ringing sound of the electric bell is clearly and loudly heard outside.

Why is sound wave called a longitudinal wave?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Definition of Longitudinal Wave:
A wave is called a longitudinal wave when the constituent particles of the medium oscillate back and forth parallel to the direction of propagation of the wave.

In a sound wave propagating horizontally through air, the air molecules oscillate along the same horizontal axis as the wave propagation, creating alternating regions of high and low pressure. Hence, sound in gases and liquids is strictly a longitudinal mechanical wave.

Exercise — Q10
Does sound follow the same laws of reflection as light does? Explain.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Answer: Yes, sound follows the EXACT SAME laws of reflection as light.

The Two Laws of Reflection of Sound:
  1. The incident sound wave, the reflected sound wave, and the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. The angle of incidence (∠i) is always equal to the angle of reflection (∠r) (∠i = ∠r).
Note: Unlike light which requires a polished mirror, sound waves have long wavelengths, so they reflect efficiently off large, rough, or unpolished surfaces like brick walls, cliffs, or wooden boards.

Exercise — Q11
When a sound is reflected from a distant object, an echo is produced. Let the distance between the reflecting surface and the source of sound production remains the same. Do you hear echo sound on a hotter day?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Scientific Analysis of Echo on a Hot Day:
1. The persistence of hearing for human ears is 0.1 s. To hear a distinct echo, the reflected sound must reach the ear at least 0.1 seconds after the original sound: t_{echo} = 2d / v \ge 0.1 s ⟹ d \ge v / 20.
2. The speed of sound in air is directly proportional to the square root of absolute temperature (v ∝ √T). On a hotter day, the speed of sound (v) increases.
3. Because v is higher, the minimum distance required to hear an echo (d_{min} = v × 0.1 / 2) increases (from ~17.2 m at 22°C to ~18 m+ on a very hot day).
4. If the distance to the obstacle was just barely sufficient on a cold day, on a hotter day the reflected sound will return in less than 0.1 s and blur into the original sound, meaning no distinct echo will be heard.

Exercise — Q15
What is reverberation? How can it be reduced?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
1. Definition of Reverberation:
The persistence of prolonged sound in an enclosed hall or room as a result of repeated and continuous multiple reflections from the walls, ceiling, and floor even after the sound source has stopped emitting is called reverberation.
Excessive reverberation causes sound overlapping and garbled, unintelligible speech.
2. Methods to Reduce Reverberation:
Reverberation is minimized by lining the hall with high sound-absorbing materials:
  1. Covering the walls and ceilings with sound-absorbent materials like compressed acoustic fiberboards, rough plaster, or perforated acoustic tiles.
  2. Hanging heavy, folded fabric draperies and curtains over windows and doors.
  3. Using plush upholstered seats with porous fabrics (and carpets on floors) that absorb sound energy.

Exercise — Q19
Explain the working and application of a SONAR.
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
SONAR (SOund Navigation And Ranging):
• Working Principle:
1. A SONAR unit installed on a ship or submarine consists of two primary components: a Transmitter and a Receiver (Detector).
2. The transmitter generates and transmits powerful ultrasonic pulses down through seawater.
3. These ultrasound pulses travel through the water, strike underwater objects (such as the seabed, sunken ships, submarines, or schools of fish), and reflect back.
4. The reflected echo is captured by the detector, which converts ultrasonic waves into electrical signals.
5. The depth/distance (d) is calculated using: 2d = v × t ⟹ d = (v × t) / 2.

• Key Applications:
- Measuring ocean depth (bathymetry).
- Detecting enemy submarines, underwater icebergs, marine trenches, and sunken shipwrecks.

Exercise — Q21
Explain how defects in a metal block can be detected using ultrasound.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Non-Destructive Flaw Detection using Ultrasound:
1. Ultrasound waves can easily penetrate deep into metallic solids.
2. In industry, metallic blocks used in building bridges, airplanes, and heavy machinery are tested for internal flaws (cracks, voids, blowholes).
3. High-frequency ultrasonic beams are passed from one side of the metal block, and sensitive electronic detectors are placed on the opposite face.
4. If the block is flawless: The ultrasound waves pass straight through the metal and are completely registered by all opposing detectors.
5. If there is a defect or crack: The ultrasound beam strikes the internal boundary of the crack, reflects prematurely backward, and does not reach the opposite detector.
6. The absence or attenuation of transmitted signal reveals the presence, depth, and exact location of internal defects without breaking the metal block.

Anatomy and Physiology of the Human Ear:
The human ear consists of three primary anatomical sections:
  1. Outer Ear:
    • Pinna: Collects sound waves from the surrounding environment.
    • Auditory Canal: Directs sound waves inward to the thin, delicate tympanic membrane (eardrum). Compressions push the membrane inward, while rarefactions pull it outward, setting it into vibration.
  2. Middle Ear:
    • Contains three tiny interconnected bones called ossicles: Hammer (Malleus), Anvil (Incus), and Stirrup (Stapes).
    • The ossicles act as a mechanical lever system, amplifying the vibration amplitude by about 20 to 30 times and transferring it through the oval window to the inner ear.
  3. Inner Ear:
    • Consists of a fluid-filled snail-shell-like coiled organ called the Cochlea.
    • Fluid pressure waves inside the cochlea stimulate microscopic hair cells (sensory receptors), transforming mechanical pressure vibrations into electrical nerve impulses.
    • These nerve impulses travel via the Auditory Nerve to the temporal lobe of the brain, which decodes them as sound.

📝 Case Study Question
Medical Echocardiography & Ultrasonography: Doctors routinely use ultrasound imaging (USG) to monitor foetal development and diagnose cardiac valve defects.
(a) Why is ultrasound preferred over X-rays for prenatal foetal examination?
(b) On what property of ultrasound does medical imaging depend?
(c) What is the frequency range of ultrasound used in medical diagnostics?
(a) Ultrasound waves are mechanical sound waves that do not cause ionizing cellular or DNA radiation damage, making them completely safe for developing foetuses, unlike hazardous ionizing X-rays.
(b) It depends on the reflection of ultrasound waves from acoustic impedance boundaries between different soft tissue layers.
(c) Typically between 2 MHz and 15 MHz (well above human hearing).
🧠 HOTS
HOTS Question: Why do dogs respond to a Galton whistle while humans standing right next to the dog cannot hear anything at all?
A Galton whistle produces high-frequency ultrasonic sound waves above 20 kHz (typically around 25 kHz to 30 kHz). The upper hearing threshold of human ears is strictly limited to 20 kHz (20,000 Hz), so humans cannot perceive it. Dogs have a much wider auditory frequency spectrum extending up to 45 kHz to 50 kHz, allowing them to hear and respond readily to the whistle.
