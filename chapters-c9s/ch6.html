What is a tissue?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Scientific Definition:
A tissue is defined as a cluster or group of structurally similar (or coordinated) cells that have a common embryonic origin and work together synergistically to perform a specific, specialized physiological function within a multicellular organism.
Examples: Phloem tissue in vascular plants (specialized for translocation of organic nutrients), Nervous tissue in animals (specialized for conduction of electrical impulses).

Page 69 — Q2
What is the utility of tissues in multi-cellular organisms?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Utility & Advantages of Tissues:
  1. Division of Labour: In multicellular organisms, different groups of specialized cells (tissues) take charge of distinct physiological tasks. For example, muscle tissues contract to produce movement, nerve cells transmit impulses, and xylem transports water.
  2. Higher Functional Efficiency: Cells specialized for one particular task carry out that function with maximum efficiency and precision compared to a solitary cell trying to perform all tasks.
  3. Structural Organization & Complexity: Tissues assemble into organs and organ systems, conferring higher morphological complexity, physical strength, and endurance.
  4. Enhanced Survival Rate: Multicellular organization ensures that damage to a single cell or group of cells does not immediately kill the entire organism.

Page 74 — Q1
Name types of simple tissues.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Simple Permanent Tissues in Plants:
Simple tissues are composed of only one single type of structurally identical cells. The three main types are:
  1. Parenchyma: Living, unspecialized cells with thin cellulose walls; primary site for food storage, photosynthesis (chlorenchyma), and buoyancy (aerenchyma).
  2. Collenchyma: Living cells with localized pectin and cellulose thickenings at the corners; provides flexibility and mechanical support without breaking.
  3. Sclerenchyma: Dead cells with heavily lignified, thick secondary walls and narrow lumens; provides immense rigidity, hardness, and mechanical strength.

Page 74 — Q3
Which tissue makes up the husk of coconut?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Tissue Identity & Characteristics:
• The fibrous husk of a coconut (coir) is made up of Sclerenchyma tissue (specifically sclerenchymatous fibres).
• Characteristics:
- The cells of this tissue are dead at maturity.
- They have extremely thick secondary cell walls heavily deposited with lignin (a natural chemical cement that hardens walls).
- They lack intracellular protoplasm and have narrow lumens, conferring extreme tensile strength, stiffness, and durability to protect the coconut seed.

Page 74 — Q4
What are the constituents of phloem?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Four Major Constituents of Phloem Tissue:
  1. Sieve Tubes (and Sieve Cells): Tubular cells with perforated end-walls (sieve plates) through which photosynthesized sugars flow.
  2. Companion Cells: Specialized parenchymatous living cells closely associated with sieve tube elements, possessing active nuclei that regulate sieve tube transport.
  3. Phloem Parenchyma: Living elongated cells that store food (starch, resins, latex) and assist in lateral conduction.
  4. Phloem Fibres (Bast Fibres): Dead, highly lignified sclerenchymatous cells that provide mechanical tensile support. (Note: Phloem fibres are the only dead elements of phloem; the other three are living).

Page 78 — Q2
What does a neuron look like?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Morphology of a Neuron (Nerve Cell):
A neuron resembles a tiny branched tree. It consists of three distinct anatomical regions:
  1. Cyton (Cell Body / Soma): Contains a prominent spherical nucleus, neuroplasm, and Nissl's granules.
  2. Dendrites: Short, highly branched, tapering cytoplasmic extensions emerging from the cyton that receive incoming nerve impulses from receptors or other neurons.
  3. Axon: A single, long, cylindrical cytoplasmic nerve fibre extending away from the cell body (often insulated by a myelin sheath) that transmits electrical action potentials away to terminal arborizations and synaptic knobs. Neurons can be up to a metre long in humans.

Page 78 — Q3
Give three features of cardiac muscles.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Characteristic Features of Cardiac Muscle Tissue:
  1. Involuntary and Rhythmic: They work automatically and unconsciously under autonomic control; they contract and relax rhythmically throughout life without showing physical fatigue.
  2. Cylindrical and Branched: Cardiac muscle cells are cylindrical fibres interconnected by specialized lateral oblique bridges, forming a 3D syncytial network.
  3. Uninucleate with Intercalated Discs: Each muscle cell typically contains a single centrally located nucleus and features dark transverse junctions called intercalated discs that allow rapid electrical coupling between adjacent cells. They also exhibit faint transverse striations.

Page 78 — Q4
What are the functions of areolar tissue?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Functions of Areolar Connective Tissue:
Areolar tissue is a loose connective tissue found between skin and muscles, around blood vessels and nerves, and in the bone marrow. Its key functions are:
  1. Packing and Structural Cushioning: It acts as a supportive packing tissue filling empty spaces inside organs and binding neighboring anatomical structures together.
  2. Support to Internal Organs: It anchors blood vessels and nerve bundles in place within organ parenchyma.
  3. Tissue Repair: Its fibroblast and macrophage cells play an active role in wound healing, collagen deposition, and defense against localized pathogens.

How many types of elements together make up the xylem tissue? Name them.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Four Types of Elements Make Up Xylem Tissue:
  1. Tracheids: Elongated, dead cells with tapering ends, lignified walls, and pits for water conduction.
  2. Vessels: Long, continuous tubular pipes formed by end-to-end fusion of dead cylindrical cells with dissolved end walls.
  3. Xylem Parenchyma: The only living element of xylem; stores starch and fats and assists in lateral sideways conduction of water.
  4. Xylem Sclerenchyma (Xylem Fibres): Highly lignified dead fibres providing tensile mechanical strength to plant axes.

Exercise — Q5
What are the functions of the stomata?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Functions of Stomata:
Stomata are microscopic pores located in the leaf epidermis, bounded by a pair of specialized kidney-shaped (or dumb-bell-shaped in grasses) guard cells.
  1. Gaseous Exchange: They facilitate the bidirectional diffusion of respiratory gases (O₂ intake and CO₂ release during respiration; CO₂ intake and O₂ release during photosynthesis) between internal mesophyll tissue and atmosphere.
  2. Transpiration: They regulate the loss of excess water in the form of water vapour from the aerial surfaces of leaves. Transpiration creates the transpiration pull essential for water and mineral ascent in tall trees and provides evaporative cooling to prevent heat injury.

Exercise — Q12
Name the regions in which parenchyma tissue is present.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Occurrence and Distribution of Parenchyma:
Parenchyma forms the major bulk of the plant body (ground tissue) and is found in:
  1. Cortex, Pith, and Medullary Rays of roots and dicot/monocot stems.
  2. Mesophyll of Leaves: Present as specialized photosynthetic chlorenchyma (palisade and spongy parenchyma) containing chloroplasts.
  3. Petioles, Flowers, and Fruits: Forms the fleshy pulp of edible fruits (e.g. apples, tomatoes) and endosperm of seeds storing starch and nutrients.
  4. Aquatic Plants: Forms aerenchyma with large air cavities in petiole and leaves of hydrophytes (e.g. lotus, hydrilla) to provide buoyancy.
  5. Vascular Bundles: Present as xylem parenchyma and phloem parenchyma.

Exercise — Q13
What is the role of epidermis in plants?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Protective Functions of Plant Epidermis:
  1. Barrier & Protection: Forms a continuous single outermost protective sheet covering the entire plant body, shielding internal tissues against mechanical injury, desiccation, and parasite invasion.
  2. Prevention of Water Loss (Cuticle): Epidermal cells of aerial parts secrete a waxy, water-resistant lipid coating called cutin on their outer walls, preventing excessive evaporative water loss.
  3. Gas Exchange & Transpiration: Epidermis houses stomata with guard cells, which regulate the intake of CO₂ for photosynthesis and release water vapour via transpiration.
  4. Water and Mineral Absorption (Root Hairs): Root epidermal cells have unicellular extensions called root hairs that increase surface area for efficient absorption of soil moisture.

Exercise — Q14
How does the cork act as a protective tissue?
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Protective Mechanisms of Cork (Phellem):
As a woody tree grows older, a secondary lateral meristem (cork cambium or phellogen) develops in the outer cortex, forming multiple compact layers of dead cork cells.
  1. Suberin Deposition: The walls of cork cells are heavily deposited with an organic waxy substance called suberin, which makes them completely impermeable to water and gases.
  2. Mechanical Protection: Cork cells are dead, tightly packed without intercellular spaces, acting as an insulating shock-absorbent shield protecting inner vascular bundles against mechanical blows, fire, and extreme temperature fluctuations.
  3. Defense Against Pathogens: Due to lack of living protoplasm and suberized impermeable walls, bacteria, fungi, and insect pests cannot penetrate into the plant body.

📝 Case Study Question
Girdling Experiment and Vascular Transport: A biologist removes a complete ring of bark (including phloem tissue) from the trunk of a woody tree while keeping the central xylem cylinder intact.
(a) What will happen to the leaves and roots initially?
(b) Which organ will starve and die first: roots or leaves? Explain why.
(c) What does this experiment prove regarding the specific functions of xylem and phloem?
(a) Initially, the leaves will remain green and healthy because the intact xylem continues to supply water and minerals upward from the roots. However, roots cannot receive organic food synthesised by leaves.
(b) The roots will die first because translocation of sucrose through downward phloem is blocked at the girdle; once root food reserves are exhausted, roots starve, stop active water uptake, and the whole tree wilts and dies.
(c) This proves that phloem is responsible for bidirectional translocation of organic food, while xylem conducts water and mineral sap unidirectionally upward.
🧠 HOTS
HOTS Question: Why do desert plants (xerophytes) have an extremely thick waxy cuticle, sunken stomata, and multiple epidermal layers?
Desert plants face extreme water scarcity and high ambient temperatures. A thick waxy cuticle (cutin), multiple epidermal layers, and stomata sunken deep in epidermal crypts (often surrounded by epidermal hairs) minimize the water vapour pressure gradient and reduce transpirational water loss to near zero, preventing dehydration and thermal death.
