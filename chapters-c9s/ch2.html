NCERT Intext Questions (Page 15)
Page 15 — Q1
What is meant by a substance?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Scientific Definition:
A substance (or pure substance) is a distinct form of matter that has:
  1. A uniform, definite, and constant chemical composition throughout.
  2. A specific and fixed set of characteristic physical and chemical properties (such as exact melting point, boiling point, and density).
  3. Cannot be separated into other kinds of matter by any physical processes (e.g. pure gold, pure water H₂O, sodium chloride NaCl).

NCERT Intext Questions (Page 18)
Page 18 — Q1
Differentiate between homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures with examples.
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Key Differences:
• Homogeneous Mixtures: Mixtures in which the components are uniformly distributed and cannot be distinguished even under an optical microscope. They consist of a single uniform phase.
Examples: Sodium chloride dissolved in water, ethanol in water, bronze (alloy).

• Heterogeneous Mixtures: Mixtures that do not possess uniform composition; individual components remain physically separate and form distinct visible phases.
Examples: Muddy water, kerosene oil in water, gunpowder (mixture of sulfur, charcoal, and potassium nitrate).

Page 18 — Q3
To make a saturated solution, 36 g of sodium chloride is dissolved in 100 g of water at 293 K. Find its concentration at this temperature.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Given Data:
• Mass of solute (Sodium chloride, NaCl) = 36 g
• Mass of solvent (Water, H₂O) = 100 g
Step 1: Calculate Total Mass of Solution:
Mass of solution = Mass of solute + Mass of solvent
Mass of solution = 36 g + 100 g = 136 g
Step 2: Concentration Formula (Mass by Mass Percentage):
Concentration (mass %) = (Mass of Solute / Mass of Solution) × 100
Concentration = (36 / 136) × 100 = (9 / 34) × 100 = 900 / 34 ≈ 26.47% (w/w)

Final Answer: The concentration of the sodium chloride solution at 293 K is 26.47% (by mass).

NCERT Intext Questions (Page 24)
Page 24 — Q1
Classify each of the following as a chemical or physical change:
• Cutting of trees
• Melting of butter in a pan
• Rusting of almirah
• Boiling of water to form steam
• Passing of electric current through water and the water breaking down into hydrogen and oxygen gases
• Dissolving common salt in water
• Making a fruit salad with raw fruits
• Burning of paper and wood
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Concept:
• Physical Change: No new chemical substance is formed; chemical identity remains intact; generally reversible.
• Chemical Change: One or more new chemical substances with distinct properties are formed; irreversible by simple physical methods.
Classification & Justifications:
  • Cutting of trees: Physical Change (alters shape and size; chemical structure of wood remains cellulose/lignin).
  • Melting of butter in a pan: Physical Change (phase change from solid to liquid lipid; no new substance formed).
  • Rusting of almirah: Chemical Change (iron oxidizes to hydrated iron(III) oxide: 4Fe + 3O₂ + 2xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O).
  • Boiling of water to form steam: Physical Change (phase transition H₂O(l) → H₂O(g); molecular composition unchanged).
  • Electrolysis of water into H₂ and O₂: Chemical Change (chemical decomposition 2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g); new substances formed).
  • Dissolving common salt in water: Physical Change (hydration of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions; salt can be recovered completely by evaporation).
  • Making a fruit salad with raw fruits: Physical Change (mechanical cutting and mixing; no chemical reaction occurs).
  • Burning of paper and wood: Chemical Change (combustion reaction producing CO₂, H₂O vapour, ash, and heat/light; irreversible).

Exercise — Q2
Write the steps you would use for making tea. Use the words solution, solvent, solute, dissolve, soluble, insoluble, filtrate and residue.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Recipe for Making Tea using Chemical Terminology:
  1. Take a required volume of water in a saucepan. Water acts as the universal solvent. Heat it over a stove.
  2. Add sugar to the hot solvent. Sugar acts as a solute which is highly soluble in water. Stir the liquid so that the sugar crystals dissolve completely to form a homogeneous solution.
  3. Add tea leaves to the boiling solution. The water-soluble aromatic compounds and pigments dissolve into the liquid, while the solid fibrous part of the tea leaves remains completely insoluble.
  4. Add milk (which is an emulsion) to this mixture and allow the concoction to boil.
  5. Pour the boiling mixture through a fine mesh strainer into a cup.
  6. The prepared tea that passes through the strainer into the cup is the filtrate.
  7. The insoluble spent tea leaves left behind on the strainer mesh constitute the residue.

Exercise — Q3
Pragya tested the solubility of three different substances at different temperatures and collected the following data (results are given in the following table, as grams of substance dissolved in 100 grams of water to form a saturated solution).
Substance Dissolved283 K293 K313 K333 K353 K
Potassium nitrate213262106167
Sodium chloride3636363737
Potassium chloride3535404654
Ammonium chloride2437415566

(a) What mass of potassium nitrate would be needed to produce a saturated solution of potassium nitrate in 50 grams of water at 313 K?
(b) Pragya makes a saturated solution of potassium chloride in water at 353 K and leaves the solution to cool at room temperature. What would she observe as the solution cools? Explain.
(c) Find the solubility of each salt at 293 K. Which salt has the highest solubility at this temperature?
(d) What is the effect of change of temperature on the solubility of a salt?
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
(a) Potassium nitrate needed in 50 g water at 313 K:
From the table, solubility of potassium nitrate at 313 K = 62 g per 100 g of water.
Therefore, mass needed in 50 g of water = (62 / 100) × 50 = 31 g.
Answer: 31 grams of KNO₃ is needed.
(b) Observation on cooling KCl solution from 353 K to room temperature (approx 293 K):
• At 353 K, 54 g of KCl dissolves in 100 g water.
• At room temperature (293 K), only 35 g of KCl can remain dissolved in 100 g water.
• As the saturated solution cools, the excess solute (54 g - 35 g = 19 g of KCl) can no longer remain in solution.
Observation: Fine solid crystals of potassium chloride (KCl) will precipitate and separate out at the bottom of the beaker (crystallization).
(c) Solubility at 293 K:
• Potassium nitrate = 32 g / 100 g water
• Sodium chloride = 36 g / 100 g water
• Potassium chloride = 35 g / 100 g water
• Ammonium chloride = 37 g / 100 g water
Answer: Ammonium chloride has the highest solubility (37 g) at 293 K.
(d) Effect of temperature on solubility:
The solubility of most solid salts in water increases with an increase in temperature (evident from KNO₃, KCl, and NH₄Cl). For some salts like NaCl, the solubility remains practically constant with temperature increase.

Exercise — Q4
Explain the following giving examples:
(a) Saturated solution
(b) Pure substance
(c) Colloid
(d) Suspension
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
(a) Saturated Solution:
A solution in which no more quantity of solute can be dissolved at a given specified temperature is called a saturated solution. Any extra solute added remains undissolved at the bottom.
Example: Dissolving table salt in water until excess salt crystals settle at the bottom and cannot dissolve further at 25°C.
(b) Pure Substance:
A material composed of only one single type of atom or molecule with fixed, invariable chemical composition and definite physical constants (such as sharp melting and boiling points). It cannot be broken down into simpler constituents by any physical means.
Example: Pure distilled water (H₂O), pure copper metal (Cu), sucrose (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁).
(c) Colloid (Colloidal Solution):
A heterogeneous mixture in which solute particle sizes range between 1 nm and 1000 nm (intermediate between true solution and suspension). The particles are small enough not to settle under gravity, pass through filter paper, and scatter a beam of visible light showing the Tyndall effect.
Example: Milk, gelatin, blood, ink, fog.
(d) Suspension:
A heterogeneous mixture containing large insoluble solid solute particles (> 1000 nm) dispersed throughout the bulk of a liquid medium without dissolving. The particles are visible to the naked eye and settle down under gravity upon standing.
Example: Chalk powder in water, muddy pond water, sand stirred in water.

Exercise — Q5
Classify each of the following as a homogeneous or heterogeneous mixture:
Soda water, wood, air, soil, vinegar, filtered tea.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Scientific Classification:
  • Homogeneous Mixtures:
    • Soda water: Clear solution of CO₂ gas uniformly dissolved in water under pressure.
    • Air: Clean, dust-free air is a uniform gaseous solution of N₂, O₂, Ar, CO₂, etc.
    • Vinegar: Homogeneous 5–8% aqueous solution of acetic acid in water.
    • Filtered tea: Uniform clear liquid solution of soluble tea extracts, sugar, and water with no suspended particles.
  • Heterogeneous Mixtures:
    • Wood: Natural composite material with visible grains, varying lignin, cellulose fibers, and sap channels.
    • Soil: Complex non-uniform mixture containing sand, clay, humus, organic decay, and mineral fragments.

Exercise — Q6
How would you confirm that a colourless liquid given to you is pure water?
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Experimental Confirmation through Physical Constants:
Every pure chemical substance has fixed, sharp, and invariable physical boiling and freezing points at 1 atmosphere pressure.
1. Boiling Point Determination: Heat a sample of the colourless liquid in a distillation apparatus with a sensitive thermometer placed in the vapour phase.
• If the liquid boils sharply at exactly 100°C (373 K) at 1 atm without any temperature variation during phase transition, it confirms the liquid is pure water.
• (If dissolved impurities like salts are present, the boiling point will elevate above 100°C).
2. Freezing Point Determination: Cool the liquid. If it freezes sharply at exactly 0°C (273 K), it proves purity.
3. Evaporation Test: Evaporate a drop on a clean watch glass; pure water leaves absolutely zero solid residue behind.
CBSE Marking Scheme 2026-27
Testing sharp boiling point at 100°C (373 K) at 1 atm1 Mark
Testing sharp freezing point at 0°C or zero residue on evaporation1 Mark
Exercise — Q7
Which of the following materials fall in the category of a 'pure substance'?
(a) Ice, (b) Milk, (c) Iron, (d) Hydrochloric acid, (e) Calcium oxide, (f) Mercury, (g) Brick, (h) Wood, (i) Air.
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Evaluation of Materials:
A pure substance is made of only one type of constituent particles (either an element or a chemical compound) with fixed chemical composition.

  • Wood: Heterogeneous mixture of cellulose, lignin, resins, and moisture.
  • Air: Gaseous mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide, etc.

(b) Sea water
(c) Air

A solution is strictly defined as a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances.

Exercise — Q9
Which of the following will show 'Tyndall effect'?
(a) Salt solution
(b) Milk
(c) Copper sulphate solution
(d) Starch solution
2 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Scientific Principle:
The Tyndall effect is the scattering of a visible light beam by colloidal particles whose diameter is comparable to the wavelength of light (1 nm to 1000 nm). True solutions have particles smaller than 1 nm and cannot scatter light.

• (b) Milk: A colloid (emulsion of liquid butterfat globules in water). Shows Tyndall effect.
• (d) Starch solution: A colloidal sol of starch macromolecules dispersed in water. Shows Tyndall effect.
• (a) Salt solution and (c) Copper sulphate solution: These are true homogeneous solutions (< 1 nm particles); light passes straight through without scattering.

Final Answer: (b) Milk and (d) Starch solution will show the Tyndall effect.

Exercise — Q11
Which of the following are chemical changes?
(a) Growth of a plant
(b) Rusting of iron
(c) Mixing of iron filings and sand
(d) Cooking of food
(e) Digestion of food
(f) Freezing of water
(g) Burning of a candle
3 Marks
✅ CBSE Standard Answer
Analysis of Changes:
  • (a) Growth of a plant: Chemical Change (Photosynthesis, cellular respiration, irreversible synthesis of organic matter).
  • (b) Rusting of iron: Chemical Change (Oxidation of iron to hydrated ferric oxide Fe₂O₃·xH₂O).
  • (c) Mixing of iron filings and sand: Physical Change (No reaction; iron can be separated with a magnet).
  • (d) Cooking of food: Chemical Change (Denaturation of proteins, breakdown of starch; new flavours/compounds formed, irreversible).
  • (e) Digestion of food: Chemical Change (Enzymatic hydrolysis of complex polymers into simple monomers).
  • (f) Freezing of water: Physical Change (Phase transition liquid → solid ice; molecular formula H₂O is unchanged).
  • (g) Burning of a candle: Both Chemical and Physical Change (Combustion of molten wax into CO₂ and H₂O is a chemical change, while melting of solid wax is a physical change. As burning is involved, it is classified as a chemical change).

Final Answer: The chemical changes are: (a) Growth of a plant, (b) Rusting of iron, (d) Cooking of food, (e) Digestion of food, and (g) Burning of a candle.

📝 Case Study Question
Colloidal Applications in Daily Life: A teacher shines a laser pointer through three beakers: Beaker A contains aqueous NaCl solution, Beaker B contains milk diluted with water, and Beaker C contains ferric hydroxide sol.
(a) In which beaker(s) will the path of the light beam become illuminated? Name this optical phenomenon.
(b) Why do the colloidal particles not settle down under gravity like a suspension?
(c) What happens if an electrolyte (like concentrated NaCl) is added to Beaker C?
(a) The path will become illuminated in Beakers B and C. This phenomenon is called the Tyndall Effect.
(b) Colloidal particles are held in continuous random zigzag motion (Brownian motion) caused by unbalanced bombardment of dispersion medium molecules, which opposes gravity and prevents settling. They also carry like electrical charges that cause mutual repulsion.
(c) Addition of an electrolyte neutralizes the surface charge on the colloidal particles, causing them to aggregate and precipitate out — a process called coagulation (flocculation).
🧠 HOTS
HOTS Question: Why is an alloy considered a mixture even though its constituent metals are completely fused and cannot be separated by physical filtration?
An alloy (such as brass = 70% Cu + 30% Zn) is classified as a mixture because: (1) It retains and displays the individual properties of its constituent metals (e.g., electrical conductivity and chemical reactivity of Cu and Zn), (2) Its composition can vary within a range (e.g., bronze can have varying Sn percentages), and (3) It does not have a single chemical formula representing fixed stoichiometric chemical bonding.
