Q1
What do you understand by globalisation? Explain the various ways in which MNCs set up, or control, production in other countries.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Meaning of Globalisation:

Globalisation is the process of rapid integration or interconnection between countries. It involves the free flow of goods, services, investments, technology, and people across national boundaries, transforming the world into a single interconnected global market.

2. Major Ways MNCs Set Up or Control Production Across Countries:

  1. Setting Up Production Jointly with Local Companies (Joint Ventures):
    • MNCs enter into partnership agreements with existing domestic companies. This provides dual benefits to local firms: MNCs bring in additional capital for modern machines and introduce advanced production technology (e.g., Ford Motors entered India in partnership with Mahindra & Mahindra).
  2. Buying Up Local Companies and Expanding Production:
    • The most common route for MNC investment is to buy up existing local companies and then expand production. MNCs with huge wealth can easily do so.
    • Example: Cargill Foods, a very large American MNC, bought over smaller Indian companies such as Parakh Foods, gaining control over large marketing networks and four oil refineries to become the largest producer of edible oil in India.
  3. Placing Orders with Small Producers (Contract Manufacturing):
    • Large MNCs in garments, footwear, and sports equipment place production orders with thousands of small producers around the world (including India, Bangladesh, and Vietnam).
    • The products are supplied to the MNCs, which then sell these under their own brand names (e.g., Nike, Zara, Adidas) to consumers, exerting tremendous power to determine price, quality, delivery, and labour conditions for distant producers.

Q2
What were the reasons for putting barriers to foreign trade and foreign investment by the Indian government after independence? Why did it wish to remove these barriers around 1991?
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The trade policy of the Indian government underwent a historic reversal from protectionism (1947–1990) to liberalisation (post-1991):

  1. Reasons for Putting Barriers After Independence (1947):
    • The Indian government, after independence, had put barriers to foreign trade and foreign investment to protect domestic infant industries from foreign competition.
    • Industries were just coming up in the 1950s and 1960s, and competition from imports at that nascent stage would not have allowed these domestic industries to survive. India allowed imports of only essential items such as machinery, fertilisers, and petroleum. All developed countries had used trade barriers during early stages of their industrialisation.
  2. Reasons for Removing Barriers in 1991 (New Economic Policy):
    • Around 1991, severe balance-of-payments crisis and foreign exchange shortages forced a re-evaluation of policy.
    • The government felt that the time had come for Indian producers to compete with producers around the globe. It was felt that competition would improve the performance and quality of goods produced by domestic manufacturers.
    • Removing barriers (trade liberalisation) allowed businesses to freely import raw materials and modern machinery, while attracting foreign direct investment (FDI) from multinational corporations.

Post-independence protectionist barriers: Shielding infant industries2.5 Marks

Q3
'The impact of globalisation has not been uniform.' Explain this statement with examples of both beneficiaries and sufferers.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The impact of globalisation in India has been deeply uneven, creating major beneficiaries alongside vulnerable groups that have suffered severely:

  1. The Big Winners (Advantaged Groups):
    • Urban Affluent Consumers: Consumers have enjoyed greater choice, improved quality, and lower prices for products like automobiles, mobile phones, television, soft drinks, and packaged fast foods.
    • Top Indian Companies as MNCs: Globalisation enabled top Indian companies to upgrade technology and emerge as multinationals themselves (e.g., Tata Motors in automobiles, Infosys in IT, Ranbaxy in pharma, Asian Paints, Sundaram Fasteners).
    • IT and Service Industry Professionals: High-paying employment opportunities in software, data processing, call centers, and engineering consultancy.
  2. The Sufferers (Disadvantaged Groups):
    • Small Scale Manufacturers: Global competition hit small producers hard in industries like batteries, capacitors, plastics, toys, tyres, dairy products, and vegetable oil. Many small units were forced to shut down because they could not compete with cheap, mass-produced Chinese imports, rendering millions of workers jobless.
    • Unorganised Industrial Labour: To cut production costs in the face of fierce global competition, employers prefer to hire workers 'flexibly' on temporary contracts. Workers are denied permanent job contracts, health insurance, and paid leave, working 12–14 hour shifts with precarious incomes.

Q6
What is globalisation? Describe the major factors that have enabled and accelerated the process of globalisation.
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Meaning of Globalisation:

Globalisation is the process of rapid integration or interconnection between countries through the cross-border movement of goods, services, investments, technology, and people.

Enabling Factors:

  1. Rapid Improvements in Transportation Technology: Dramatic developments in shipping containerisation, long-distance air freight, and mega-vessels have drastically cut transportation costs and accelerated goods delivery across continents.
  2. Revolution in Information and Communication Technology (ICT): Telecommunication facilities (telegraph, mobile phones, satellite links) and the Internet allow instantaneous communication around the globe at negligible cost.
  3. Liberalisation of Foreign Trade and Investment Policies: Removing government barriers, import licensing, and trade tariffs has enabled unrestricted flow of goods and Multinational Corporations (MNCs) to invest across national borders.
  4. International Organisations (WTO): Pressure from multilateral institutions like the World Trade Organisation to remove protective trade barriers worldwide.

Q7
What are Multinational Corporations (MNCs)? Explain the three distinct ways in which MNCs set up or control production in other countries.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Definition of Multinational Corporation (MNC):

An MNC is a company that owns or controls production of goods or services in more than one nation, locating its factories and offices in regions where cheap labour and other resources are readily available to minimize costs and maximize profits.

Three Ways MNCs Control Production Across Borders:

  1. Joint Ventures with Local Companies:
    • MNCs set up production jointly with existing local companies in the host country.
    • Benefit to local company: The MNC brings additional financial investment for new machines and provides advanced international production technology (e.g., Ford Motors entered India jointly with Mahindra & Mahindra).
  2. Buying Up Local Companies and Expanding Production:
    • The most common route for MNC investment is to buy up established domestic companies and then expand production utilizing vast financial reserves.
    • Example: Cargill Foods (a giant American MNC) bought over Parakh Foods in India, gaining access to their vast marketing network and oil refineries.
  3. Placing Orders with Small Producers (Outsourcing / Contract Manufacturing):
    • Large MNCs in garments, footwear, and sports equipment place orders with thousands of small producers around the world. The MNCs then sell these products under their own prestigious brand names, controlling price, quality, delivery, and labour conditions.

Q8
Why did the Indian government put trade barriers on foreign trade and investment after independence? Why did it wish to remove these barriers around 1991?
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Reasons for Putting Trade Barriers After Independence (1947–1990):

  • Protecting Infant Domestic Industries: Domestic industries were just coming up in the 1950s and 1960s, and competition from technologically advanced foreign imports at that stage would not have allowed these infant industries to survive.
  • Encouraging Indigenous Self-Reliance: India allowed imports of only essential items such as machinery, fertilizers, and petroleum to conserve scarce foreign exchange. All developed nations protected their early industries through similar trade barriers.

2. Reasons for Removing Trade Barriers in 1991 (New Economic Policy / Liberalisation):

  • Improving Competitiveness of Indian Producers: The government felt that the time had come for Indian producers to compete with producers around the globe, believing that international competition would force Indian companies to upgrade quality.
  • Economic Crisis and International Support: In 1991, India faced a severe balance-of-payments crisis with dwindling foreign exchange reserves, requiring financial assistance from the World Bank and IMF, which mandated economic liberalisation.
  • Attracting Foreign Direct Investment (FDI): Removing licensing restrictions and import duties attracted foreign technology, modern management, and capital inflows.

Q9
What is the role of the World Trade Organisation (WTO) in liberalising international trade? Mention two major criticisms leveled against the functioning of the WTO.
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Role of the World Trade Organisation (WTO):

  1. WTO is an international organisation whose aim is to liberalise international trade. Started at the initiative of developed countries, it establishes rules regarding international trade and sees that these rules are obeyed.
  2. It provides a multilateral forum for negotiating the reduction of tariffs and elimination of non-tariff trade barriers among over 160 member countries.

Two Major Criticisms against the WTO:

  1. Unfair Imbalance in Practice: Although WTO is supposed to allow free trade for all, in practice, developed countries have unfairly retained protective trade barriers and massive subsidies (especially agricultural subsidies to US and EU farmers), while forcing developing countries to remove their import protections.
  2. Domination by Developed Powers: Developed nations dominate decision-making, compelling developing nations to open their markets to foreign manufactured goods while blocking free movement of labour and agricultural exports from developing countries.

Q10
Analyze the positive impacts of globalisation on Indian consumers, top Indian producers, and service providers.
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Globalisation has delivered significant benefits to various sectors in India:

  1. Advantage to Urban Consumers: Well-off consumers in urban areas enjoy greater choice, improved quality, and lower prices for automobiles, mobile phones, televisions, processed foods, and garments, enjoying a higher standard of living.
  2. Rise of Indian Multinationals: Several top Indian companies have been able to benefit from increased competition by investing in newer technology and production methods. Some have emerged as multinational corporations themselves:
    • Tata Motors (automobiles)
    • Infosys (IT and software)
    • Ranbaxy (pharmaceuticals)
    • Asian Paints (paints)
  3. Creation of New Opportunities in Services: Globalisation has created remarkable opportunities for companies providing services, particularly in Information and Communication Technology (IT). The Indian call centre, data entry, and accounting industries have boomed due to global outsourcing.
  4. Inflow of Foreign Capital and Jobs: Massive Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) in electronics, automobiles, and telecommunications has created millions of skilled jobs.

Q11
How has globalisation created severe challenges for small producers and workers in India? Explain with examples.
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Globalisation has posed grave survival challenges for vulnerable economic groups:

  1. Crisis for Small Manufacturers: Small-scale industries employing millions of workers—producing batteries, capacitors, plastics, toys, tyres, dairy products, and vegetable oil—have been hit hard by cheap foreign imports. Unable to compete on price, many small units have shut down, rendering workers jobless.
  2. Insecurity of Employment: To compete globally and minimize costs, employers prefer to hire workers flexibly on a temporary contract basis rather than permanent jobs. Workers have no job security and can be retrenched when demand slumps.
  3. Erosion of Labour Rights and Long Hours: In export-oriented sectors like readymade garments, workers endure extremely long working hours (10–12 hours daily), night shifts, low wages, and denial of overtime compensation and statutory provident fund benefits.
  4. Vulnerability of Unorganized Labour: While competition has increased profits for MNC brand owners, workers' share in the gains of globalisation has significantly declined.

Q12
What is meant by 'Fair Globalisation'? What role can the government play to ensure that the benefits of globalisation are shared more equitably?
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Meaning of Fair Globalisation:

Fair globalisation is a system of international economic integration that creates opportunities for all citizens and ensures that the benefits of globalisation are shared equitably across all sections of society, rather than enriching only the elite.

Role of the Government in Achieving Fair Globalisation:

  1. Strict Enforcement of Labour Laws: The government must ensure that labour laws are strictly implemented and workers get their statutory rights, fair minimum wages, and social security.
  2. Support to Small Producers: Provide financial subsidies, technological upgrades, and credit to small producers until they become strong enough to compete with giant foreign MNCs.
  3. Judicious Use of Trade and Investment Barriers: If necessary, the government can use trade and investment barriers to protect vulnerable sectors from unfair dumping of cheap foreign goods.
  4. Alliances with Developing Nations at the WTO: Align with other developing countries to negotiate collectively at the WTO against the domination and agricultural subsidies of developed countries.

Q13
How has rapid advancement in Information and Communication Technology (ICT) stimulated the process of globalisation? Explain with an example of global outsourcing of services.
4 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Role of ICT in Stimulating Globalisation:

  1. Instantaneous Global Communication: Telecommunications facilities (telegraph, mobile phones, fax) and satellite communication allow people across continents to access information instantly and communicate from remote locations.
  2. The Internet Revolution: The Internet allows us to send instant electronic mail (e-mail) and voice-mail across the world at negligible costs. It enables real-time sharing of data, blueprints, accounting ledgers, and financial payments.

Concrete Example: Publishing a London News Magazine in India:

  • A news magazine published for readers in London is designed and printed in Delhi.
  • The text of the magazine is transmitted over the Internet from the London editorial office to Delhi. The designers in Delhi receive orders on how to design the magazine from the London office using telecommunication facilities.
  • The designing is done on a computer. After printing, the physical magazines are sent by air to London.
  • Even the payment of money for designing and printing from a bank in London to a bank in Delhi is done instantly through electronic funds transfer (e-banking).

Competency Case-Based Analysis
Read the following extract and answer the question:
'World Trade Organisation (WTO) is one such organisation whose aim is to liberalise international trade. Started at the initiative of the developed countries, WTO establishes rules regarding international trade, and sees that these rules are obeyed. About 164 countries of the world are currently members of the WTO. Though WTO is supposed to allow free trade for all, in practice, it is seen that the developed countries have unfairly retained trade barriers.'

Question: Why do developing nations like India argue that WTO rules are biased in favor of developed countries?

CBSE Competency Answer:

  • Hypocrisy on Farm Subsidies: While the WTO forces developing countries to stop agricultural subsidies and open their markets to foreign food grains, developed countries (US and EU) continue to give massive government subsidies (billions of dollars) to their own farmers, depressing world prices and ruining third-world farmers.
  • Unequal Bargaining Power: Developed nations use non-tariff barriers (sanitary standards, carbon border adjustments) to block goods from developing nations while forcing complete market access for Western goods.
Assertion & Reason Assessment
Assertion (A): Special Economic Zones (SEZs) have been set up in India to attract foreign companies to invest.
Reason (R): Companies that set up production units in SEZs do not have to pay taxes for an initial period of five years and are provided with world-class infrastructure and flexible labour laws.

Evaluate whether Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are correct and if (R) explains (A).

CBSE Competency Answer:

Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, and Reason (R) is the correct explanation of Assertion (A).

Explanation: To attract Foreign Direct Investment (FDI), state and central governments established SEZs equipped with uninterrupted electricity, water, roads, storage, and tax holidays for 5 years, along with relaxed labour hiring regulations.
