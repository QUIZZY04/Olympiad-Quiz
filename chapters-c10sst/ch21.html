Q1
What do you understand by globalisation? Explain the various ways in which MNCs set up, or control, production in other countries.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Meaning of Globalisation:

Globalisation is the process of rapid integration or interconnection between countries. It involves the free flow of goods, services, investments, technology, and people across national boundaries, transforming the world into a single interconnected global market.

2. Major Ways MNCs Set Up or Control Production Across Countries:

  1. Setting Up Production Jointly with Local Companies (Joint Ventures):
    • MNCs enter into partnership agreements with existing domestic companies. This provides dual benefits to local firms: MNCs bring in additional capital for modern machines and introduce advanced production technology (e.g., Ford Motors entered India in partnership with Mahindra & Mahindra).
  2. Buying Up Local Companies and Expanding Production:
    • The most common route for MNC investment is to buy up existing local companies and then expand production. MNCs with huge wealth can easily do so.
    • Example: Cargill Foods, a very large American MNC, bought over smaller Indian companies such as Parakh Foods, gaining control over large marketing networks and four oil refineries to become the largest producer of edible oil in India.
  3. Placing Orders with Small Producers (Contract Manufacturing):
    • Large MNCs in garments, footwear, and sports equipment place production orders with thousands of small producers around the world (including India, Bangladesh, and Vietnam).
    • The products are supplied to the MNCs, which then sell these under their own brand names (e.g., Nike, Zara, Adidas) to consumers, exerting tremendous power to determine price, quality, delivery, and labour conditions for distant producers.

Q2
What were the reasons for putting barriers to foreign trade and foreign investment by the Indian government after independence? Why did it wish to remove these barriers around 1991?
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The trade policy of the Indian government underwent a historic reversal from protectionism (1947–1990) to liberalisation (post-1991):

  1. Reasons for Putting Barriers After Independence (1947):
    • The Indian government, after independence, had put barriers to foreign trade and foreign investment to protect domestic infant industries from foreign competition.
    • Industries were just coming up in the 1950s and 1960s, and competition from imports at that nascent stage would not have allowed these domestic industries to survive. India allowed imports of only essential items such as machinery, fertilisers, and petroleum. All developed countries had used trade barriers during early stages of their industrialisation.
  2. Reasons for Removing Barriers in 1991 (New Economic Policy):
    • Around 1991, severe balance-of-payments crisis and foreign exchange shortages forced a re-evaluation of policy.
    • The government felt that the time had come for Indian producers to compete with producers around the globe. It was felt that competition would improve the performance and quality of goods produced by domestic manufacturers.
    • Removing barriers (trade liberalisation) allowed businesses to freely import raw materials and modern machinery, while attracting foreign direct investment (FDI) from multinational corporations.

Q3
'The impact of globalisation has not been uniform.' Explain this statement with examples of both beneficiaries and sufferers.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The impact of globalisation in India has been deeply uneven, creating major beneficiaries alongside vulnerable groups that have suffered severely:

  1. The Big Winners (Advantaged Groups):
    • Urban Affluent Consumers: Consumers have enjoyed greater choice, improved quality, and lower prices for products like automobiles, mobile phones, television, soft drinks, and packaged fast foods.
    • Top Indian Companies as MNCs: Globalisation enabled top Indian companies to upgrade technology and emerge as multinationals themselves (e.g., Tata Motors in automobiles, Infosys in IT, Ranbaxy in pharma, Asian Paints, Sundaram Fasteners).
    • IT and Service Industry Professionals: High-paying employment opportunities in software, data processing, call centers, and engineering consultancy.
  2. The Sufferers (Disadvantaged Groups):
    • Small Scale Manufacturers: Global competition hit small producers hard in industries like batteries, capacitors, plastics, toys, tyres, dairy products, and vegetable oil. Many small units were forced to shut down because they could not compete with cheap, mass-produced Chinese imports, rendering millions of workers jobless.
    • Unorganised Industrial Labour: To cut production costs in the face of fierce global competition, employers prefer to hire workers 'flexibly' on temporary contracts. Workers are denied permanent job contracts, health insurance, and paid leave, working 12–14 hour shifts with precarious incomes.

Competency Case-Based Analysis
Read the following extract and answer the question:
'World Trade Organisation (WTO) is one such organisation whose aim is to liberalise international trade. Started at the initiative of the developed countries, WTO establishes rules regarding international trade, and sees that these rules are obeyed. About 164 countries of the world are currently members of the WTO. Though WTO is supposed to allow free trade for all, in practice, it is seen that the developed countries have unfairly retained trade barriers.'

Question: Why do developing nations like India argue that WTO rules are biased in favor of developed countries?

CBSE Competency Answer:

  • Hypocrisy on Farm Subsidies: While the WTO forces developing countries to stop agricultural subsidies and open their markets to foreign food grains, developed countries (US and EU) continue to give massive government subsidies (billions of dollars) to their own farmers, depressing world prices and ruining third-world farmers.
  • Unequal Bargaining Power: Developed nations use non-tariff barriers (sanitary standards, carbon border adjustments) to block goods from developing nations while forcing complete market access for Western goods.
Assertion & Reason Assessment
Assertion (A): Special Economic Zones (SEZs) have been set up in India to attract foreign companies to invest.
Reason (R): Companies that set up production units in SEZs do not have to pay taxes for an initial period of five years and are provided with world-class infrastructure and flexible labour laws.

Evaluate whether Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are correct and if (R) explains (A).

CBSE Competency Answer:

Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, and Reason (R) is the correct explanation of Assertion (A).

Explanation: To attract Foreign Direct Investment (FDI), state and central governments established SEZs equipped with uninterrupted electricity, water, roads, storage, and tax holidays for 5 years, along with relaxed labour hiring regulations.
