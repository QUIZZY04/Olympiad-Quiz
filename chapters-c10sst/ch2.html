Q1
Explain: (a) Why growth of nationalism in the colonies is linked to an anti-colonial movement, (b) How the First World War helped in the growth of the National Movement in India, (c) Why Indians were outraged by the Rowlatt Act, (d) Why Gandhiji decided to withdraw the Non-Cooperation Movement.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(a) Why growth of nationalism in the colonies is linked to an anti-colonial movement:

  • Shared Oppression: Colonised people began discovering their unity in the process of their struggle with colonialism. The sense of being oppressed under colonial rule provided a shared bond that tied many different groups together.
  • Conflicting Experiences Unified: Although each class and group felt the effects of colonialism differently and their notions of freedom were not always the same, the common enemy (foreign British rule) united them under a common national platform forged by the Congress and Mahatma Gandhi.

(b) How the First World War helped in the growth of the National Movement in India:

  • Huge Increase in Defence Expenditure: The war created a new economic and political situation. It led to a huge increase in defence expenditure, which was financed by war loans and increasing taxes — customs duties were raised and income tax was introduced for the first time.
  • Price Rise and Extreme Hardship: Between 1913 and 1918, prices doubled, leading to extreme hardship for the common people.
  • Forced Recruitment in Rural Areas: Villages were called upon to supply soldiers, and the forced recruitment in rural areas caused widespread anger and resentment.
  • Crop Failure and Influenza Epidemic: In 1918–19 and 1920–21, crops failed in many parts of India, resulting in acute shortages of food. This was accompanied by an influenza epidemic. According to the census of 1921, 12 to 13 million people perished as a result of famines and the epidemic. When hardships did not end after the war as promised, people rallied behind the national movement.

(c) Why Indians were outraged by the Rowlatt Act (1919):

  • Hurried Enactment despite Opposition: The Rowlatt Act was hurriedly passed through the Imperial Legislative Council despite the united opposition of the Indian members.
  • Enormous Repressive Powers: It gave the British government enormous powers to repress political activities, and allowed detention of political prisoners without trial for up to two years.
  • Violation of Fundamental Liberty: It denied the fundamental legal safeguard of habeas corpus and was condemned as a 'Black Act' aimed at crushing civil liberties.

(d) Why Gandhiji decided to withdraw the Non-Cooperation Movement (1922):

  • Turn towards Violence (Chauri Chaura Incident): In February 1922, Mahatma Gandhi decided to withdraw the Non-Cooperation Movement because he felt the movement was turning violent in many places. At Chauri Chaura in Gorakhpur (Uttar Pradesh), a peaceful demonstration in a bazaar turned into a violent clash with the police, resulting in the burning of a police station and the death of 22 policemen.
  • Need for Mass Training in Satyagraha: Gandhiji realized that satyagrahis needed to be properly trained before they would be ready for genuine mass struggles, and violence gave the colonial state an excuse for ruthless armed repression.

Q2
What is meant by the idea of Satyagraha?
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The idea of Satyagraha formulated by Mahatma Gandhi is based on the following fundamental core principles:

  1. Power of Truth: The idea of Satyagraha emphasized the power of truth and the need to search for truth. It suggested that if the cause was true, if the struggle was against injustice, then physical force was not necessary to fight the oppressor.
  2. Pure Non-Violence (Ahimsa): Without being aggressive, without revenge or malice, a satyagrahi could win the battle through non-violence. This could be done by appealing directly to the conscience of the oppressor.
  3. Persuading the Oppressor: People — including the oppressors — had to be persuaded to see the truth, instead of being forced to accept it through the use of violence. By this struggle, truth was bound to ultimately triumph.
  4. Dharma of Universal Unity: Mahatma Gandhi firmly believed that this dharma of non-violence could unite all Indians regardless of caste, creed, or religion.
  5. Successful Early Applications: Gandhiji successfully organised Satyagraha movements in Champaran (Bihar, 1917) against oppressive indigo plantation system, Kheda (Gujarat, 1917) for revenue remission following crop failure, and Ahmedabad (1918) for cotton mill workers' wage demands.

Q3
Write a newspaper report on: (a) The Jallianwala Bagh massacre (b) The Simon Commission.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Newspaper Report: The Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (Amritsar, 13 April 1919)

Amritsar, 14 April 1919: Yesterday, on the holy festive day of Baisakhi, one of the most horrific atrocities in colonial history was perpetrated at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar. A large, peaceful crowd had gathered in the enclosed ground — some to protest against the government's repressive Rowlatt Act and the arrest of leaders Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr. Satyapal, while others had arrived from surrounding villages to attend the Baisakhi fair, unaware of the martial law imposed by General Dyer. Dyer entered the area with armed troops, blocked all narrow exit points, and without giving any warning, ordered his men to open fire on the trapped, unarmed crowd. Hundreds of innocent men, women, and children were butchered on the spot, and over a thousand were severely injured. General Dyer later declared brazenly that his objective was to 'produce a moral effect' and strike terror into the hearts of satyagrahis. Widespread strikes, clashes with police, and government bombings followed across Punjab, leading Rabindranath Tagore to renounce his British Knighthood in sheer protest.

2. Newspaper Report: Protests against the Simon Commission (Bombay/Lahore, 1928)

Lahore, 31 October 1928: Massive nationwide protests erupted across Indian cities as the Indian Statutory Commission, headed by Sir John Simon, arrived in India. Set up by the British Conservative government to review the working of the constitutional system and suggest changes, the Commission has provoked absolute fury across all political spectrums — including the Indian National Congress and the Muslim League. The central reason for public outrage is that the Commission does not contain a single Indian member; all seven members are British politicians. Wherever the Commission arrived, it was greeted with black-flag demonstrations, mass hartals, and loud chants of 'Simon Go Back'. During a peaceful demonstration in Lahore led by veteran nationalist leader Lala Lajpat Rai, police under Superintendent Scott unleashed a brutal lathi-charge. Lala Lajpat Rai sustained grave chest injuries and subsequently succumbed, declaring that 'every blow aimed at me is a nail in the coffin of British imperialism'.

Q5
List all the different social groups that joined the Non-Cooperation Movement of 1921. Then choose any three and write about their hopes and struggles to show why they joined the movement.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Social Groups Participating in the Non-Cooperation Movement (1921):

Middle-class citizens in towns (students, teachers, lawyers), peasants in Awadh, tribal peasants in the Gudem Hills of Andhra Pradesh, and plantation workers in Assam.

2. Detailed Analysis of Three Chosen Social Groups:

  • (a) Peasants of Awadh (Led by Baba Ramchandra):
    • Hopes and Grievances: The movement was against talukdars and landlords who demanded exorbitantly high rents and a variety of other cesses. Peasants had to do begar (unpaid forced labour) and work at landlords’ farms without any payment. As tenants, they had no security of tenure and were regularly evicted.
    • Struggle & Action: The peasant movement demanded reduction of revenue, abolition of begar, and social boycott of oppressive landlords. In many places, nai-dhobi bandhs were organised. In 1920, the Oudh Kisan Sabha was set up headed by Jawaharlal Nehru and Baba Ramchandra. However, as the movement spread in 1921, the houses of talukdars and merchants were attacked, bazaars were looted, and grain hoards were taken over, which alarmed the Congress leadership.
  • (b) Tribal Peasants of Gudem Hills, Andhra Pradesh (Led by Alluri Sitaram Raju):
    • Hopes and Grievances: In the Gudem Hills, a militant guerrilla movement spread. The colonial government had closed large forest areas, preventing people from entering the forests to graze cattle or collect fuelwood and fruits. This enraged the hill people, affecting their livelihoods and traditional rights. When the government began forcing them to contribute begar for road building, they revolted.
    • Struggle & Action: Their leader, Alluri Sitaram Raju, claimed special powers (astrological predictions, healing, surviving bullet shots). Inspired by Gandhiji, he persuaded people to wear khadi and give up drinking. But he asserted that India could be liberated only by the use of force, not non-violence. The Gudem rebels attacked police stations, attempted to kill British officials, and carried on guerrilla warfare until Raju was captured and executed in 1924.
  • (c) Plantation Workers in Assam:
    • Hopes and Grievances: Under the Inland Emigration Act of 1859, plantation workers were not permitted to leave tea gardens without permission, and in fact they were rarely given such permission. For workers, freedom meant the right to move freely in and out of the confined spaces in which they were enclosed, and retaining a link with their native villages.
    • Struggle & Action: When they heard of the Non-Cooperation Movement, thousands of workers defied the authorities, left the plantations, and headed home. They believed that 'Gandhi Raj' was coming and everyone would be given land in their own villages. Tragically, stranded on the way due to railway and steamer strikes, they were caught by the police and brutally beaten up.

Q6
Discuss the Salt March to make clear why it was an effective symbol of resistance against colonialism.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Mahatma Gandhi found in salt a remarkably powerful and effective symbol that could unite the entire nation against colonial exploitation:

  1. Universal Consumption Across All Classes: Salt was an essential item of food consumed equally by the rich and the poor alike. The tax on salt and the government monopoly over its production revealed the most oppressive face of British rule.
  2. Ultimatum to Viceroy Irwin: On 31 January 1930, Gandhiji sent a letter to Viceroy Irwin stating eleven demands, the most stirring of which was the demand to abolish the salt tax. When Irwin was unwilling to negotiate, Gandhiji launched the historic March.
  3. The Dandi March (12 March – 6 April 1930): Accompanied by 78 of his trusted volunteers, Gandhiji walked 240 miles from his ashram in Sabarmati to the Gujarati coastal town of Dandi over 24 days (about 10 miles a day). Thousands came to hear Gandhiji wherever he stopped.
  4. Defying Colonial Law: On 6 April 1930, Gandhiji reached Dandi, ritually manufactured salt by boiling sea water, and formally broke the colonial salt law. This marked the dramatic beginning of the Civil Disobedience Movement.
  5. Mobilisation of Entire Society: Breaking the salt law inspired millions across India to manufacture salt in front of government salt factories, boycott foreign cloth, picket liquor shops, refuse to pay chaukidari taxes, and violate forest laws. It transformed the national struggle into an active mass defiance of unjust imperial laws.

Q7
Imagine you are a woman participating in the Civil Disobedience Movement. Explain what the experience meant to your life.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Perspective of a Woman Participant in the Civil Disobedience Movement:

  • Breaking Out of Domestic Seclusion: Participating in the Civil Disobedience Movement was a transformative milestone in my life. Before Gandhiji's call, society expected women to remain confined within the four walls of the home, limited to child-rearing and domestic chores.
  • Answering Gandhiji's Call: When Gandhiji marched to Dandi and called upon women to serve the nation, thousands of us came out of our homes to listen to him. We saw service to the nation as a sacred religious duty (dharma) of women.
  • Active Ground-Level Participation: In urban areas, we women from high-caste families, and in rural areas, women from rich peasant households, participated in protest marches, manufactured salt, and courageously picketed foreign cloth and liquor shops. Many of us were baton-charged by the police and willingly went to jail.
  • New Sense of Self-Worth and Dignity: This experience instilled immense self-confidence and political awareness in us. Although the Congress leadership was initially reluctant to grant women positions of authority within the organisation and viewed our role primarily as symbolic, our active participation permanently changed the public perception of women's strength and role in India's freedom struggle.

Q8
Why did political leaders differ sharply over the question of separate electorates?
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Political leaders differed sharply over the question of separate electorates because of conflicting visions of community representation and national unity:

  1. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar and Dalit Representation:
    • Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, who organised the Dalits into the Depressed Classes Association in 1930, clashed with Mahatma Gandhi at the second Round Table Conference by demanding separate electorates for Dalits.
    • Ambedkar argued that political empowerment was the only genuine path to resolve the social backwardness, untouchability, and historical oppression of Dalits.
  2. Mahatma Gandhi’s Opposition and Fast unto Death:
    • Gandhiji vehemently opposed separate electorates for Dalits, arguing that it would permanently segregate the Dalits from mainstream Hindu society and fatally disrupt national integration.
    • When the British government conceded Ambedkar’s demand under the Communal Award, Gandhiji began a fast unto death in Yerwada Jail (Pune).
    • This resulted in the historic Poona Pact (September 1932), where Ambedkar accepted joint electorates in exchange for reserved seats for the Depressed Classes in provincial and central legislative councils.
  3. Muhammad Ali Jinnah and Muslim League Demands:
    • Muhammad Ali Jinnah and the Muslim League insisted on separate electorates or reserved seats in proportion to population in Muslim-majority provinces (Bengal and Punjab) and one-third representation in the Central Assembly.
    • They feared that under a joint electorate with a Hindu majority, the culture, identity, and political voice of the Muslim minority would be submerged.
    • Negotiations broke down completely in 1928 when M.R. Jayakar of the Hindu Mahasabha strongly opposed any compromise on the issue.

Competency Source-Based Analysis
Read the following extract and answer the question:
'It is said of 'passive resistance' that it is the weapon of the weak, but the power which is the subject of this article can be used only by the strong. This power is not passive resistance; indeed it calls for intense activity. The movement in South Africa was not passive, but active... Satyagraha is not physical force. A satyagrah does not inflict pain on the adversary; he does not seek his destruction... In the use of satyagraha, there is no ill-will whatever.'

Question: How does Mahatma Gandhi distinguish Satyagraha from passive resistance in this text?

CBSE Competency Answer:

  • Active Soul-Force, Not Passive Submission: Passive resistance is often considered a defensive weapon of the physically weak, whereas Satyagraha is the weapon of the morally strong, requiring immense courage, active resistance, and unyielding truth-force.
  • Total Renunciation of Malice: Unlike passive resistance, which may harbor latent hatred or use violence if opportunity permits, Satyagraha is rooted purely in soul-force and love. It seeks to convert the oppressor through voluntary self-suffering without causing any injury or ill-will to the adversary.
Assertion & Reason Assessment
Assertion (A): The Simon Commission was greeted with the slogan 'Simon Go Back' when it arrived in India in 1928.
Reason (R): The Commission did not have a single Indian member; all members were British.

Evaluate whether Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are correct and if (R) explains (A).

CBSE Competency Answer:

Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, and Reason (R) is the correct explanation of Assertion (A).

Explanation: The British government appointed the statutory commission under Sir John Simon to review the Indian constitutional system, but deliberately excluded all Indians. Indian leaders felt deeply insulted that foreigners were deciding India's constitutional destiny, prompting unanimous nationwide protests and black-flag demonstrations.
