Q1
Answer the following in about 30 words: (i) Explain how water becomes a renewable resource, (ii) What is water scarcity and what are its main causes? (iii) Compare the advantages and disadvantages of multi-purpose river projects.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(i) How Water Becomes a Renewable Resource:

  • Water is a renewable resource because it is continuously recycled, purified, and recharged through the hydrological cycle.
  • Water evaporates from oceans and water bodies due to solar heat, condenses to form clouds in the atmosphere, and precipitates back to Earth as rain and snow. It flows through rivers, infiltrates underground aquifers, and returns to the oceans, maintaining a continuous perpetual cycle.

(ii) What is Water Scarcity and What are its Main Causes?

  • Water Scarcity: A situation where water availability is insufficient to meet the basic drinking, sanitation, and economic demands of a region's population.
  • Main Causes:
    1. Rapid Population Growth: Growing population requires more water for domestic use and to produce more food grains.
    2. Expansion of Irrigated Agriculture: Water-intensive cash crops and tube-wells have caused catastrophic depletion of underground aquifers.
    3. Industrialisation and Urbanisation: Industries are heavy consumers of water and hydroelectric power, while urban housing complexes draw massive quantities of groundwater using personal submersible pumps.
    4. Water Pollution: Even where water is amply available, it is often contaminated by domestic and industrial wastes, chemicals, pesticides, and fertilisers, making it hazardous for human use.

(iii) Compare the Advantages and Disadvantages of Multi-purpose River Projects:

Advantages (Merits) Disadvantages (Demerits)
1. Generation of clean, renewable hydroelectric power. 1. Regulating and damming rivers affects natural flow, causing excessive sedimentation at the reservoir bottom.
2. Irrigation facilities to drought-prone agricultural regions. 2. Submergence of fertile agricultural lands and valuable natural forest ecosystems.
3. Flood control during monsoon surges. 3. Large-scale displacement of local indigenous populations without proper rehabilitation.
4. Inland navigation and recreational fisheries. 4. Induces seismicity (earthquakes) and triggers water-borne diseases and pests.

Q2
Discuss how rainwater harvesting in semi-arid regions of Rajasthan is carried out.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

In the semi-arid and arid regions of Rajasthan (particularly Bikaner, Phalodi, and Barmer), rainwater harvesting was traditionally carried out through an ingenious underground structure known as a 'Tanka':

  1. Structure and Capacity: The tankas were large underground tanks built inside the main house or the courtyard. They could be as large as a big room (e.g., a tanka in Phalodi was 6.1 metres deep, 4.27 metres long, and 2.44 metres wide).
  2. Rooftop Catchment System: The tankas were part of the well-developed rooftop rainwater harvesting system. Sloping roofs of the houses were connected to the underground tanka through a pipe. Rain falling on the rooftops would travel down the pipe and get stored in the underground tankas.
  3. Filtration of First Rain: The first spell of rain was usually not collected as this would clean the roof and the pipes. The rainwater from subsequent showers was then collected.
  4. Reliable Year-round Drinking Water: The water collected in these tankas was called Palar Pani, considered the purest form of natural water. It was sealed and kept cool underground, providing sweet drinking water through the scorching summer months when all other sources dried up.
  5. Cooling the Household: Many houses built rooms adjoining the tanka to beat the desert summer heat, as it kept the room pleasantly cool.

Q3
Describe how modern adaptations of traditional rainwater harvesting methods are being carried out to conserve and store water.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Modern adaptations of traditional rainwater harvesting are being actively implemented across urban and rural India to combat water crises:

  1. Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting in Urban Areas: Modern residential colonies, apartment complexes, and schools install PVC pipes connecting rooftop runoff directly through a filtration pit (filled with sand and gravel) into underground storage tanks or recharge borewells, directly replenishing the city's water table.
  2. Tamil Nadu Model (Mandatory Legal Framework): Tamil Nadu is the first state in India which has made rooftop rainwater harvesting structure compulsory to all houses across the state. There are legal provisions to punish the defaulters. This has resulted in a spectacular recharge of groundwater levels across Chennai and rural districts.
  3. Gendathur Village, Karnataka (Rural Success Story): In Gendathur, a remote backward village in Mysuru, Karnataka, villagers installed rooftop rainwater harvesting systems in nearly 200 households. With an annual precipitation of 1,000 mm and 80% collection efficiency, the village harvests nearly 1,00,000 litres of water annually per household, earning the distinction of being rich in rainwater.
  4. Modern Check Dams and Johads in Rural Watersheds: Traditional Johads and Khadins in Rajasthan have been revived by NGOs (like Tarun Bharat Sangh headed by Rajendra Singh) using modern engineering to check soil erosion and recharge dry borewells.
  5. Bamboo Drip Irrigation in Meghalaya: In Meghalaya, a 200-year-old system of tapping stream and spring water by using bamboo pipes is still adapted to irrigate betel leaf crops on steep slopes without water wastage.

Competency Case-Based Analysis
Read the following extract and answer the question:
'Jawaharlal Nehru proudly proclaimed the dams as the 'temples of modern India'; the reason being that it would integrate development of agriculture and the village economy with rapid industrialisation and growth of the urban economy. However, in recent years, multi-purpose projects and large dams have come under great scrutiny and opposition from environmental and human rights groups.'

Question: Why have social movements like 'Narmada Bachao Andolan' and 'Tehri Dam Andolan' emerged against multi-purpose river projects?

CBSE Competency Answer:

  • Massive Involuntary Displacement: Building large dams submerges vast areas of ancestral land and forests, forcibly uprooting tribal and rural communities from their livelihoods without adequate rehabilitation.
  • Ecological Destruction and Siltation: Damming disrupts natural river ecology, prevents fish migration, causes excessive reservoir sedimentation, waterlogging, and increases the salinity of surrounding agricultural soil.
Assertion & Reason Assessment
Assertion (A): Rainwater, or 'Palar Pani', as commonly known in parts of Rajasthan, is considered the purest form of natural water.
Reason (R): Rainwater collected directly from clean rooftops and stored in dark, underground, sealed tankas is completely protected from sunlight, air pollutants, and bacterial decay.

Evaluate whether Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are correct and if (R) explains (A).

CBSE Competency Answer:

Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, and Reason (R) is the correct explanation of Assertion (A).

Explanation: The traditional wisdom of building underground tankas in western Rajasthan ensured that rainwater remained clean, sweet, and uncontaminated for months, serving as an indispensable lifeline during harsh desert summers.
