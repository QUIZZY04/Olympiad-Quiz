Q1
What are the different forms of power sharing in modern democracies? Give an example of each of these.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

In modern democracies, power sharing arrangements take many different institutional forms:

  1. Horizontal Distribution of Power (Among Organs of Government):
    • Power is shared among different organs of government, such as the Legislature, Executive, and Judiciary.
    • It is called horizontal because it allows different organs placed at the same level to exercise different powers. Such a separation ensures that none of the organs can exercise unlimited power. Each organ checks the others, resulting in a system of checks and balances (e.g., judges appointed by the executive can check the functioning of executive or laws made by the legislature).
  2. Vertical Distribution of Power (Federal Division at Different Levels):
    • Power is shared among governments at different levels — a general government for the entire country (Central or Union Government) and governments at the provincial or sub-national level (State Governments).
    • In India, this is further extended downwards to local self-governments (Panchayats and Municipalities). It represents a vertical division from higher to lower tiers of governance.
  3. Power Sharing among Different Social and Linguistic Groups:
    • Power may also be shared among different social groups, such as the religious and linguistic groups.
    • Example: Community Government in Belgium, elected by people belonging to one language community (Dutch, French, or German-speaking) regardless of where they live, with power regarding cultural, educational, and language-related issues. In India, the system of 'reserved constituencies' in assemblies and parliament for SCs, STs, and women is another prime example.
  4. Power Sharing among Political Parties, Pressure Groups and Movements:
    • In a democracy, citizens must have freedom to choose among various contenders for power. Competition among different political parties ensures that power does not remain in one hand.
    • In contemporary politics, this takes the form of coalition governments, where two or more parties form an alliance to contest elections and share power if elected. Furthermore, interest groups (traders, industrialists, farmers, workers) share power through participation in governmental committees or bringing influence on decision-making.

Q2
State one prudential reason and one moral reason for power sharing with an example from the Indian context.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Both prudential and moral reasons support the vital need for power sharing in a democracy:

  1. Prudential Reasons (Calculated Benefits and Risk Avoidance):
    • Concept: Power sharing is desirable because it helps to reduce the possibility of conflict between social groups. Since social conflict often leads to violence and political instability, power sharing is a good way to ensure the stability of political order. Imposing the will of the majority community over others may look like an attractive option in the short run, but in the long run, it undermines the unity of the nation. Tyranny of the majority is not just oppressive for the minority; it often brings ruin to the majority as well.
    • Indian Example: Reservation of seats in Lok Sabha and state assemblies for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes ensures that marginalized communities have a fair share in state power without resorting to violent alienation.
  2. Moral Reasons (The Very Spirit of Democracy):
    • Concept: Power sharing is the very spirit of democracy. A democratic rule involves sharing power with those affected by its exercise, and who have to live with its effects. People have a legitimate right to be consulted on how they are to be governed. A legitimate government is one where citizens, through participation, acquire a stake in the system. While prudential reasons stress that power sharing will bring out better outcomes, moral reasons emphasize the very act of power sharing as valuable.
    • Indian Example: Decentralisation of governance through the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments (1992), granting constitutionally protected power to rural Panchayats and urban Municipalities where common citizens participate directly in local decision-making.

Q3
Compare the ways in which the Belgian and Sri Lankan governments solved the problem of cultural diversity.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Comparison of Belgium and Sri Lanka in handling Cultural Diversity:

Feature Belgium (Accommodation Model) Sri Lanka (Majoritarian Model)
Ethnic Composition 59% Dutch-speakers (Flemish region), 40% French-speakers (Wallonia), 1% German-speakers. In Brussels capital: 80% French, 20% Dutch. 74% Sinhala-speakers (mostly Buddhist), 18% Tamil-speakers (13% Sri Lankan Tamils, 5% Indian Tamils — Hindu and Muslim).
Government Policy Approach Accommodation: Between 1970 and 1993, the constitution was amended four times to work out an arrangement that would enable everyone to live together peacefully. Majoritarianism: The democratically elected government adopted a series of majoritarian measures to establish Sinhala supremacy (1956 Act).
Key Institutional Measures 1. Equal number of Dutch and French ministers in Central Government.
2. Many powers of central govt given to state governments.
3. Separate capital government for Brussels with equal representation.
4. Unique 'Community Government' for cultural/linguistic issues.		 1. 1956 Act made Sinhala the sole official language, disregarding Tamil.
2. Preferential policies favored Sinhala applicants for university jobs.
3. State constitution mandated protecting and fostering Buddhism.
Ultimate Outcome Avoided civil strife, preserved national unity, and Brussels was chosen as the headquarters of the European Union. Alienated Tamils, spawned LTTE militant struggle for Tamil Eelam, and plunged the country into a catastrophic decades-long civil war.

Competency Case-Based Analysis
Read the following extract and answer the question:
'In 1956, an Act was passed in Sri Lanka to recognise Sinhala as the only official language, thus disregarding Tamil. The governments followed preferential policies that favoured Sinhala applicants for university positions and government jobs. A new constitution stipulated that the state shall foster Buddhism. All these government measures, coming one after the other, gradually increased the feeling of alienation among the Sri Lankan Tamils.'

Question: What were the major grievances of Sri Lankan Tamils that ultimately led to the civil war?

CBSE Competency Answer:

  • Denial of Linguistic & Cultural Rights: The 1956 Act made Sinhala the only official language, denying official status to Tamil and eroding their cultural identity.
  • Systemic Discrimination in Jobs and Education: Preferential state policies systematically excluded Tamil youth from government employment and top university admissions.
  • Religious Marginalisation: State patronage to Buddhism alienated Hindu, Muslim, and Christian Tamils, leading to demands for an independent state ('Tamil Eelam') and armed conflict.
Assertion & Reason Assessment
Assertion (A): Power sharing is good for democracies.
Reason (R): It helps to reduce the possibility of conflict between social groups and ensures the political stability of the nation.

Evaluate whether Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are correct and if (R) explains (A).

CBSE Competency Answer:

Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, and Reason (R) is the correct explanation of Assertion (A).

Explanation: Power sharing prevents the concentration of power in any single hand or social group. By accommodating diverse social interests, it averts mutual hostility and civil strife, forming the core prudential and moral justification of democratic governance.
