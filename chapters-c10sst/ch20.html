Q1
How does money solve the problem of double coincidence of wants? Explain with an example of your own.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Meaning of Double Coincidence of Wants:

In a barter system where goods are directly exchanged without the use of money, double coincidence of wants is an essential condition: what a person desires to sell must be exactly what the other person wishes to buy. Both parties have to agree to sell and buy each other’s commodities.

2. How Money Solves the Problem:

  • Money eliminates the cumbersome need for double coincidence of wants by acting as an intermediate in the exchange process, known as a medium of exchange.
  • Original Example: Suppose a shoe manufacturer wants to sell shoes in the market and buy wheat. Under a barter system, the shoe manufacturer would have to find a wheat-growing farmer who not only wants to sell wheat, but also simultaneously wants to buy shoes. Finding such an exact match is extremely difficult.
  • With money, the shoe manufacturer simply sells shoes for rupees in the open market and then uses that money to purchase wheat from any farmer or grocer selling wheat. The transaction is split into two independent, effortless steps.

Q2
In situations with high risks, credit might create further problems for the borrower. Explain with the help of an example.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

In high-risk situations (particularly small-scale crop cultivation), credit can push the borrower into a devastating debt trap instead of improving their earnings:

  1. Case Analysis of a Small Farmer (Swapna):
    • Swapna, a small farmer, borrows money from a local moneylender at high interest rates to meet the expenses of cultivating groundnuts on her 3 acres of land, hoping that her harvest would repay the loan.
    • Midway through the season, pests attack the crop and the crop fails despite spraying expensive pesticides. She cannot repay the moneylender, and the debt grows over the year into a large amount.
    • Next year, she takes a fresh loan for cultivation. It is a normal crop this year, but the earnings are not enough to cover the old debt plus the new loan.
    • She is caught in a debt trap. To clear the loan, she is forced to sell off a portion of her valuable ancestral farmland.
  2. Structural Vulnerability: In crop production, the risk of failure is high due to unpredictable monsoon rains, pest attacks, or price crashes in the mandi. If crops fail, credit turns from an instrument of growth into an instrument of financial ruin, leaving the borrower in a far worse situation than before.

Q3
In what ways does the Reserve Bank of India supervise the functioning of banks? Why is this necessary?
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) plays a crucial supervisory role over the formal banking sector in India:

  1. Maintenance of Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR): The RBI monitors that commercial banks actually keep a minimum cash balance out of the deposits they receive. In India, banks currently maintain about 4.5% to 15% of their deposits as cash reserve to cater to daily withdrawal demands of depositors.
  2. Ensuring Credit Delivery to Priority Sectors: The RBI sees that banks give loans not just to profit-making businesses, large traders, and corporate houses, but also to small cultivators, small-scale industries, small borrowers, and SHGs. This is enforced through Priority Sector Lending (PSL) norms.
  3. Monitoring Interest Rates and Terms: Periodically, banks have to submit information to the RBI on how much they are lending, to whom, and at what interest rates, ensuring that commercial banks do not exploit vulnerable citizens with usurious terms.
  4. Why Supervision is Indispensable:
    • Without RBI supervision, commercial banks would operate solely for private profit maximization, lending exclusively to rich corporate entities in cities and neglecting poor rural farmers.
    • It prevents bank failures, safeguards public deposits, maintains confidence in the financial system, and promotes balanced regional development.

Q4
What is the basic idea behind the Self-Help Groups (SHGs) for the poor? Explain in your own words.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Self-Help Groups (SHGs) have emerged as an extraordinary institutional innovation to empower poor women and overcome the barriers of formal bank credit:

  1. Basic Structure: A typical SHG consists of 15 to 20 members, usually belonging to one neighbourhood, who meet and save money regularly. Saving per member varies from ₹25 to ₹100 or more, depending on the ability to save.
  2. Small Internal Loans without Collateral: Members can take small loans from the group itself to meet their immediate emergency needs (buying seeds, fertilizers, school fees, releasing mortgaged land). The group charges interest on these loans, but this is far lower than what informal moneylenders charge.
  3. Bank Linkage and Collective Guarantee: After a year or two, if the group is regular in savings, it becomes eligible for availing bank loans. The loan is sanctioned in the name of the group to create self-employment opportunities (buying sewing machines, cattle, handlooms). The formal bank lends to poor women without individual collateral because the entire SHG provides collective guarantee; any non-repayment is followed up rigorously by other members.
  4. Financial Independence for Women: It frees poor rural women from the clutches of predatory moneylenders, enabling them to build assets and generate steady livelihoods.
  5. Platform for Social Empowerment: The regular meetings of the SHG provide an active platform to discuss and act on a variety of social issues like health, nutrition, child marriage, and domestic violence.

Competency Case-Based Analysis
Read the following extract and answer the question:
'Every loan agreement specifies an interest rate which the borrower must pay to the lender along with the repayment of the principal. In addition, lenders may demand collateral (security) against loans. Collateral is an asset that the borrower owns (such as land, building, vehicle, livestock, deposits with banks) and uses this as a guarantee to a lender until the loan is repaid.'

Question: Why do formal commercial banks insist on collateral before granting loans, and how does this requirement exclude poor borrowers?

CBSE Competency Answer:

  • Risk Mitigation for Banks: Collateral serves as a legal guarantee; if the borrower defaults, the lender has the right to sell the asset to recover loan dues.
  • Exclusion of the Poor: Poor rural labourers and small artisans lack land deeds, permanent houses, or bank deposits to pledge as collateral. Consequently, formal banks refuse them loans, forcing them to borrow from exploitative local moneylenders at exorbitant interest rates (36%–60% per annum).
Assertion & Reason Assessment
Assertion (A): Cheap and affordable credit is crucial for a country's economic development.
Reason (R): High interest rates charged by informal lenders mean that a large part of the earnings of the borrowers is spent to repay the loan, leaving them with little income for reinvestment and growth.

Evaluate whether Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are correct and if (R) explains (A).

CBSE Competency Answer:

Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, and Reason (R) is the correct explanation of Assertion (A).

Explanation: When farmers and small entrepreneurs have access to low-interest formal loans, they can invest in seeds, machinery, and business expansion, generating profits, higher employment, and accelerated national economic development.
