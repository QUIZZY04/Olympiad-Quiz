Q1
Give reasons for the following: (a) Woodblock print only came to Europe after 1295, (b) Martin Luther was in favour of print and spoke out in praise of it, (c) The Roman Catholic Church began keeping an Index of Prohibited books from the mid-sixteenth century, (d) Gandhi said the fight for Swaraj is a fight for liberty of speech, liberty of the press, and freedom of association.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(a) Woodblock print only came to Europe after 1295:

  • Woodblock printing was developed in China in the sixth century. In 1295, the great Italian explorer Marco Polo returned to Italy after many years of exploration in China.
  • Marco Polo brought this knowledge of woodblock printing back with him. Italians began producing books with woodblocks, and soon the technology spread to other parts of Europe.

(b) Martin Luther was in favour of print and spoke out in praise of it:

  • In 1517, the religious reformer Martin Luther wrote Ninety Five Theses criticizing many of the practices and rituals of the Roman Catholic Church. A printed copy of this was pasted on a church door in Wittenberg.
  • Luther's writings were immediately reproduced in vast numbers and read widely. This led to a division within the Church and to the beginning of the Protestant Reformation. Luther’s translation of the New Testament sold 5,000 copies within a few weeks. Deeply moved, Luther declared: 'Print is the ultimate gift of God and the greatest one.'

(c) The Roman Catholic Church began keeping an Index of Prohibited Books from the mid-sixteenth century:

  • Print and popular religious literature stimulated distinctive individual interpretations of faith even among little-educated people. In Italy, a miller named Menocchio read books available in his locality, reinterpreted the message of the Bible, and formulated a view of God and Creation that enraged the Catholic Church.
  • When the Roman Church began its Inquisitorial campaign to suppress heretical ideas, Menocchio was hauled up twice and ultimately executed. Troubled by such effects of popular readings and questionings of faith, the Roman Catholic Church imposed severe controls over publishers and booksellers and, from 1558, began maintaining an Index of Prohibited Books.

(d) Gandhi said the fight for Swaraj is a fight for liberty of speech, liberty of the press, and freedom of association:

  • Mahatma Gandhi believed that liberty of speech, liberty of the press, and freedom of association were the three most vital instruments of cultivating and mobilizing public opinion.
  • Colonial rulers sought to stifle these freedoms through censorship acts (such as the Vernacular Press Act) to deny Indians the right to criticize foreign exploitation. Gandhi argued that without these basic democratic liberties, no nation could assert self-determination or attain genuine Swaraj.

Q2
Write short notes on: (a) The Gutenberg Press, (b) Erasmus's idea of the printed book, (c) The Vernacular Press Act.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(a) The Gutenberg Press:

  • Inventor and Inspiration: Johannes Gutenberg developed the first known printing press at Strasbourg, Germany, in the 1430s. Drawing on his knowledge of wine presses, olive presses, and metal goldsmithing, he created metal types for each letter of the alphabet.
  • First Printed Book: By 1448, Gutenberg perfected the movable type system. The first book he printed was the Bible. About 180 copies were printed, taking three years — which was remarkably fast for that era.

(b) Erasmus’s Idea of the Printed Book:

  • Latin Scholar and Catholic Reformer: Erasmus of Rotterdam expressed deep anxiety about the unregulated avalanche of printed books.
  • Critique of Bad Books: In his book Adages (1508), he wrote that the world was being swamped with books that were ignorant, scandalous, irreligious, and seditious. He feared that the sheer volume of worthless publications eroded the value of valuable scholarship.

(c) The Vernacular Press Act (1878):

  • Modelled on Irish Press Laws: Passed by Lord Lytton's administration in 1878, it gave the colonial government extensive rights to censor reports and editorials in the vernacular press.
  • Suppression of Nationalist Voice: When a report was judged seditious, the newspaper was warned, and if the warning was ignored, the press was liable to be seized and its printing machinery confiscated. Despite this, nationalist newspapers grew in numbers across India.

Q3
What did the spread of print culture in nineteenth-century India mean to: (a) Women, (b) The poor, (c) Reformers?
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The spread of print culture in nineteenth-century India profoundly impacted different marginalized segments of society:

  1. Impact on Women:
    • Women's reading increased enormously in middle-class homes. Liberal husbands and fathers began educating women at home and sent them to schools.
    • Women authors emerged: Rashsundari Debi in East Bengal secretly learned to read in the kitchen and wrote the first full-length autobiography in Bengali, Amar Jiban (1876). In Maharashtra, Kailashbashini Debi, Tarabai Shinde, and Pandita Ramabai wrote with passionate anger about the miserable plight of upper-caste Hindu widows, domestic oppression, and denial of education.
  2. Impact on the Poor:
    • Very cheap small books were brought to markets in nineteenth-century Madras towns and sold at crossroads, allowing poor people travelling to markets to buy them.
    • Public libraries were set up from the early twentieth century, expanding access to books. Mill workers in Kanpur, Bombay, and Calcutta began writing and reading: Kashibaba, a Kanpur millworker, wrote Chhote Aur Bade Ka Sawal (1938) to expose links between caste and class exploitation.
  3. Impact on Reformers:
    • Print became the primary vehicle for social and religious debate. Reformers used newspapers and tracts to attack orthodox practices like sati, child marriage, caste hierarchy, and idolatry.
    • Raja Rammohun Roy published the Sambad Kaumudi from 1821, and the Hindu orthodoxy commissioned the Samachar Chandrika to oppose his ideas. Similarly, Urdu translations of scriptures and fatwas were printed in thousands by institutions like the Deoband Seminary to counter colonial Christian missionary influences.

Q4
Why did some people in eighteenth-century Europe think that print culture would bring enlightenment and end despotism?
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

By the mid-eighteenth century, there was a common conviction that books were a means of spreading progress and enlightenment, as expressed by the French novelist Louis-Sébastien Mercier:

  1. Spreading Reason and Rational Thinking: The writings of philosophers like Voltaire, Rousseau, and Montesquieu were widely printed and read. Their literature questioned traditional authority, religious dogma, and custom, arguing that everything ought to be judged through reason and rationality.
  2. Eroding the Legitimacy of Absolutist Monarchy: Print popularised the critical thinking that stripped the monarchy of its sacred aura. Cartoons and caricatures portrayed the monarchy as indulging in sensual pleasures while the common people suffered hardships, undermining public respect for despotism.
  3. Creation of a Public Sphere of Debate: Print created a new culture of dialogue and debate. All values, norms, and institutions were re-evaluated by a reading public that had become aware of the power of reason.
  4. Mercier’s Proclamation: Louis-Sébastien Mercier proclaimed: 'The printing press is the most powerful engine of progress and public opinion is the force that will sweep despotism away.' In his novels, heroes are transformed by reading books. Mercier declared: 'Tremble, therefore, tyrants of the world! Tremble before the virtual writer!'

Q5
Why did some people fear the effect of easily available printed books? Choose one example from Europe and one from India.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Many religious authorities, monarchs, and orthodox elites feared the spread of printed books because they worried that without control over what was published, rebellious and irreligious thoughts would gain currency:

  1. Example from Europe (Menocchio and the Roman Catholic Church):
    • In sixteenth-century Italy, a miller named Menocchio began reading books available in his locality. He reinterpreted the Bible and developed a theology that questioned the authority of the Catholic Church.
    • The Inquisition condemned him as a heretic and executed him. The Roman Church feared that print empowered commoners to question sacred scriptures, undermine clerical authority, and destroy the traditional social hierarchy, leading them to create the Index of Prohibited Books in 1558.
  2. Example from India (Orthodox Hindu and Muslim Backlash):
    • In India, conservative Hindus feared that educated women would be widowed, and conservative Muslims feared that educated women would be corrupted by reading romantic Urdu novels.
    • Orthodox Hindus worried that print would spread lower-caste ideas and challenge Brahminical dominance. Similarly, Islamic scholars (Ulama) feared that colonial rule would collapse Muslim personal laws, using lithographic presses to print thousands of fatwas telling Muslims how to conduct their lives.

Q6
Explain how print culture assisted the growth of nationalism in India.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Print culture was the primary catalyst in the growth of Indian nationalism during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries:

  1. Exposing Colonial Exploitation: Despite repressive censorship laws, nationalist newspapers grew in all parts of India. They reported on colonial misrule, economic drainage, famine mismanagement, and racial arrogance, encouraging people to unite.
  2. Connecting Communities Across India: Print connected Indians living in different linguistic regions. A Gujarati paper discussed events in Bengal; a Tamil paper debated issues in Punjab. It forged a pan-Indian national consciousness.
  3. Propagating Nationalist Ideas: Prominent leaders published newspapers — Bal Gangadhar Tilak edited Kesari (Marathi) and Mahratta (English), while Gandhiji edited Young India and Harijan. Tilak’s reportage on the deportation of Punjab nationalists in 1907 led to his imprisonment in 1908, provoking widespread strikes across India.
  4. Revival of National Literature and Visuals: Nationalist songs like Bankim Chandra’s Vande Mataram, folk tales, and visual prints of Bharat Mata were printed and circulated by millions, giving Indians a profound sense of pride in their heritage.

Competency Case-Based Analysis
Read the following extract and answer the question:
'From the late nineteenth century, issues of caste discrimination began to be written about in many printed tracts and essays. Jyotirao Phule, the Maratha pioneer of 'low caste' protest movements, wrote about the injustices of the caste system in his Gulamgiri (1871). In the twentieth century, B.R. Ambedkar in Maharashtra and E.V. Ramaswamy Naicker in Madras, better known as Periyar, wrote powerfully on caste and their writings were read by people all over India.'

Question: How did print empower anti-caste movements in India?

CBSE Competency Answer:

  • Democratising Knowledge: Historically, lower castes were forbidden from reading sacred texts. Print demolished upper-caste monopolies over knowledge by producing accessible, inexpensive books in vernacular languages.
  • Mobilising All-India Protest: Phule's Gulamgiri, Ambedkar's writings, and Periyar's radical critiques reached masses across provincial boundaries, sparking organized social movements against untouchability and caste dominance.
Assertion & Reason Assessment
Assertion (A): The Vernacular Press Act was passed by the British colonial government in 1878.
Reason (R): The colonial government sought to suppress nationalist and anti-colonial criticism emerging in Indian language newspapers.

Evaluate whether Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are correct and if (R) explains (A).

CBSE Competency Answer:

Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, and Reason (R) is the correct explanation of Assertion (A).

Explanation: As vernacular newspapers became assertively nationalist following the 1876–77 famines, Lord Lytton enacted the Vernacular Press Act, modeled on Irish coercion laws, giving magistrates the power to confiscate printing presses if seditious material was published against the British Crown.
