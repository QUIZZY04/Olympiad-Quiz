Q1
Give two examples of different types of global exchanges which took place before the seventeenth century, choosing one example from Asia and one from the Americas.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Pre-seventeenth century global exchanges:

  1. From Asia (The Silk Routes):
    • The Silk Routes are a prime example of vibrant pre-modern trade and cultural links connecting Asia with Europe and northern Africa.
    • Chinese silk cargoes, Indian spices, and Chinese pottery travelled along these maritime and overland routes to Western markets. In return, precious metals — gold and silver — flowed from Europe to Asia. Alongside trade, cultural and religious exchanges took place: Buddhism from India spread in several directions through intersecting points on the silk routes.
  2. From the Americas (Introduction of New Food Crops):
    • Before Christopher Columbus accidentally discovered the vast continent later known as the Americas, many of our common foods were unknown.
    • Potatoes, maize, tomatoes, chillies, soya, groundnuts, and sweet potatoes were introduced to Europe and Asia from the Americas. These new crops transformed European lives: the humble potato significantly improved the nutrition and life expectancy of Europe’s poorest peasants (e.g., Ireland's peasants became so dependent on potatoes that when disease destroyed the crop in the mid-1840s, hundreds of thousands died of starvation during the Great Irish Potato Famine).

Q2
Explain how the global transfer of disease in the pre-modern world helped in the colonisation of the Americas.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The global transfer of disease in the pre-modern world proved to be the deadliest weapon in the European colonisation of the Americas:

  1. Biological Warfare via Germs: The Portuguese and Spanish conquest and colonisation of America was decisively under way by the mid-sixteenth century. European conquest was not just a result of superior military firepower; in fact, the most powerful weapon of the Spanish conquerors was not a conventional military weapon at all. It was the germs such as those of smallpox that they carried on their person.
  2. Lack of Immunity among Native Americans: Because of their long isolation from the rest of the world, America’s original inhabitants had no immunity against these diseases that came from Europe.
  3. Decimation of Entire Communities: Smallpox in particular proved a deadly killer. Once introduced into the continent, it spread deep into the interior, even ahead of any Europeans reaching there. It decimated and wiped out whole communities, paving the way for effortless conquest.
  4. Invulnerability of European Conquerors: Guns and firearms could be bought or captured and turned against the invaders. But diseases like smallpox could not be fought back because the European conquerors were mostly immune to it, whereas the indigenous defenders had no biological defense.

Q3
Write an educational note to explain the effects of the following: (a) The British government's decision to abolish the Corn Laws, (b) The coming of rinderpest to Africa, (c) The death of men of working age in Europe because of the World War, (d) The Great Depression on the Indian economy, (e) The decision of MNCs to relocate production to Asian countries.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(a) Abolition of the Corn Laws in Britain:

  • Under pressure from urban industrialists and city dwellers, the British government abolished the Corn Laws (tariffs on imported food).
  • Effects: Food could now be imported into Britain much more cheaply than it could be produced within the country. British agriculture was unable to compete with imports. Vast areas of land were left uncultivated, and thousands of farm labourers lost their jobs, migrating to cities or emigrating overseas. However, lower food prices led to higher consumption and stimulated global agricultural trade.

(b) The Coming of Rinderpest (Cattle Plague) to Africa:

  • Rinderpest arrived in Africa in the late 1880s, carried by infected cattle imported from British Asia to feed Italian soldiers invading Eritrea.
  • Effects: Moving like forest fire, it killed 90 per cent of Africa's cattle. The loss of cattle destroyed African livelihoods, devastated their subsistence economy, and forced previously self-reliant Africans into the labour market, allowing European colonisers to easily conquer and monopolise the continent.

(c) Death of Working-Age Men in Europe during the First World War:

  • The First World War was the first modern industrial war, utilizing machine guns, tanks, aircraft, and chemical weapons. Most of the 9 million dead and 20 million injured were men of working age.
  • Effects: These deaths drastically reduced the able-bodied workforce in Europe. Household incomes plummeted. Furthermore, as men went to battle, women stepped in to undertake jobs that earlier only men were expected to do, leading to a profound transformation in women's social role.

(d) The Great Depression on the Indian Economy (1929–1934):

  • The Depression immediately affected Indian trade. India’s exports and imports nearly halved between 1928 and 1934.
  • Effects: Wheat prices in India fell by 50 per cent. Peasants and farmers suffered more than urban dwellers; though agricultural prices collapsed, colonial authorities refused to reduce land revenue demands. Peasants used up their savings, mortgaged lands, and sold gold and jewellery (turning India into an exporter of precious metal), leading to widespread rural indebtedness.

(e) Relocation of Production by MNCs to Asian Countries:

  • From the late 1970s, multinational corporations began relocating production operations to low-wage Asian countries like China, India, and Southeast Asia.
  • Effects: Wages were relatively low in countries like China. This stimulated world trade and capital flows, created enormous employment opportunities in Asian economies, and resulted in rapid economic transformation, making countries like China and India major hubs of global manufacturing and services.

Q4
Give two examples from history to show the impact of technology on food availability.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Technology played a revolutionary role in improving food availability across nineteenth-century global markets, as shown by these two major historical examples:

  1. Development of Refrigerated Ships:
    • Earlier Condition: Till the 1870s, animals were shipped live from America to Europe and then slaughtered. Live animals took up immense ship space, many died in voyage, fell ill, or became too lean to eat. Meat was consequently an expensive luxury beyond the reach of European poor.
    • Technological Breakthrough: The invention of refrigerated ships enabled the transport of perishable foods over long distances. Animals could now be slaughtered at the starting point (in America, Australia, or New Zealand) and then transported to Europe as frozen meat. This reduced shipping costs, lowered meat prices in European markets, and allowed poor workers to consume a varied diet including meat, butter, and eggs.
  2. Railways and Faster Steamships:
    • Railways linked distant agricultural hinterlands to coastal ports, while larger, faster steamships transported bulk food grains across oceans quickly and cheaply.
    • Raw food items like wheat from the American prairies, Russian steppes, and Australian plains could be transported in massive quantities to feed urban industrial populations in Western Europe without spoiling.

Q5
What is meant by the Bretton Woods Agreement? Explain its significance.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Meaning of the Bretton Woods Agreement:

The Bretton Woods Agreement was finalized in July 1944 at the United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference held at Bretton Woods in New Hampshire, USA. Its main aim was to preserve economic stability and full employment in the industrial world after the devastation of the Second World War.

2. Core Bretton Woods Institutions:

  • International Monetary Fund (IMF): Established to deal with external surpluses and deficits of its member nations.
  • International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (World Bank): Set up to finance post-war reconstruction of war-shattered economies.
  • These two institutions are commonly known as the Bretton Woods Twins. They commenced financial operations in 1947. Decision-making was dominated by Western industrial powers, particularly the US, which held effective veto power over key decisions.

3. Historical Significance:

  • Inaugurated an era of unprecedented growth of trade and incomes for Western industrial nations and Japan between 1950 and the early 1970s.
  • Fixed exchange rates based on the US dollar anchored to gold at a fixed price ($35 per ounce of gold).
  • When developing nations later realized that IMF and World Bank were controlled by Western powers, they organised themselves as the Group of 77 (G-77) to demand a New International Economic Order (NIEO) with real control over natural resources and fairer trade terms.

Competency Case-Based Analysis
Read the following extract and answer the question:
'The Great Depression began around 1929 and lasted till the mid-1930s. During this period, most parts of the world experienced catastrophic declines in production, employment, incomes and trade. Agricultural regions and communities were the most severely affected. This was because the fall in agricultural prices was greater and more prolonged than that in the prices of industrial goods.'

Question: Why were agricultural regions hit harder than industrial sectors during the Great Depression?

CBSE Competency Answer:

  • Agricultural Overproduction and Price Collapse: Agricultural overproduction remained a severe problem. As prices slumped, farmers tried to expand production and bring larger volumes of produce to the market to maintain overall income. This flooded the market further, pushing down prices even more drastically.
  • Inability to Cut Output Instantly: Unlike industrial factories that could shut down machines, lay off workers, and curtail manufacturing instantly, agricultural production depends on biological cycles and cannot be halted abruptly, leaving farmers trapped with massive unsellable stocks.
Assertion & Reason Assessment
Assertion (A): Nineteenth-century 'indentured labour migration' has often been described as a 'new system of slavery'.
Reason (R): Agents gave false information to poor migrants about final destinations, modes of travel, the nature of the work, and living conditions; on arrival, labourers found harsh living conditions and few legal rights.

Evaluate whether Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are correct and if (R) explains (A).

CBSE Competency Answer:

Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, and Reason (R) is the correct explanation of Assertion (A).

Explanation: Hundreds of thousands of Indian and Chinese indentured labourers were recruited under contracts promising return travel after 5 years of work in Caribbean plantations, Fiji, and Mauritius. In reality, recruiters tricked illiterate peasants, wages were extremely low, deductions were heavy, and any attempt to escape was punished with severe imprisonment and corporal punishment, mirroring chattel slavery.
