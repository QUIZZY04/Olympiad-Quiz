History: India and the Contemporary World – II

The Age of Industrialisation

Unit 4: History • Proto-industrialisation, Factories, Hand Labour & Indian Mills — CBSE Board Marking Scheme 2026-27

NCERT In-Text & Exercise Questions — Board Examination Solutions
Q1
Explain the following: (a) Women workers in Britain attacked the Spinning Jenny, (b) In the seventeenth century merchants from towns in Europe began employing peasants and artisans within the villages, (c) The port of Surat declined by the end of the eighteenth century, (d) The East India Company appointed gomasthas to supervise weavers in India.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

(a) Women workers in Britain attacked the Spinning Jenny:

  • Invented by James Hargreaves in 1764, the Spinning Jenny speeded up the spinning process and reduced labour demand. A single wheel could turn a number of spindles and spin several threads simultaneously.
  • Hand-spinning was the primary livelihood of thousands of women workers in Britain. The fear of unemployment made women workers hostile to the new machine. When the Spinning Jenny was introduced in the woollen industry, women who survived on hand spinning began attacking the machines.

(b) In the seventeenth century, merchants from towns in Europe began employing peasants and artisans within the villages:

  • In towns, urban craft and trade guilds were extremely powerful. These associations of producers trained craftspersons, maintained control over production, regulated competition and prices, and restricted the entry of new people into the trade.
  • Monarchs granted different guilds the monopoly right to produce and trade in specific products. It was therefore difficult for new merchants to set up business in towns. So they turned to the countryside, supplying money to poor peasants and artisans and persuading them to produce for an international market.

(c) The port of Surat declined by the end of the eighteenth century:

  • Before the age of machine industries, Surat on the Gujarat coast connected India to the Gulf and Red Sea ports through a vibrant network controlled by Indian merchants and bankers.
  • By the 1750s, this network was breaking down. European companies gradually gained power — first securing a variety of concessions from local courts, and then the monopoly rights to trade. They operated through their own chosen ports like Bombay and Calcutta. As credit dried up and local bankers went bankrupt, trade through Surat slumped dramatically (gross value of trade fell from Rs 16 million in the late 17th century to Rs 3 million by the 1740s).

(d) The East India Company appointed Gomasthas to supervise weavers in India:

  • The East India Company wanted to eliminate competition from existing traders, French, Dutch, and Portuguese, and secure a direct, unbroken monopoly over the supply of cotton and silk textiles.
  • It established a system of direct management and control by appointing a paid servant called the Gomastha to supervise weavers, collect supplies, and examine the quality of cloth, preventing weavers from dealing with other buyers through coercive advance loan systems.

Q2
State whether True or False with detailed historical reasoning: (a) 80% industrial workforce in 19th-century Europe, (b) Indian dominance of fine textiles, (c) American Civil War and cotton exports, (d) The fly shuttle and handloom productivity.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Examination of Historical Statements (True / False with Explanations):

  1. (a) At the end of the nineteenth century, 80 per cent of the total workforce in Europe was employed in the technologically advanced industrial sector.
    • FALSE: Even by the late nineteenth century, less than 20 per cent of the total workforce was employed in technologically advanced industrial sectors. Traditional industries like food processing, pottery, glasswork, and furniture making employed the vast bulk of labourers.
  2. (b) The international market for fine textiles was dominated by India till the eighteenth century.
    • TRUE: Indian cotton and silk goods dominated the international market in quality and craftsmanship before mechanical industrialisation in Britain. Armenian and Persian merchants took goods from Punjab to Afghanistan, eastern Persia, and Central Asia.
  3. (c) The American Civil War resulted in the reduction of cotton exports from India.
    • FALSE: When the American Civil War broke out in 1861, raw cotton supplies from the US to Britain were cut off. Britain turned urgently to India. As raw cotton exports from India soared, prices skyrocketed, causing extreme hardship to Indian handloom weavers who could not afford expensive raw cotton.
  4. (d) The introduction of the fly shuttle increased the productivity of handloom workers without pushing up labour demand.
    • TRUE: The fly shuttle was a mechanical device operated by ropes and pullies that speeded up weaving. It enabled handloom weavers to operate large looms and weave wide pieces of cloth rapidly, increasing output per worker without increasing overall labour costs.

Statement (b) validation with Indian textile dominance1.25 Marks
Statement (c) correction on American Civil War boom1.25 Marks

Q3
Explain what is meant by proto-industrialisation.
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

1. Meaning of Proto-Industrialisation:

Proto-industrialisation refers to the phase of industrialisation that existed before the emergence of modern mechanized factories. There was a large-scale industrial production for an international market that was not based in factories, but centered in peasant households across the countryside.

2. Key Features and Working Mechanism:

  • Merchant-Producer Relationship: Merchants were based in towns, but the work was done mostly in the countryside. A merchant clothier in England bought wool from a wool stapler, carried it to the spinners, the yarn that was spun was taken to fullers, and then to dyers.
  • London as the Finishing Centre: The finishing of the cloth was done in London before the export merchant sold the cloth in the international market. London in fact came to be known as a finishing centre.
  • Supplementary Income for Farming Households: In the countryside, poor peasants and cottagers eagerly worked for merchants because open fields were disappearing and commons were being enclosed. Tiny family plots could not provide enough work for all members; working for proto-industrial merchants allowed them to remain in the countryside and cultivate their small plots while earning supplementary income.
  • Family Labour: The whole family — men, women, and children — participated in different stages of the production process.

Q4
Why did some industrialists in nineteenth-century Europe prefer hand labour over machines?
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

In nineteenth-century Europe (especially Britain), many industrialists preferred hand labour over machines due to the following compelling economic reasons:

  1. Abundance of Cheap Labour: Poor peasants and vagrants moved to cities in large numbers in search of work. When labour was plentiful, wages were extremely low. Industrialists had no incentive to invest heavily in expensive machinery that required costly maintenance.
  2. Seasonal Demand for Labour: In many industries, demand for labour was seasonal. Gas works and breweries were especially busy through the cold winter months. Bookbinders and printers needed extra hands before Christmas. In all such seasonal industries, employers preferred hand labour, hiring workers only for the peak season rather than buying machines that sat idle for the rest of the year.
  3. Demand for Intricate and Varied Designs: A large range of products could be produced only with hand labour. Machines were oriented to producing uniform, standardized goods for a mass market. But the market often demanded goods with specific shapes, intricate designs, and colours (e.g., 500 varieties of hammers and 45 kinds of axes produced in Britain), which required human skill, not mechanical uniformity.
  4. Aristocratic Taste for Handmade Goods: In Victorian Britain, the upper classes — the aristocrats and the bourgeoisie — preferred things produced by hand. Handmade goods came to symbolize refinement, class, and exclusivity. They were individually made and carefully finished. Machine-made goods were considered cheap and meant for export to the colonies.

Q5
How did the East India Company procure regular supplies of cotton and silk textiles from Indian weavers?
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

The English East India Company established a coercive system of managerial and political control to procure regular supplies of cotton and silk textiles from Indian weavers:

  1. Eliminating Competitors: The Company first established political power, which enabled it to assert a monopoly right to trade. It eliminated existing traders and brokers connected with the cloth trade.
  2. Appointment of Gomasthas: It appointed a paid servant called the Gomastha to supervise weavers, collect supplies, and examine the quality of cloth.
  3. System of Advances (Tied Credit): It prevented Company weavers from dealing with other buyers through the system of advances. Once an order was placed, weavers were given loans to purchase raw materials for their production. Those who took loans had to hand over the cloth they produced exclusively to the Gomastha. They could not sell it to any other trader.
  4. Loss of Independence and Land: Many weavers had small plots of land that they earlier cultivated alongside weaving. The demand for Company cloth forced them to lease out their lands and devote all their time to weaving. Entire families — children and women — were constantly engaged in different stages.
  5. Coercion and Punishment: The Gomasthas were outsiders with no social links to the villages. They acted arrogantly, marched into villages with sepoys and peons, and flogged and beat weavers for delays in supply. Prices given by the Company were pitiably low, driving many weavers to desert their villages and migrate.

Q6
Why did industrial production in India increase during the First World War?
5 Marks
✅ CBSE Board Standard Answer (Max Word Count)

Industrial production in India expanded dramatically during the First World War (1914–1918) due to the following wartime developments:

  1. Collapse of British Imports into India: With British mills busy with war production to meet the needs of the army, Manchester imports into India declined dramatically. Suddenly, Indian factories had a vast home market to supply without competition from foreign goods.
  2. War Orders from Colonial Government: As the war prolonged, Indian factories were called upon to supply war needs: jute bags, cloth for army uniforms, tents, leather boots, horse and mule saddles, and a host of other military equipment.
  3. Establishment of New Mills and Multiple Shifts: New factories were set up and old ones ran multiple shifts. Many new workers were employed and everyone was made to work longer hours. Over the war years, industrial production boomed.
  4. Permanent Loss of British Monopoly After the War: After the war, Manchester could never recapture its old position in the Indian market. Unable to modernise and compete with the US, Germany, and Japan, the economy of Britain crumbled. Within India, local industrialists consolidated their position, substituting foreign manufactures and capturing the domestic market.

Competency Case-Based Analysis
Read the following extract and answer the question:
'When Indian manufacturers advertised, the nationalist message was clear and loud. If you care for the nation then buy products that are made in India. Advertisements became a vehicle of the nationalist message of swadeshi. Images of Indian gods and goddesses regularly appeared on calendars to make a divine appeal to consumers.'

Question: How did marketing and advertisements help create a market for British and Indian goods in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries?

CBSE Competency Answer:

  • Labels and Brand Credibility: British manufacturers put labels on cloth bundles (e.g., 'Made in Manchester') to ensure consumers trusted the quality. Images of gods (Krishna, Saraswati) were used on labels so that buyers felt a divine sanction when purchasing the cloth.
  • Calendars in Common Households: Calendars were hung in tea shops and poor people's homes; unlike newspapers, even illiterate people saw these daily visual advertisements.
  • Swadeshi Propaganda: Indian manufacturers used advertisements featuring nationalist leaders and the Bharat Mata figure to champion the Swadeshi movement, linking consumer choices directly with patriotism.
Assertion & Reason Assessment
Assertion (A): In the eighteenth century, the wages of workers in England were low and unemployment was widespread.
Reason (R): The introduction of steam-powered machinery in all industries caused immediate displacement of the entire artisanal workforce.

Evaluate whether Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are correct and if (R) explains (A).

CBSE Competency Answer:

Assertion (A) is true, but Reason (R) is false.

Explanation: While workers faced extreme poverty, low wages, and seasonal unemployment, machines were NOT introduced in all industries immediately. Technological changes occurred slowly; machines were expensive, often broke down, and their repair was costly. Traditional hand-based methods dominated the vast majority of industries until well into the nineteenth century.
