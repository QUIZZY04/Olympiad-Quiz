Chapter 1: Locating Places on the Earth

NCERT Exploring Society: India and Beyond (Class 6) — 100% Complete Textbook Questions, The Big Questions, Let's Explore, Crossword & CBSE Rubrics | CBSE 2026-27

📌 Key Geographic Concepts & Principles
  • Earth's Shape & Axis: The Earth is slightly flattened at the North and South Poles and bulges in the middle, a unique shape called a Geoid (Earth-like). The imaginary line about which the Earth spins is called its axis, tilted at an angle of 23.5° to the vertical.
  • Equator & Hemispheres: An imaginary circular line drawn around the middle of the globe is the Equator (0° latitude). It divides the globe into two equal halves: the Northern Hemisphere and the Southern Hemisphere.
  • Parallels of Latitude: Concentric circles drawn parallel to the Equator up to the poles (0° to 90°N/S). Important parallels include Tropic of Cancer (23½° N), Tropic of Capricorn (23½° S), Arctic Circle (66½° N), and Antarctic Circle (66½° S).
  • Heat Zones: Torrid Zone (between tropics; direct sunrays, hottest), Temperate Zones (moderate climate), and Frigid Zones (beyond polar circles; extreme cold).
  • Meridians of Longitude: Semicircular lines of reference running from the North Pole to the South Pole. The Prime Meridian passes through Greenwich, London (0° longitude).
  • The Earth Grid & Standard Time: The intersection of latitudes and longitudes forms a coordinate grid that pinpoints any location. Earth rotates 360° in 24 hours, i.e., 15° per hour (1° every 4 minutes). Indian Standard Time (IST) is based on 82°30' E (Mirzapur, UP), which is GMT + 5:30.

The Big Questions (Chapter Inquiry & Openers)
Big Q1. What is a Map and What Are Its Main Components? Short ▼

Question: What is a map? How do we use it to represent places on Earth, and what are its three essential components?

1. Definition of a Map:
A map is a visual representation of the Earth’s surface, or a part of it, drawn on a flat surface according to a specific scale. Unlike a 3D globe, maps can show specific regions, cities, roads, rivers, and thematic details with high precision.

2. Three Essential Components of a Map:
• Distance (Scale): The ratio between the distance shown on the map and the actual distance on the ground (e.g., 1 cm = 100 km).
• Direction: Indicated by the North arrow ('N') pointing towards geographic north. Cardinal directions are North, South, East, and West.
• Symbols & Colours: Standard signs, letters, shades, and colours representing real features like water bodies (blue), mountains (brown), plains (green), and roads (red/black).

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for definition of map + 2 Marks for stating and explaining Distance (Scale), Direction, and Symbols.

Big Q2. What are Geographic Coordinates and the Earth Grid? Concept ▼

Question: What are geographic coordinates? How do parallels of latitude and meridians of longitude work together to pinpoint any location on Earth?

1. Concept of Coordinates:
Geographic coordinates are an address system for the planet consisting of two numbers: latitude (angular distance north or south of the Equator) and longitude (angular distance east or west of the Prime Meridian).

2. The Earth Grid:
Parallels of latitude and meridians of longitude cross each other at right angles to form a criss-cross network called the Earth Grid. Because every point on the globe lies at the exact intersection of one parallel and one meridian, any point—from a tiny island in the Pacific to a skyscraper in Delhi—can be located uniquely without confusion (e.g., New Delhi is at 28°38' N, 77°13' E).

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for defining coordinates + 1 Mark for explaining how the grid intersection provides unique location addresses.

Big Q3. Relationship Between Earth’s Rotation, Longitude and Time Explain ▼

Question: How are local time and standard time related to the longitudinal rotation of the Earth? Why do different places experience sunrise at different times?

1. Planetary Rotation and Time:
The Earth completes one full axial rotation of 360° in 24 hours from West to East. This means: $$\frac{360^\circ}{24\text{ hours}} = 15^\circ\text{ per hour} \implies 1^\circ\text{ every 4 minutes}$$ As the Earth rotates eastward, places situated east of Greenwich see the sun earlier, so their local time is ahead. Places situated to the west see the sun later, so their local time is behind.

2. Local Time vs. Standard Time:
• Local Time: The time determined by the sun's position at a specific meridian (noon is when the sun is at highest point in the sky). Every meridian has a different local time.
• Standard Time: To avoid chaos in timetables, a country adopts the local time of one central meridian as the uniform legal time for the entire nation (e.g., IST along 82°30' E in India).

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for rate of rotation (15°/hr = 4 min/deg) + 1 Mark for explaining West-to-East rotation + 1 Mark for distinction between local time and standard time.

Let's Explore & In-Text Practical Activities
Activity 1. Scale Calculation: School Playground Diagonal Numerical ▼

Question: In-Text "Let’s Explore" Activity:
A school playground measures 40 metres in length and 30 metres in width. Draw a plan of the playground using a scale of 1 cm = 10 metres. What will be the diagonal distance across the playground on the map and on the ground?

Step 1: Convert ground dimensions to map dimensions using the scale (1 cm = 10 m):
• Length on map = 40 m ÷ 10 m/cm = 4 cm
• Width on map = 30 m ÷ 10 m/cm = 3 cm

Step 2: Calculate the diagonal distance on the map (Pythagoras theorem):
$$\text{Diagonal}^2 = 4^2 + 3^2 = 16 + 9 = 25 \implies \text{Diagonal on map} = \sqrt{25} = 5\text{ cm}$$

Step 3: Calculate the actual diagonal distance on the ground:
Actual ground distance = 5 cm × 10 m/cm = 50 metres.

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for converting length & width to cm + 1 Mark for Pythagoras calculation (5 cm) + 1 Mark for actual ground distance (50 m).

Activity 2. In-Text Time Calculation: Porbandar vs. Tinsukia Numerical ▼

Question: In-Text "Let’s Explore" Activity:
Porbandar in Gujarat is located at approximately 69° E longitude, while Tinsukia in Assam is located at approximately 95° E longitude. Calculate the difference in local sunrise time between these two towns. Why do they follow the same watch time?

Step 1: Calculate the longitudinal difference:
Longitude of Tinsukia = 95° E
Longitude of Porbandar = 69° E
Difference = 95° − 69° = 26°

Step 2: Calculate time difference:
Since 1° = 4 minutes: 26° × 4 minutes = 104 minutes = 1 hour and 44 minutes (~nearly 2 hours).

Step 3: Explanation:
The sun rises nearly 1 hour and 44 minutes earlier in Tinsukia than in Porbandar. However, both towns set their clocks to the same time because India follows a single Indian Standard Time (IST) fixed at 82°30' E to ensure smooth administration, railway schedules, and broadcast communications.

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for longitudinal difference of 26° + 1 Mark for time difference of 104 minutes (1 hr 44 min) + 1 Mark for explaining uniform IST.

Q1. Map Scale: Narmada River Estuary to Ganga River Numerical ▼

Question: On the physical map of India, the distance between the estuary of the Narmada River (Gulf of Khambhat) and the Ganga River is measured as approximately 5.5 cm. If the map scale is 1 cm = 200 km, calculate the actual ground distance in kilometres.

• Distance on the map = 5.5 cm
• Map scale: 1 cm = 200 km

Conclusion:
The actual ground distance between the mouth of the Narmada River and the Ganga River is approximately 1,100 kilometres.

Q2. Why is it 5:30 PM in India when it is 12:00 PM in London? Explain ▼

Question: Why is it 5:30 PM in India when it is 12:00 PM (noon) in London? Explain with full step-by-step mathematical calculation. [3 Marks]

Step 1: Identify the standard longitudes:
• London (Greenwich, UK) lies on the Prime Meridian = 0° Longitude.
• India follows Indian Standard Time (IST) along = 82°30' E Longitude (82.5° E).

Step 2: Find the difference in longitudes:
$$\text{Difference} = 82.5^\circ - 0^\circ = 82.5^\circ$$

Step 3: Calculate the time difference:
Since the Earth takes 4 minutes to rotate through 1° of longitude:
$$\text{Time difference} = 82.5 \times 4\text{ minutes} = 330\text{ minutes}$$ $$330\text{ minutes} = \frac{330}{60} = 5\text{ hours and } 30\text{ minutes}$$

Step 4: Determine the time:
Because India lies to the East of the Prime Meridian, the sun rises earlier here, so Indian time is ahead (+):
$$\text{Time in India} = 12:00\text{ PM (noon)} + 5\text{ hrs } 30\text{ mins} = 5:30\text{ PM}$$

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for stating 0° and 82.5° E meridians + 1 Mark for calculation: 82.5 × 4 = 330 mins = 5 hr 30 min + 1 Mark for adding to 12:00 PM to get 5:30 PM.

Q3. Why Do We Need Symbols and Colours on a Map? Short ▼

Question: Why do we need symbols and colours on a map? Why can’t we just draw actual objects like real buildings, trees, and rivers?

1. Space Limitation on Flat Paper:
It is impossible to draw the actual size and shape of houses, bridges, trees, railway tracks, or post offices on a small sheet of paper.

2. Universal Clarity and Easy Reading:
By using standardized symbols (letters, shades, lines) and conventional colours, huge amounts of geographic information can be communicated clearly in limited space.

3. International Convention:
Maps use globally agreed conventional colours: • Blue: Water bodies (rivers, lakes, oceans)
• Brown: Mountains and hills
• Yellow: Plateaus and dry uplands
• Green: Plains and agricultural lands

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for space limitation reason + 1 Mark for conventional colours and universal comprehension.

Question: Differentiate between Local Time and Standard Time. Why is standard time necessary for large nations?

Feature Local Time Standard Time
Basis Calculated by the overhead position of the sun (solar noon) at a particular meridian. Adopted as the uniform legal time along a chosen central meridian of a whole country.
Variation Varies from one longitude to another (differs by 4 minutes per degree). Remains identical and uniform across the entire standard time zone.

Q5. What is a Grid and How Does It Help Locate Places? Short ▼

Question: What is a grid? How does the network of parallels and meridians help us locate places on a globe or map?

1. Definition:
A grid is a network of intersecting horizontal lines (parallels of latitude) and vertical lines (meridians of longitude) drawn across maps and globes.

2. Function in Pinpointing Locations:
Every specific location on Earth lies at the precise crossing point (intersection) of one parallel and one meridian. If we know the coordinates (latitude and longitude) of a place, we can find its exact position instantly. For example, knowing that Dhubri in Assam is located at 26° N latitude and 90° E longitude allows us to locate it exactly where the 26° N line intersects the 90° E line.

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for definition of grid + 1 Mark for explaining how coordinate intersection pinpoints exact locations.

Q6. Difference in Local Time: New Delhi vs. Bengaluru Numerical ▼

Question: New Delhi is located at approximately 29° N latitude and 77° E longitude. Bengaluru is located at approximately 13° N latitude and 77° E longitude. What is the difference in local time between these two cities? Give reasons.

Answer: There is ZERO (0 minutes) difference in local time between New Delhi and Bengaluru.

Reason:
• Local time depends entirely on meridians of longitude, NOT on parallels of latitude.
• Both New Delhi (29° N, 77° E) and Bengaluru (13° N, 77° E) lie on the exact same meridian of longitude (77° E).
• Since the sun crosses the 77° E meridian at the exact same instant along its entire length from north to south, both cities experience midday (solar noon) simultaneously.

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for stating 0 minutes difference + 2 Marks for explaining that local time depends on longitude, and both lie on 77° E.

Q7. True / False Statements with Detailed Reasons Short ▼

Question: State whether the following statements are True or False. Give valid geographical reasons for each:
(i) All parallels of latitude have the same length.
(ii) The length of a meridian of longitude is half the length of the Equator.
(iii) The South Pole has a latitude of 90° S.
(iv) Assam has the same local time as Indian Standard Time (IST).
(v) Time zones are roughly identical with meridians of longitude.
(vi) The Equator is a parallel of latitude.

  • (i) False: Parallels of latitude are concentric circles whose circumferences decrease gradually from the Equator towards the poles, shrinking to a single point (0 km) at 90° N and 90° S.
  • (ii) True: The Equator is a complete great circle around the middle of the Earth (~40,075 km), whereas a meridian is a semicircle extending from the North Pole to the South Pole (~20,004 km), which is half the Equator's circumference.
  • (iii) True: Angular distance from the Equator (0°) to either pole is one-fourth of a circle (360° ÷ 4 = 90°). Thus, the South Pole is designated 90° S.
  • (iv) False: Assam lies along 92°–95° E, whereas IST is fixed along 82°30' E. Thus, the local solar time of Assam is around 40 to 50 minutes ahead of IST.
  • (v) True: Standard time zones are generally organized in longitudinal bands of approximately 15° each, corresponding to 1 hour of difference.
  • (vi) True: The Equator is the largest parallel of latitude (0°), serving as the starting reference line for measuring all other latitudes.

Q9. Heat Zones of the Earth & Torrid Zone Warmth Explain ▼

Question: Name the three heat zones of the Earth. Why does the Torrid Zone receive the maximum amount of solar heat compared to other zones? [3 Marks]

1. Three Heat Zones of the Earth:
• Torrid Zone: The belt between the Tropic of Cancer (23½° N) and Tropic of Capricorn (23½° S).
• Temperate Zones: North Temperate Zone (between Tropic of Cancer and Arctic Circle) and South Temperate Zone (between Tropic of Capricorn and Antarctic Circle).
• Frigid Zones: North Frigid Zone (north of Arctic Circle) and South Frigid Zone (south of Antarctic Circle).

2. Reason for Maximum Heat in the Torrid Zone:
The midday sun is directly overhead at least once a year at all latitudes in this zone. When the sun rays fall vertically, the heat energy is concentrated over a small surface area with minimum atmospheric dispersion. In contrast, in the Temperate and Frigid zones, rays always fall at a slant.

🎯 CBSE Marking Scheme Rubric (Exam Guide):

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1.5 Marks for naming all three heat zones + 1.5 Marks for explaining vertical rays and overhead midday sun.

Question: Competency-Based Question (CBQ):
A distress call from a fishing vessel indicates coordinates 20° N latitude and 65° E longitude. Explain how the Indian Coast Guard rescue helicopter uses the Earth’s grid system to navigate directly to the vessel without visual landmarks.

1. Using Latitude (20° N):
The helicopter’s GPS system navigates along the parallel 20 degrees north of the Equator.

2. Using Longitude (65° E):
The navigation system tracks the meridian 65 degrees east of the Prime Meridian in Greenwich.

3. Pinpointing the Intersection Point:
Since lines of latitude and longitude intersect at a single unique mathematical point in the Arabian Sea, following these GPS coordinates guides the rescue crew directly to the vessel even in heavy fog or night darkness.

📌 Key Geographical Concepts: Hydrosphere & Lithosphere
  • The Blue Planet: Approximately 71% of the Earth's surface is covered with water (the hydrosphere) and only 29% is land (the lithosphere). Seen from space, water reflects sunlight, making Earth appear radiant blue.
  • The Seven Continents (in order of size):
    1. Asia: The largest continent, covering one-third of total landmass. Home to Mount Everest and the highest population.
    2. Africa: The second-largest continent; the Equator, Tropic of Cancer, and Tropic of Capricorn all pass through it. Features the Sahara Desert and Nile River.
    3. North America: Third-largest continent, completely in Northern and Western hemispheres. Connected to South America by the Isthmus of Panama.
    4. South America: Mostly in the Southern Hemisphere. Contains the Amazon River basin and the Andes mountains.
    5. Antarctica: A massive frozen continent completely south of the Antarctic Circle around the South Pole. Permanently covered in thick ice sheets.
    6. Europe: Located west of Asia, separated by the Ural Mountains. Combined landmass is known as Eurasia.
    7. Australia: The smallest continent, surrounded entirely by oceans (known as the Island Continent).
  • The Five Major Oceans:
    • Pacific Ocean: Largest and deepest; circular in shape; contains the Mariana Trench (11,022 m) and the volcanic "Ring of Fire".
    • Atlantic Ocean: Second-largest; distinctive 'S' shape; highly indented coastline makes it the busiest ocean for international trade.
    • Indian Ocean: The only ocean named after a country (India); roughly triangular in shape.
    • Southern Ocean: Surrounds the continent of Antarctica up to 60° S latitude.
    • Arctic Ocean: Smallest ocean, surrounding the North Pole within the Arctic Circle.
  • Oceans and Life: Oceans regulate global climate by absorbing heat and circulating currents, provide marine food, generate over 50% of the Earth's oxygen via microscopic phytoplankton, and enable international maritime shipping.

The Big Questions (Chapter Inquiry & Openers)
Big Q1. What Are Oceans and Continents, and How Are They Distributed? Concept ▼

Question: What are oceans and continents? List their names and describe their global distribution across the Earth.

1. Definitions:
• Continents: The vast, continuous landmasses that rise above sea level, making up 29% of the Earth's surface.
• Oceans: The massive, continuous bodies of saline water that cover 71% of the Earth's surface.

2. Global Distribution:
• There are seven continents: Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe, and Australia.
• There are five oceans: Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern, and Arctic.
• The distribution is unequal: The Northern Hemisphere contains the majority of the landmass (Land Hemisphere), whereas the Southern Hemisphere is dominated by vast oceans (Water Hemisphere).

Big Q2. How Do Oceans and Continents Impact Life and Climate? Explain ▼

Question: In what ways do oceans and continents impact life on Earth, including human life, weather, and the global climate?

1. Climate & Weather Regulation:
Oceans absorb solar radiation and distribute heat across the planet through marine currents. They provide the moisture that evaporates to form clouds and life-giving rainfall (the Water Cycle). Without oceans, continental interiors would experience extreme, uninhabitable temperature swings.

2. Production of Oxygen:
Tiny marine plants called phytoplankton produce over 50% of the Earth's oxygen through photosynthesis.

3. Human Livelihoods & Trade:
Oceans supply millions of tons of seafood, provide petroleum and salt resources, and serve as natural global shipping highways carrying over 80% of world trade.

Let's Explore & In-Text Thought Experiments
Activity 1. Why Is There Water Scarcity When Earth Is 71% Water? Explain ▼

Question: In-Text "Think About" Activity:
If approximately 71% of the Earth’s surface is covered with water, why do people around the world frequently suffer from acute water scarcity? [3 Marks]

1. High Salinity of Ocean Water:
About 97.5% of all water on Earth is saline (salty) ocean water, which cannot be directly drunk, used for irrigating crops, or used in manufacturing.

2. Freshwater Is Locked in Ice Caps:
Only 2.5% of the planet's water is freshwater. Out of this tiny fraction, nearly 70% is frozen in the ice sheets and glaciers of Antarctica, the Arctic, and high mountain ranges, making it inaccessible.

3. Accessible Freshwater Is Very Limited and Polluted:
Less than 1% of all freshwater is available in rivers, lakes, and underground aquifers. Due to over-extraction, population growth, and pollution of freshwater bodies, human communities face severe water scarcity.

CBSE Marking Scheme: 1 Mark for stating 97.5% is saline ocean water + 1 Mark for ~70% of freshwater locked in glaciers + 1 Mark for uneven distribution, pollution & over-extraction.

Activity 2. Land Hemisphere vs. Water Hemisphere Analysis ▼

Question: In-Text Activity:
Why is the Northern Hemisphere called the "Land Hemisphere" and the Southern Hemisphere called the "Water Hemisphere"?

1. The Northern Hemisphere (Land Hemisphere):
Over 67% (two-thirds) of the Earth’s total landmass lies in the Northern Hemisphere, including the vast expanses of North America, Europe, Asia, and northern Africa.

2. The Southern Hemisphere (Water Hemisphere):
In the Southern Hemisphere, approximately 81% of the surface is covered by water, and only 19% is land (parts of South America, southern Africa, Australia, and Antarctica). Due to this overwhelming ocean dominance, it is termed the Water Hemisphere.

Question: Define the following terms with appropriate examples:
(a) Continent
(b) Ocean
(c) Island [3 Marks]

(a) Continent:
A continent is a very large, continuous landmass separated from other landmasses by vast bodies of water or mountain systems.
Example: Asia (the largest continent) and Africa.

(b) Ocean:
An ocean is a vast, interconnected body of saline water that separates continents and covers most of the Earth's surface.
Example: Pacific Ocean (largest and deepest) and Indian Ocean.

(c) Island:
An island is a piece of land completely surrounded by water on all sides, smaller than a continent.
Example: Greenland (the world's largest island), Sri Lanka, and the Andaman & Nicobar Islands.

📌 Key Geological Concepts: Major Landforms & Human Life
  • Earth's Geological Processes: Landforms are shaped by two forces:
    • Internal Processes: Upliftment, folding, and sinking of the earth’s crust caused by tectonic movements (e.g., formation of the Himalayas).
    • External Processes: Continuous wearing down (erosion) and rebuilding (deposition) of the land surface by running water, wind, and glaciers.
  • Major Landforms:
    • Mountains: Natural elevations rising steeply above surrounding land with high peaks and cold climates. Types: Fold Mountains (Himalayas, Alps), Block Mountains (Vindhyas, Vosges), and Volcanic Mountains (Mt Kilimanjaro, Mt Fuji).
    • Plateaus: Elevated flat-topped tablelands standing sharply above surrounding areas (e.g., Deccan Plateau, Chhota Nagpur Plateau, Tibetan Plateau). They are storehouses of rich mineral deposits.
    • Plains: Vast expanses of flat, low-lying land (usually not more than 200 m above sea level). Formed by sediment deposition (alluvium) by rivers and their tributaries (e.g., Northern Indian Gangetic Plain).
  • Landforms Dictate Life: Human occupations, houses, food, clothing, and transport networks are directly shaped by landforms. Plains support dense populations and farming; mountains foster terrace cultivation, forestry, and tourism; plateaus sustain mining and heavy industries.

Q5. Types of Mountains: Fold, Block, and Volcanic Explain ▼

Question: Explain the three major types of mountains based on their mode of formation. Give two examples of each type from India and around the world.

1. Fold Mountains:
Formed when tectonic plates push against each other, causing layers of the Earth’s crust to compress and wrinkle into gigantic folds.
• Young Fold Mountains: Have rugged relief and high conical peaks (e.g., The Himalayas in Asia, The Alps in Europe).
• Old Fold Mountains: Worn down by erosion with rounded peaks (e.g., Aravalli Range in India, Appalachians in North America).

2. Block Mountains:
Formed when large areas of the crust crack, fracture, and are displaced vertically along geological faults. The uplifted blocks are called horsts and the sunken valleys are called grabens (rift valleys).
• Examples: Vindhya and Satpura ranges in central India, Rhine Valley and Vosges mountain in Europe.

3. Volcanic Mountains:
Formed by the buildup and cooling of molten magma, volcanic ash, and lava erupting from the Earth’s interior through a vent.
• Examples: Mount Kilimanjaro in Africa, Mount Fuji in Japan, and Barren Island in the Andaman Sea (India).

📌 Key Historical Concepts: Dating, Timelines & Evidence
  • What is History? History is the systematic inquiry into the human past, based on verifiable evidence. Historians investigate what people did, ate, wore, believed, and built over centuries.
  • Measuring Historical Time (Dating System):
    • BCE (Before Common Era): Years before the birth of Jesus Christ. Counted backwards towards zero (e.g., 2500 BCE is older than 500 BCE). Formerly known as BC (Before Christ).
    • CE (Common Era): Years from the birth of Christ onwards. Counted forwards (e.g., 2026 CE). Formerly known as AD (Anno Domini — "in the year of our Lord").
    • Circa ('c.' or 'ca.'): Latin word meaning "approximately" or "around" used when the exact date is uncertain (e.g., c. 1500 BCE).
  • Two Main Branches of Historical Sources:
    • Archaeological Sources (Material Clues): Physical artifacts dug up from the ground—pottery, tools, weapons, ornaments, terracotta toys, coins (numismatics), seals, and ancient buildings/monuments.
    • Literary Sources (Written Clues):
      • Manuscripts: Hand-written records composed on palm leaves or the birch bark of Himalayan trees.
      • Inscriptions: Writings engraved on hard, durable surfaces like stone rocks, temple pillars, and copper plates (epigraphy).
  • Historians as Detectives: Just like detectives who collect fingerprints and footprints to reconstruct a crime, historians assemble scattered physical fragments and ancient texts to reconstruct past human life.

📌 Key Archaeological Concepts: The Harappan Civilisation
  • Terminology: Also known as the Harappan Civilisation (after Harappa, the first excavated site in 1921), the Indus Valley Civilisation, or the Indus-Sarasvatī Civilisation (over 60% of known settlements lie along the dried-up bed of the ancient Sarasvatī/Ghaggar-Hakra river).
  • Chronology: Mature urban phase flourished between c. 2600 BCE and 1900 BCE.
  • Outstanding Urban Features:
    • Town Planning: Divided into two sections: an elevated Citadel in the west (public buildings) and a larger Lower Town in the east (residential houses). Grid pattern streets intersecting at right angles.
    • Standardised Baked Bricks: Interlocking burnt bricks made in a precise mathematical ratio of 1:2:4 (thickness:width:length) used uniformly across thousands of kilometres.
    • Covered Drainage & Sanitation: Household wastewater flowed through terracotta pipes into covered street sewers equipped with inspection traps and silt pits.
    • Water Architecture: The Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro made watertight with natural bitumen; massive rock-cut water reservoirs at Dholavira; world's earliest tidal dockyard at Lothal.
  • Crafts, Trade & Decline: Steatite seals with unicorn/bull motifs and undeciphered script, bronze casting (Dancing Girl), cubical chert weights, and overseas trade with Mesopotamia (Meluhha). The civilisation declined around 1900 BCE due to the drying up of the Sarasvatī River and climatic shifts.

Question: Why is this civilisation known by multiple names—Harappan, Indus Valley, and Indus-Sarasvatī Civilisation? Why is "Harappan Civilisation" the standard archaeological term? [3 Marks]

1. Indus Valley Civilisation:
Early excavations in the 1920s took place around the Indus River valley (Harappa on the Ravi and Mohenjo-daro on the Indus), leading historians to coin this name.

2. Indus-Sarasvatī Civilisation:
Subsequent discoveries revealed that over 60% of all Harappan settlements were situated along the basin of the sacred Sarasvatī River (today’s Ghaggar-Hakra dry river bed), justifying this title.

3. Why "Harappan Civilisation" Is Preferred:
In archaeology, international convention mandates that when a previously unknown ancient culture is discovered, it is officially named after the type-site—the very first site where its remains were excavated. Since Harappa was excavated first in 1921 by Daya Ram Sahni, archaeologists call it the Harappan Civilisation.

Repeated silt-laden flooding of the Indus submerged cities like Mohenjo-daro, while the collapse of trade with Mesopotamia impoverished the urban merchant class.

📌 Key Rural Governance Concepts: The Panchayati Raj System
  • Gram Sabha: The general body of the village comprising all adults (18 years and above) registered as voters. It is the purest form of direct grassroots democracy in India, meeting regularly to approve village development plans and review expenses.

  • Women's Leadership: By constitutional mandate (73rd Amendment), at least one-third (33%, up to 50% in many states) of all seats and Sarpanch posts are reserved for women, revolutionizing rural female leadership.

📌 Key Economic & Ethical Concepts: Work, Dignity & Care Economy
  • What is Work? Any physical or mental effort undertaken to produce goods, provide services, or sustain life.

    • Mahatma Gandhi & Shramdaan: Advocated voluntary manual labour (*Bread Labour*) and insisted that everyone in his ashrams, including himself, clean their own latrines to eliminate caste-based stigmas.
    • Basaveshwara (*Kayakave Kailasa*): The 12th-century philosopher-reformer declared that dedicated, honest physical work itself is Heaven / Worship.

📌 Key Economic Concepts: The Three Sectors & Markets
  • Three Sectors of the Economy:
    • Primary Sector (Nature-Based): Economic activities that extract or harvest natural resources directly from the Earth (e.g., agriculture, dairy farming, fishing, forestry, mining).

  • Rotation: Daily movement of Earth on its tilted axis (23½° tilt); causes Day and Night.

• Reason for Seasons: Seasons change because the Earth revolves around the Sun in an elliptical orbit with its axis permanently tilted at an angle of 66½° to its orbital plane (or 23½° to the vertical) in the same direction throughout the year.
