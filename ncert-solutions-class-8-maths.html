Chapter 1: A Square and A Cube

NCERT Ganita Prakash (Class 8 Part 1) — Square Numbers, Square Roots, Long Division, Patterns, Cube Numbers, Cube Roots & Pythagorean Triplets | CBSE 2026-27

📌 Key Mathematical Concepts & Formulas
  • Square Numbers: A natural number n is a perfect square if n = m² for some natural number m.
    • Square numbers end only in digits 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9 at units place. Numbers ending in 2, 3, 7, 8 can never be perfect squares.
    • A number with n zeros at the end has 2n zeros when squared.
    • Non-square numbers between n² and (n + 1)² is exactly 2n: (n + 1)² − n² − 1 = 2n.
    • Sum of first n consecutive odd natural numbers is n²: 1 + 3 + 5 + ... + (2n − 1) = n².
    • Pythagorean Triplet: For any natural number m > 1, (2m)² + (m² − 1)² = (m² + 1)².
  • Square Roots (√n): The inverse operation of squaring.
    • Repeated Subtraction: Successively subtract consecutive odd numbers (1, 3, 5, 7...) starting from 1 until 0 is reached. Count of subtractions is √n.
    • Prime Factorisation: Pair identical prime factors in twos. Square root is product of one factor from each pair.
    • Long Division Method: Group digits in pairs (periods) from right to left (for decimals, from decimal point outwards). Essential for large numbers and decimal square roots.
  • Cube Numbers & Cube Roots (∛n): A number n is a perfect cube if n = m³.
    • In prime factorisation of a perfect cube, every prime factor appears in triplets (multiples of 3).
    • Cube of an even number is even; cube of an odd number is odd.
    • Cube root by estimation: Group digits in triplets from right. First group gives units digit (by unit digit cube mapping); remaining group gives tens digit by nearest cube.

What will be the unit digit of the squares of the following numbers?
(i) 81    (ii) 272    (iii) 799    (iv) 3853    (v) 1234    (vi) 26387    (vii) 52698    (viii) 99880    (ix) 12796    (x) 55555
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution

Property: The unit digit of the square of a number depends solely on the unit digit of the number itself. If a number ends in digit u, its square ends in the unit digit of u².

• (i) 81: Unit digit is 1. Since 1² = 1, unit digit of 81² is 1.
• (ii) 272: Unit digit is 2. Since 2² = 4, unit digit of 272² is 4.
• (iii) 799: Unit digit is 9. Since 9² = 81 (unit digit 1), unit digit of 799² is 1.
• (iv) 3853: Unit digit is 3. Since 3² = 9, unit digit of 3853² is 9.
• (v) 1234: Unit digit is 4. Since 4² = 16 (unit digit 6), unit digit of 1234² is 6.
• (vi) 26387: Unit digit is 7. Since 7² = 49 (unit digit 9), unit digit of 26387² is 9.
• (vii) 52698: Unit digit is 8. Since 8² = 64 (unit digit 4), unit digit of 52698² is 4.
• (viii) 99880: Unit digit is 0. Since 0² = 0, unit digit of 99880² is 0.
• (ix) 12796: Unit digit is 6. Since 6² = 36 (unit digit 6), unit digit of 12796² is 6.
• (x) 55555: Unit digit is 5. Since 5² = 25 (unit digit 5), unit digit of 55555² is 5.

[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution

• (i) Between 12² and 13²:
Here n = 12.
Number of non-square numbers = 2n = 2 × 12 = 24.

• (ii) Between 25² and 26²:
Here n = 25.
Number of non-square numbers = 2n = 2 × 25 = 50.

• (iii) Between 99² and 100²:
Here n = 99.
Number of non-square numbers = 2n = 2 × 99 = 198.

Q5
Write a Pythagorean triplet whose smallest member is:
(i) 6     (ii) 14     (iii) 16     (iv) 18
[3 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution

General Form of Pythagorean Triplet: For any integer m > 1, the three numbers 2m, m² − 1, and m² + 1 form a Pythagorean triplet.

• (i) Smallest member = 6:
Let 2m = 6 ⇒ m = 3.
m² − 1 = 3² − 1 = 9 − 1 = 8.
m² + 1 = 3² + 1 = 9 + 1 = 10.
Check: 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10².
Hence, the required triplet is (6, 8, 10).

• (ii) Smallest member = 14:
Let 2m = 14 ⇒ m = 7.
m² − 1 = 7² − 1 = 49 − 1 = 48.
m² + 1 = 7² + 1 = 49 + 1 = 50.
Check: 14² + 48² = 196 + 2304 = 2500 = 50².
Hence, the required triplet is (14, 48, 50).

• (iii) Smallest member = 16:
Let 2m = 16 ⇒ m = 8.
m² − 1 = 8² − 1 = 64 − 1 = 63.
m² + 1 = 8² + 1 = 64 + 1 = 65.
Hence, the required triplet is (16, 63, 65).

• (iv) Smallest member = 18:
Let 2m = 18 ⇒ m = 9.
m² − 1 = 9² − 1 = 81 − 1 = 80.
m² + 1 = 9² + 1 = 81 + 1 = 82.
Hence, the required triplet is (18, 80, 82).

Q14
Find the cube root of 857375 through the Estimation Method without prime factorisation.
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
1. Form groups of three digits from the right:
First group (units group) = 375
Second group = 857

2. Determining the units digit:
The first group is 375, ending in 5. Since only 5³ = 125 ends in 5, the units digit of the cube root must be 5.

3. Determining the tens digit:
Consider the second group: 857.
We know 9³ = 729 and 10³ = 1000.
Clearly, 729 < 857 < 1000, i.e., 9³ < 857 < 10³.
Taking the smaller integer, the tens digit is 9.

Therefore, ∛857375 = 95.

Q15
Parikshit makes a cuboid of plasticine of sides 5 cm, 2 cm, 5 cm. How many such cuboids will he need to form a cube?
[2 Marks]
✅ CBSE Standard Step-by-Step Solution
1. Volume of one cuboid = 5 × 2 × 5 = 50 cm³.
In prime factor form: 5 × 2 × 5 = 2¹ × 5².

2. For these cuboids to combine and form a perfect cube, the prime factors of the total volume must appear in triplets (groups of 3):
- Factor 2 appears 1 time (needs two more 2s: 2 × 2 = 4).
- Factor 5 appears 2 times (needs one more 5).

3. Therefore, to make the volume a perfect cube, we must multiply by:
2 × 2 × 5 = 20.

Hence, Parikshit requires 20 such cuboids to form a cube.

Context — Community Hall Renovation & Cube Packaging: An architect is renovating a municipal hall whose floor is in the shape of a perfect square with an area of 5929 m². The flooring is to be paved with square marble tiles of side 1 m.
Additionally, a warehouse supplies cubic storage cartons each of volume 1728 cm³ that are to be packed inside a large master cubic container of side 72 cm.

(a) Find the length of each side of the square community hall.
(b) If decorative border tiles are placed along the entire boundary (perimeter) of the hall, find how many linear meters of border are needed.
(c) Calculate the edge length of each cubic storage carton and determine how many cartons fit exactly into the master container.
Detailed Analytical Solution & Marking Scheme

(a) Side length of the square community hall:
Area = side² = 5929 m² ⇒ side = √5929.
Using Long Division on 5929:
7² = 49 ≤ 59, remainder = 10. Bring down 29 ⇒ 1029.
Double 7 = 14. 147 × 7 = 1029. Remainder = 0.
Therefore, side = 77 meters.

(b) Boundary (Perimeter) of the hall:
Perimeter = 4 × side = 4 × 77 = 308 meters of decorative border needed.

(c) Edge length of carton & Master packaging count:
- Edge of one small carton = ∛1728 = ∛(12³) = 12 cm.
- Master cube edge = 72 cm.
- Number of cartons along each dimension = 72 ÷ 12 = 6 cartons.
- Total cartons packed = 6 × 6 × 6 = 6³ = 216 cartons.
(Verification: Master volume = 72³ = 373,248 cm³. Total = 373,248 ÷ 1728 = 216).

📌 Key Laws of Exponents & Scientific Notation
  • Definition: For any non-zero real number a and integer n, aⁿ denotes repeated multiplication of a by itself n times (where a is the base and n is the exponent / power).
  • Fundamental Laws of Exponents (for non-zero a, b and integers m, n):
    • Product Law: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ
    • Quotient Law: aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ
    • Power of a Power Law: (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ
    • Power of a Product: aᵐ × bᵐ = (ab)ᵐ
    • Power of a Quotient: aᵐ ÷ bᵐ = (a/b)ᵐ
    • Zero Exponent: a⁰ = 1 (for any a ≠ 0)
    • Negative Exponent (Reciprocal): a⁻ⁿ = 1 / aⁿ and (a/b)⁻ⁿ = (b/a)ⁿ
  • Scientific Notation (Standard Form): Expressing numbers as k × 10ᵐ where 1 ≤ k < 10 and m is an integer.
  • Cyclicity of Units Digits:
    • Powers of 2 repeat units digits in cycles of 4: 2, 4, 8, 6.
    • Powers of 3 repeat in cycles of 4: 3, 9, 7, 1.
    • Powers of 7 repeat in cycles of 4: 7, 9, 3, 1.
    • Powers of 8 repeat in cycles of 4: 8, 4, 2, 6.
    • Powers of 4 repeat in cycles of 2: 4, 6. Powers of 9 repeat in cycles of 2: 9, 1.

📌 Key Geometric Theorems & Properties of Quadrilaterals
  • Polygons: A simple closed curve made up of only line segments.
    • Convex Polygon: All interior angles are strictly less than 180°, and line segments joining any two internal points lie entirely inside the polygon.
    • Concave Polygon: At least one interior angle is reflex (> 180°).
    • Regular Polygon: Both equilateral (all sides equal) and equiangular (all interior angles equal).
  • Fundamental Angle Theorems:
    • Interior Angle Sum: For an n-sided polygon, Sum = (n − 2) × 180°. For a quadrilateral (n = 4), Sum = (4 − 2) × 180° = 360°.
    • Exterior Angle Sum: Sum of the exterior angles of any convex polygon is always 360°.
    • Each exterior angle of a regular n-sided polygon = 360° / n. Number of sides = 360° / (Exterior angle).
  • Family of Quadrilaterals:
    • Trapezium: Quadrilateral having at least one pair of parallel sides.
    • Parallelogram: Both pairs of opposite sides are parallel and equal. Opposite angles are equal; adjacent angles are supplementary (sum = 180°); diagonals bisect each other.
    • Rhombus: Parallelogram with all 4 sides equal. Diagonals are perpendicular bisectors of each other.
    • Rectangle: Parallelogram with four right angles (90°). Diagonals are equal in length and bisect each other.
    • Square: Regular quadrilateral (both rhombus and rectangle). All sides equal, all angles 90°, diagonals equal and perpendicular bisectors.
    • Kite: Has two pairs of equal adjacent sides. Diagonals are perpendicular, and one diagonal bisects the other.

📌 Key Algebraic Frameworks & Divisibility Theorems
  • Generalized Form of Numbers:
    • A two-digit number with tens digit a and units digit b is expressed as ab = 10a + b. Reversing its digits gives ba = 10b + a.
    • A three-digit number with hundreds digit a, tens digit b, and units digit c is abc = 100a + 10b + c.
  • Digit Reversal Properties:
    • Sum of 2-digit reversals: (10a + b) + (10b + a) = 11(a + b) — Always divisible by 11 and (a + b).
    • Difference of 2-digit reversals: (10a + b) − (10b + a) = 9(a − b) (if a > b) — Always divisible by 9 and (a − b).
    • Difference of 3-digit reversals: (100a + 10b + c) − (100c + 10b + a) = 99(a − c) — Always divisible by 99, 33, 11, 9, 3, and (a − c).
    • Cyclic Sum: abc + bca + cab = 111(a + b + c) = 37 × 3 × (a + b + c) — Always divisible by 111, 37, 3, and (a + b + c).
  • Divisibility Tests (Algebraic Derivation):
    • Divisibility by 10, 5, 2: Depends solely on units digit b (because 10a is a multiple of 10, 5, and 2).
    • Divisibility by 9 and 3: 100a + 10b + c = 99a + 9b + (a + b + c) = 9(11a + b) + (a + b + c). Hence divisible if and only if sum of digits (a + b + c) is divisible by 9 (or 3).
    • Divisibility by 11: 100a + 10b + c = (99 + 1)a + (11 − 1)b + c = (99a + 11b) + (a − b + c). Divisible if difference between sum of odd-placed digits and even-placed digits is 0 or a multiple of 11.
  • Cryptarithms (Letters for Digits): Puzzles where alphabetic letters replace numerical digits. Rules: (1) Each letter corresponds to a unique digit (0–9), (2) The first digit cannot be zero.

📌 Key Mathematical Principles of Proportion
  • Direct Proportion: Two quantities x and y are said to be in direct proportion if they increase or decrease together such that their ratio remains constant:
    • x / y = k (where k is a positive constant) or x₁ / y₁ = x₂ / y₂.
    • Graphically, a directly proportional relationship is represented by a straight line passing through the origin (0, 0).
    • Common examples: Distance travelled at constant speed vs time; cost of items vs number of items; length of an object vs length of its shadow at a fixed time.
  • Inverse Proportion: Two quantities x and y are said to be in inverse proportion if an increase in x causes a proportional decrease in y (and vice-versa) such that their product remains constant:
    • x × y = k (where k is a constant) or x₁ × y₁ = x₂ × y₂.
    • Graphically, an inverse relationship is represented by a smooth hyperbola curve in the first quadrant.
    • Common examples: Speed vs time for a fixed distance; number of workers vs days required to complete a job; number of pipes vs time taken to fill a tank.

📌 Key Decimal Representations & Rational Number Properties
  • Rational Numbers: Any number that can be expressed as p/q where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.
    • Terminating Decimals: The decimal terminates after a finite number of digits. A simplified fraction p/q terminates if and only if the prime factorization of q contains only powers of 2 and/or 5 (i.e., q = 2ⁿ × 5ᵐ).
    • Non-Terminating Repeating (Recurring) Decimals: The division continues infinitely with a repeating block of digits (the period). Every repeating decimal represents a rational number.
  • Algorithm for Converting Recurring Decimals into p/q Form:
    1. Let x be the given repeating decimal (e.g., x = 0.3̄).
    2. Multiply x by a power of 10 equal to the number of non-repeating decimal places to shift the repeating block right after the decimal point.
    3. Multiply by 10ᵖ (where p is the period length) to align identical repeating decimal tails.
    4. Subtract the equations so that the recurring fractional parts cancel out completely.
    5. Solve for x and reduce the fraction to lowest terms.
  • Density Property of Rational Numbers: Between any two distinct rational numbers, there are infinitely many rational numbers. They can be found using the Mean Method (a + b) / 2 or by equating denominators.

📌 Historical Heritage & Geometric Formulation
  • Baudhāyana's Śulbasūtra Theorem (c. 800 BCE):

    "The rope stretched along the length of the diagonal of a rectangle produces an area which the vertical and horizontal sides make together."
    In modern algebraic notation: Diagonal² = Length² + Breadth².

  • Pythagoras Theorem: In any right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse (the longest side opposite the right angle) is equal to the sum of the squares of the other two sides:
    • Hypotenuse² = Base² + Perpendicular² (or c² = a² + b²).
  • Converse of the Theorem: If the lengths of the three sides of a triangle satisfy a² + b² = c², then the triangle is necessarily a right-angled triangle, with the right angle located opposite side c.
  • Pythagorean Triplets: A set of three positive integers (a, b, c) satisfying a² + b² = c².
    • Primitive Triplets: (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (7, 24, 25), (8, 15, 17), (9, 40, 41), (11, 60, 61), (12, 35, 37), (20, 21, 29).
    • For any natural number m > 1: (2m)² + (m² − 1)² = (m² + 1)² generates a Pythagorean triplet.

📌 Necessary Conditions for Unique Geometric Construction

📌 Key Coordinate Geometry Principles & Graphing Rules
  • Cartesian Coordinate System: A 2D planar system created by two mutually perpendicular number lines intersecting at the Origin O(0, 0):
    • X-axis: The horizontal number line.
    • Y-axis: The vertical number line.
    • The two axes divide the plane into four Quadrants (I, II, III, IV).
  • Ordered Pair (x, y):
    • The first coordinate x is the abscissa (perpendicular distance from the y-axis).
    • The second coordinate y is the ordinate (perpendicular distance from the x-axis).
    • Points lying on the x-axis always have ordinate y = 0 (form: (x, 0)).
    • Points lying on the y-axis always have abscissa x = 0 (form: (0, y)).
  • Linear Graphs: A line graph in which all data points lie on a single continuous straight line.
    • Direct proportional relationships (e.g., Perimeter of square P = 4s, Cost = k × Quantity) always form straight linear graphs passing through the origin.
    • Non-linear relations (e.g., Area of square A = s²) form parabolic curves, not straight lines.

📌 Key Algebraic Solving Techniques & Factorisation Methods
  • Linear Equations in One Variable: An algebraic equation of degree 1 with only one variable, of the general form ax + b = cx + d (where a ≠ c).

    • Common Factor Method: Extract the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of all terms: ka + kb = k(a + b).

    • Using Identities:
      • a² + 2ab + b² = (a + b)²
      • a² − 2ab + b² = (a − b)²

    • Splitting the Middle Term: For quadratic trinomials x² + px + q, find two numbers a and b such that a + b = p and ab = q. Then x² + px + q = (x + a)(x + b).
