The Mughals (16th to 17th Century)

📜 Our Pasts – II (Chapter 4) — Babur to Aurangzeb: Mansabdari, Jagirdari, Zabt, Akbar Nama, and Sulh-i Kul

Chapter Summary & Key Historical/Geographical Concepts
The Mughals were descendants of two great lineages: Genghis Khan on the mother's side and Timur on the father's side. Babur founded the empire in 1526 by defeating Ibrahim Lodi at Panipat using cannons and matchlocks. Following Humayun's turbulent reign and exile by Sher Shah Suri, Akbar (1556–1605) consolidated the empire into an enduring imperial apparatus. The Mughal tradition of succession followed coparcenary inheritance (sharing authority among brothers) rather than primogeniture. Akbar forged alliances with Rajput houses by marrying Rajput princesses and granting their rulers high administrative positions (e.g., Raja Man Singh). The administrative bedrock was the Mansabdari system, where every military-bureaucratic officer was assigned a 'mansab' (rank) graded by 'Zat' (determining personal status and salary) and 'Sawar' (specifying the number of cavalrymen to maintain). Mansabdars were paid through revenue assignments called 'Jagirs', collected by their agents without direct territorial ownership. Akbar's revenue minister Todar Mal established the 'Zabt' system, conducting careful 10-year land yield surveys (1570–1580) and fixing cash tax rates per crop. Abul Fazl penned the three-volume 'Akbar Nama' and 'Ain-i Akbari', documenting administration, army, revenues, and ethnography. At Fatehpur Sikri, Akbar held philosophical discussions at the Ibadat Khana with ulema, priests, and Zoroastrians, leading him to formulate the doctrine of 'Sulh-i Kul' (universal peace and tolerance).
🎯 Central Theme: The architecture of imperial consolidation: fiscal systems, noble integration, and ideological synthesis under Akbar.

Intext Question — Page 49
What was the difference between Mughal coparcenary inheritance and primogeniture?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
  • Primogeniture: A succession custom where the eldest son inherits the entire father's estate, title, and kingdom.
  • Mughal Coparcenary Inheritance: The Timurid custom where the inheritance, sovereignty, and empire were partitioned equally among all surviving sons. This custom frequently triggered fierce civil wars of succession among royal brothers upon a Sultan's demise.

What were the central provinces under the control of the Mughals?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
The core imperial provinces (subas) under direct Mughal control included:
  • Delhi and Agra (the twin imperial capitals).
  • Lahore and Multan (in the north-west).
  • Awadh and Allahabad (in the Gangetic heartland).
  • Gujarat, Malwa, and Ajmer (trade and strategic gateways).
  • Bengal (in the east) and later Deccan subas (Khandesh, Berar, Ahmadnagar).

Let's Discuss — Q4
What was the relationship between the mansabdar and the jagir?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
The relationship between a mansabdar and a jagir had specific institutional characteristics:
  1. Revenue Assignment vs Ownership: A jagir was a revenue assignment given to a mansabdar as salary. Mansabdars did not reside in or govern their jagirs; they merely possessed the legal right to collect the assessed revenue produced by that land.
  2. Collection by Servants: Revenue was collected from the cultivators by the mansabdar's agents (servants), while the mansabdars themselves served on military campaigns in distant provinces of the empire.
  3. Strict Assessment (Under Akbar): Jagirs were carefully assessed so that the revenue yield (jama) roughly equaled the mansabdar's sanctioned salary (based on his zat and sawar rank).

Let's Discuss — Q5
What was the role of the zamindar in Mughal administration?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
In Mughal terminology, the word zamindar was an umbrella term used to describe all rural intermediaries—ranging from powerful local hereditary village headmen (muqaddam) to prominent autonomous chieftains:
  • Revenue Intermediary: Zamindars were responsible for collecting land tax directly from the peasant cultivators and remitting the imperial treasury's share to the state.
  • Local Law & Order: Maintained armed retainers (cavalry, infantry, artillery) to enforce order and protect agricultural villages against bandits.
  • Political Leverage: Possessed enormous customary power in rural society. If imperial revenue demands became oppressive, zamindars frequently organized and led peasant rebellions against Mughal authorities.

Let's Discuss — Q6
How were the debates with religious scholars important in the formation of Akbar’s ideas on governance?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
Akbar held intense theological debates in the Ibadat Khana at Fatehpur Sikri with scholars of diverse faiths—including Muslim ulema, Hindu pandits, Roman Catholic Jesuit priests, Zoroastrians (Parsis), and Jaina monks:
  1. Realization of Dogmatism: Akbar observed that orthodox theologians often displayed extreme bigotry, insisted on narrow rituals, and created religious friction among citizens.
  2. Formulation of Sulh-i Kul: This disillusionment led Akbar to abandon sectarian bias and champion the philosophical principle of Sulh-i Kul ('universal peace' or absolute tolerance).
  3. Universal Ethics in Governance: Instead of imposing one religion upon his diverse subjects, Akbar centered his administrative system on universal moral values: justice, peace, and honesty. This policy enabled the empire to unite millions across differing faiths.

Competency-Based Fiscal Analysis: Todar Mal's Zabt System
How did Raja Todar Mal's 'Zabt' land revenue system balance the fiscal needs of the Mughal imperial treasury with agricultural fairness for the peasantry?

Todar Mal's Zabt was an extraordinarily modern econometric system based on empirical data:

  • Empirical 10-Year Survey: Between 1570 and 1580, royal surveyors meticulously recorded crop yields, market prices, and cultivated acreage across every suba.
  • Fixed Fair Cash Assessment: Tax was not an arbitrary whim; it was fixed scientifically at one-third (1/3) of the average produce, converted into uniform cash rates per crop.
  • Protection Against Volatility: Because individual tax schedules were published for every revenue circle, corrupt tax collectors could not extort arbitrary surcharges, providing peasants fiscal predictability and incentivizing them to cultivate lucrative cash crops like cotton and sugarcane.
