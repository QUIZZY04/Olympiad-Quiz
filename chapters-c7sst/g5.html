Water

🌍 Our Environment (Chapter 5) — The hydrological cycle, water distribution, waves, tsunamis, tides, and ocean currents

Chapter Summary & Key Historical/Geographical Concepts
Water is an indispensable resource that constantly changes its form and circulates between oceans, atmosphere, and land through the Hydrological Cycle (evaporation, condensation, precipitation, runoff). The Earth is like a terrarium: the same water that existed centuries ago still exists today. About three-fourths (71%) of the Earth's surface is covered with water, but 97.3% of it is saline ocean water unfit for human consumption. Freshwater accounts for only 2.7%, of which the vast majority is locked in ice caps and glaciers (2.0%) or deep groundwater (0.68%), leaving less than 1% readily accessible in freshwater lakes, rivers, and atmosphere. Ocean waters are in constant motion through three primary movements: 1) Waves: Formed when winds scrape across the ocean surface; underwater earthquakes, landslides, or volcanic eruptions trigger giant seismic sea waves called Tsunamis (exemplified by the devastating December 26, 2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami which submerged the Indira Point); 2) Tides: The rhythmic rise and fall of ocean water twice a day caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun. High spring tides occur during New Moon and Full Moon when Sun, Moon, and Earth align in a straight line; low neap tides occur during the first and third quarters when solar and lunar gravity act at right angles; 3) Ocean Currents: Streams of water flowing constantly on the ocean surface in definite directions. Warm currents (like the Gulf Stream) originate near the Equator and flow poleward, warming coastal climates; Cold currents (like the Labrador Current) carry polar water towards lower latitudes. Where warm and cold currents mix, they create nutrient-rich phytoplankton zones that form the world's richest fishing grounds (e.g., seas around Japan and eastern North America), though they also produce hazardous navigational fogs.
🎯 Central Theme: The global circulation of water and the gravitational, oceanic, and meteorological mechanisms driving marine systems.

Intext Question — Page 34
Why are the areas where warm and cold ocean currents meet the best fishing grounds in the world?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
The mixing of warm and cold ocean currents generates upwelling that brings deep mineral nutrients to the sunlit surface. This fosters the massive proliferation of phytoplankton (microscopic marine plants), which is the primary food supply for millions of fish. Consequently, commercial marine life concentrates in these zones, creating the world's richest fishing grounds (e.g., around Japan and Newfoundland).

Exercises — Q1 (i to vi)
Answer the following questions:
(i) What is precipitation?
(ii) What is water cycle?
(iii) What are the factors affecting the height of the waves?
(iv) Which factors affect the movement of ocean water?
(v) What are tides and how are they caused?
(vi) What are ocean currents?
6 Marks
Detailed Solution
(i) Precipitation: The falling of condensed atmospheric water vapor onto the Earth's surface in the form of rain, snow, sleet, or hail.

(ii) Water Cycle: The continuous, unbroken process by which water circulates between the hydrosphere, atmosphere, and lithosphere through evaporation, transpiration, condensation, and precipitation.

(iii) Factors Affecting Height of Waves:
  • Wind velocity (speed of the wind scraping the ocean surface).
  • Duration of the wind blowing over the water.
  • The distance of open water over which the wind blows (fetch).
  • Undersea earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, or underwater landslides (triggering catastrophic tsunamis).
(iv) Factors Affecting Ocean Water Movement: Temperature variations, salinity differences, planetary wind patterns, the Coriolis effect (Earth's rotation), and the gravitational pull of the Sun and Moon.

(v) Tides & Their Cause: The rhythmic rise and fall of ocean water twice a day. Tides are caused by the powerful gravitational pull exerted by the Moon and the Sun on the Earth's surface.

(vi) Ocean Currents: Continuous, predictable, directional streams of seawater flowing horizontally across the ocean surface over thousands of kilometers, categorized as warm currents or cold currents.

Exercises — Q2 (Give Reasons)
Give reasons:
(i) Ocean water is salty.
(ii) The quality of water is deteriorating.
4 Marks
Detailed Solution
(i) Ocean water is salty because:
Over millions of years, continental rainwater and rivers wash dissolved mineral salts (primarily sodium chloride, ordinary table salt) from surface rocks and carry them into the oceans. Furthermore, undersea volcanic vents and hydrothermal fissures release volcanic salts directly into seawater. Because water evaporates from the ocean while minerals remain behind, salt concentrates permanently, giving ocean water an average salinity of 35 parts per thousand.

(ii) The quality of water is deteriorating because:
Human activities are dumping untreated industrial chemical effluents, raw sewage, and municipal waste directly into freshwater rivers and lakes. Furthermore, the excessive runoff of chemical fertilizers and agricultural pesticides into groundwater aquifers, combined with plastic pollution and deforestation, contaminates freshwater sources, rendering them hazardous for human consumption and aquatic life.

Competency-Based Oceanographic Analysis: Spring vs Neap Tides
Using lunar and solar gravitational mechanics, explain why spring tides produce the highest tidal range while neap tides produce the lowest tidal range.

Tidal amplitude depends directly on the orbital geometry of the Sun, Moon, and Earth:

  • Spring Tides (Maximum Range): Occur twice a lunar month—during the New Moon (conjunction) and Full Moon (opposition). In these phases, the gravitational attraction of the Sun and the Moon align along the same axis (syzygy), combining their gravitational forces to pull ocean waters higher, producing extraordinarily high high-tides and very low low-tides.
  • Neap Tides (Minimum Range): Occur during the Moon's first and third quarters (quadrature). The gravitational pull of the Sun and Moon act at right angles (90°) to each other. Solar gravity partially cancels lunar gravity, resulting in weak high-tides and modest low-tides.
