Delhi: 12th to 15th Century

📜 Our Pasts – II (Chapter 3) — The Delhi Sultanate: From Tomara Rajputs to Khaljis, Tughluqs, and Sayyids

Chapter Summary & Key Historical/Geographical Concepts
Delhi first emerged as a political capital under the Tomara Rajputs, who were defeated by the Chauhans of Ajmer in the 12th century. Under the Chauhans and Tomaras, Delhi became an important commercial center inhabited by wealthy Jaina merchants who minted coins called 'dehliwal'. The Delhi Sultanate was founded in 1206 CE following the Ghorian conquests. Five successive dynasties ruled: the Early Turkish / Mamluk Sultans (1206–1290), the Khaljis (1290–1320), the Tughluqs (1320–1414), the Sayyids (1414–1451), and the Lodis (1451–1526). Historians reconstruct Sultanate history through Persian court chronicles called 'Tawarikh' written by learned secretaries, administrators, and poets who advised kings on the 'Circle of Justice'. Notable rulers included Raziyya Sultana (1236–1240), who faced severe patriarchal resistance from chroniclers like Minhaj-i Siraj and Turkish nobles because she was a woman. Under Alauddin Khalji and Muhammad Tughluq, the Sultanate expanded beyond fortified garrison towns (hinterland control) into the deep South (external frontier). To administer this vast territory, Sultans appointed military commanders as governors of territories called 'Iqtas' (their holders known as 'Iqtadars' or 'Muqtis'). Defending against relentless Mongol onslaughts led by Genghis Khan necessitated massive standing armies, price control systems (Alauddin Khalji), and radical administrative experiments such as the token bronze currency and capital transfer to Daulatabad (Muhammad Tughluq).
🎯 Central Theme: The transformation of Delhi from a fortified commercial outpost into an imperial capital through military expansion and administrative centralization.

Intext Question — Page 33
What was the 'Circle of Justice' as described by chronicler Fakhr-i Mudabbir?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
The Circle of Justice explained the mutual interdependence between monarch, soldiers, and peasants:
  1. A king cannot survive without an army of soldiers.
  2. Soldiers cannot live without salaries.
  3. Salaries come from the revenue collected from peasants.
  4. Peasants can pay revenue only when they are prosperous and happy.
  5. This happens only when the king promotes justice and equitable, honest governance.

Explaining the unbroken chain of interdependence: King -> Army -> Revenue -> Peasants -> Justice 2 Marks
Intext Question — Page 36
Why did chronicler Minhaj-i Siraj believe that Raziyya's rule went against the ideal social order?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
Minhaj-i Siraj acknowledged that Raziyya was more talented, courageous, and capable than all her brothers. However, he believed that a woman ruler violated the divine social order created by God, in which women were naturally subordinate to men. In his view, a female monarch disrupted male aristocratic hierarchy, and Turkish nobles resented being commanded by a woman.

Let's Discuss — Q5
What were the duties of the muqtis, and what steps were taken to ensure that they performed their duties?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
1. Duties of Muqtis / Iqtadars:
  • Lead military campaigns on behalf of the Sultan.
  • Maintain law, order, and defense in their assigned administrative territory (iqta).
  • Collect the state-assessed land revenue from the peasants of their iqta, from which they paid their soldiers' salaries and maintained their cavalry quota.
2. Control Measures Enforced by Sultans:
  • Non-Hereditary Office: Iqtas were strictly not hereditary; officers were transferred frequently from one iqta to another.
  • Auditing Accountants: Special royal accountants (mutasarrif) were dispatched by the central Diwan to inspect records and verify that only state-prescribed taxes were collected.
  • Troop Muster Inspections: The number of soldiers and horses maintained by each muqti was strictly audited using branding systems (dagh and chihra).

Let's Discuss — Q6
Compare the administrative measures of Alauddin Khalji and Muhammad Tughluq in dealing with the Mongol threat.
5 Marks
Detailed Solution
Aspect Alauddin Khalji (1296–1316) Muhammad Tughluq (1324–1351)
Strategy Defensive strategy: Strengthened borders, constructed new fortresses, and stationed garrison troops at Siri. Aggressive strategy: Defeated the Mongol army early in his reign, and planned an ambitious invasion of Transoxiana.
Troop Stationing Constructed an entirely new garrison city named Siri for his vast standing army. Evacuated the residents of the oldest city of Delhi (Delhi-i Kuhna) and sent them to Daulatabad; turned the city into a soldiers' camp.
Feeding Troops Imposed land tax on agricultural lands between Ganga & Yamuna fixed at 50% of the produce to feed his army. Levied heavy additional taxes in the Doab region during a severe famine, sparking widespread peasant rebellions.
Payment of Soldiers Paid soldiers cash salaries and instituted strict Market Price Controls in Delhi, punishing hoarders. Paid cash salaries but introduced a Token Currency made of cheap brass and copper coins without royal mint safeguards.
Outcome Highly successful: Kept Mongol invasions at bay, maintained stable prices, and left a secure treasury. Administrative failure: The token currency was widely counterfeited and had to be recalled, Daulatabad capital move was abandoned.

Competency-Based Case Study: Monetary Experiments in History
Why is Muhammad Tughluq's token currency regarded by modern economists as a brilliant monetary idea undermined by primitive institutional security?

Muhammad Tughluq's token currency was centuries ahead of its time, yet collapsed due to systemic design flaws:

  • Theoretical Genius: Just like modern paper fiat currency, Tughluq realized that money does not need intrinsic metal value (gold/silver); an economy can function on cheap token metal (copper/brass) backed by the sovereign decree of the state. This would conserve gold and silver for international trade.
  • Execution Disaster: The state possessed no secret watermarks, anti-counterfeiting mint technology, or state monopoly on coining. Every goldsmith and householder minted fake copper coins from household utensils, paid their royal taxes in worthless token money, and hoarded genuine gold and silver tangas. The market crashed, proving that advanced monetary policy fails without robust institutional verification.
