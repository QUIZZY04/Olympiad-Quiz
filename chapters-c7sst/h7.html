H7

The Making of Regional Cultures

📜 Our Pasts – II (Chapter 7) — Language, temple cults, Rajput heroic sagas, Kathak dance, and miniature paintings

Chapter Summary & Key Historical/Geographical Concepts
Regional cultures today are often defined by specific languages, dances, cuisines, and attire, which developed through centuries of cultural exchange and royal patronage. In the South, the Chera kingdom of Mahodayapuram (present-day Kerala) incorporated the regional language Malayalam into official royal inscriptions in the 9th century, drawing upon Sanskrit literary traditions (exemplified by the 14th-century grammar work 'Lilatilakam' written in Manipravalam, a linguistic blend of Malayalam and Sanskrit). In Orissa, local religious traditions merged with imperial authority around the Jagannatha cult of Puri; King Anangabhima III dedicated his entire kingdom to Lord Jagannatha and proclaimed himself the deity's deputy. In Rajasthan, Rajput rulers fostered romantic and heroic ballads extolling warriors who fought valiantly, preferred death over surrender, and celebrated widows committing Sati. Classical dance forms evolved from temple storytelling into sophisticated court arts, exemplified by Kathak migrating from temples of North India to the courts of the Mughals and Wajid Ali Shah of Awadh, crystalizing into Lucknow and Jaipur gharanas. Miniature painting traditions flourished from Mughal atelier workshops to the Himalayan foothills, giving birth to the bold Basohli school and the lyrical, pastoral Kangra school of Pahari painting. In Bengal, regional identity coalesced around the Bengali language, terracotta temples, Mangalakavyas, and the cultural dietary acceptance of fish among Bengali Brahmanas.
🎯 Central Theme: How regional identities—languages, cuisines, performance arts, and religious traditions—developed through cross-cultural synthesis across the subcontinent.

2 Marks
Detailed Solution

Let's Recall — Q2
What is Kathak? Trace its historical evolution into a classical dance form.
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
1. Meaning & Origin: The word Kathak is derived from katha (story). It originated as a caste of storytellers in north Indian temples who narrated mythological epics with expressive gestures and songs.
2. Evolution Under Bhakti Movement: During the 15th and 16th centuries, with the rise of the Radha-Krishna Bhakti movement, folk plays called rasa lila blended Kathak gestures with devotional enactments.
3. Mughal Court Transformation: Under Mughal emperors, Kathak was performed in royal courts, acquiring its distinctive technical virtuosity—spectacular rapid pirouettes (chakkars), lightning footwork (tatkar), and subtle facial expressions (abhinaya).
4. Development of Gharanas: It developed into two major traditions: the Jaipur gharana (focusing on intricate footwork and rhythm) and the Lucknow gharana (refined under Nawab Wajid Ali Shah, focusing on lyrical elegance and emotion).

Let's Discuss — Q3
What were the important architectural features of the temples of Bengal?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
The medieval temples of Bengal developed a distinctive vernacular architectural style:
  1. Thatched Roof Imitation: Architects copied the curved double-roofed (dochala) or four-roofed (chauchala) bamboo huts of Bengali villages.
  2. Chauchala Structure: In a four-roofed structure, four triangular roofs converge on a curved central ridge or spire.
  3. Square Base & Brick Construction: Temples were built on a square platform with relatively plain interior chambers.
  4. Terracotta Plaques: Exterior walls were lavishly decorated with terracotta tiles and carved brick plaques depicting scenes from the Ramayana, Mahabharata, daily life, and Krishna's antics.

Let's Discuss — Q4
Why did conquerors try to control the temple of Jagannatha at Puri?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
Conquerors (Mughals, Marathas, and the English East India Company) systematically sought control over the Jagannatha temple at Puri because:
  • Center of Pilgrimage & Legitimacy: The temple was the supreme pilgrimage center of eastern India. Rulers who controlled the shrine gained instant moral and political legitimacy among the local Oriya population.
  • Imposition of Imperial Rule: The local populace believed that true sovereignty over Orissa belonged to Lord Jagannatha; hence, ruling the temple made colonial or conqueror authority acceptable to the subjects.
  • Colonial Revenue: The temple attracted immense pilgrim traffic, generating massive pilgrim taxes and trade revenues for the governing authority.

Competency-Based Case Study: Dietary Exceptions in Cultural History
Why did orthodox Brahmanas in Bengal consume fish, despite strict Vedic dietary prohibitions against eating non-vegetarian food?

Cultural and dietary norms are shaped profoundly by local geography and ecology:

  • Ecological Reality: Bengal is a riverine delta crisscrossed by thousands of rivers, wetlands, and creeks. Fish and rice formed the indispensable, plentiful staple diet of the entire regional population.
  • Accommodation through Sacred Texts: Strict enforcement of Vedic vegetarianism would have alienated the entire population. Therefore, local Brahmana scholars incorporated special dispensations into regional legal texts—notably the 13th-century Sanskrit text Brihaddharma Purana—explicitly permitting Bengali Brahmanas to consume specific varieties of clean river fish. This demonstrates how religious codes adapt to regional ecological imperatives.
