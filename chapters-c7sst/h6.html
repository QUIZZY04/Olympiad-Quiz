Devotional Paths to the Divine

📜 Our Pasts – II (Chapter 6) — Bhakti traditions, Nayanars, Alvars, Shankara, Virashaivism, Maharashtra saints, Sufism, and Kabir

Chapter Summary & Key Historical/Geographical Concepts
Between the 7th and 17th centuries, widespread religious reform movements swept the subcontinent, challenging rigid Brahmanical caste hierarchies and complex Vedic animal sacrifices. In South India, the Bhakti revolution was spearheaded by Tamil poet-saints: the Nayanars (devoted to Shiva, numbering 63) and the Alvars (devoted to Vishnu, numbering 12), including figures from lower castes like Appar and Andal. Their ecstatic songs were compiled in the 'Tevaram' and 'Divya Prabandham'. Philosopher Shankara (born in Kerala) preached Advaita (monism/oneness of the individual soul with the Supreme Brahman), while Ramanuja (born in Tamil Nadu) expounded Vishishtadvaita (qualified monism), teaching that intense devotion to Vishnu yields salvation. In 12th-century Karnataka, Basavanna, Allama Prabhu, and Akkamahadevi led the Virashaiva (Lingayat) movement, rejecting caste discrimination, idolatry, and the subjugation of women through vernacular 'vachanas'. In Maharashtra, saints of Pandharpur (Jnaneshwar, Namdev, Eknath, Tukaram, Sakkubai) centered devotion on Lord Vitthala, rejecting ascetic withdrawal in favor of living as ordinary householders serving humanity. In North India, Sufi mystics (Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti) taught love for God through music and meditation in hospice centers ('khanqahs'). Reformer-poets like Kabir and Baba Guru Nanak rejected external rituals, caste prejudice, and polytheism, preaching devotion to a formless Supreme Divine, while Mirabai dedicated her life to Lord Krishna.
🎯 Central Theme: The democratization of religious devotion through popular vernacular poetry, personal communion with God, and resistance to ritual orthodoxy.

Intext Question — Page 85
What were the major teachings of Baba Guru Nanak?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
Baba Guru Nanak's core spiritual and social teachings include:
  1. Monotheism (Ik Onkar): Insisted on the worship of one formless, timeless Supreme God (Nirgun Bhakti).
  2. Rejection of External Piety: Denounced caste distinctions, religious orthodoxy, gender inequality, and ascetic penances. True religion consists of pure living amidst worldly responsibilities.
  3. Threefold Pillar of Life:
    • Nam-Japna: Continuous remembrance and meditation on God's divine name.
    • Kirt-Karna: Earning an honest, lawful living through industrious labor.
    • Vand-Chhakkna: Sharing one's earnings with the needy and eating together in communal community kitchens (langar).

Let's Discuss — Q3
Describe the beliefs and practices of the Nathpanthis, Siddhas and Yogis.
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
The Nathpanthis, Siddhacharyas, and Yogis rejected conventional orthodox religion:
  1. Critique of Social Order: They fiercely criticized the ritualism and caste supremacy of orthodox religion using simple, logical arguments in local vernacular tongues.
  2. Path of Renunciation: Advocated complete renunciation of the material world, arguing that the path to salvation lay in intense meditation on the formless Ultimate Reality.
  3. Yogic Discipline: Developed rigorous psychosomatic training—including asanas (postures), pranayama (breath control exercises), and deep meditation—to discipline the mind and body.
  4. Popular Base: Because they rejected caste divisions, their teachings gained immense popularity among lower-caste groups, creating fertile ground for devotional religion.

Let's Discuss — Q4
What were the major ideas expressed by Kabir? How were these expressed?
4 Marks
Detailed Solution
1. Major Ideas of Kabir:
  • Formless Supreme God: Kabir believed in a single, formless Supreme Divinity (Nirguna Bhakti) accessible solely through genuine, loving personal devotion.
  • Rejection of Religious Orthodoxy: Scorned the external rituals of both Hinduism and Islam—including idol worship, pilgrimages, fasting, bathing in holy rivers, circumcision, and sacred threads.
  • Eradication of Caste Hierarchy: Vehemently denounced caste discrimination and untouchability, asserting that all humans are born of the same divine light.
2. How These Ideas Were Expressed:
  • Kabir expressed his ideas in pithy vernacular poems—primarily sakhis (couplets) and pads (devotional songs).
  • His language was a spoken dialect of Hindi (Bhojpuri/Brajbhasha blended with Persian and Urdu words) easily understood by ordinary peasants and artisans.
  • He often used cryptic ulatbansi (upside-down sayings) that challenged listeners to look beneath superficial appearances. His verses were preserved in the Guru Granth Sahib, Kabir Bijak, and Panch Vani.

Competency-Based Comparative Analysis: Advaita vs Vishishtadvaita
Compare the metaphysical philosophies of Shankara (Advaita) and Ramanuja (Vishishtadvaita). Why did Ramanuja's philosophy exert a greater immediate appeal on popular devotional movements?

The debate between Shankara and Ramanuja represents two foundational philosophies of medieval Hinduism:

  • Shankara's Advaita (8th Century): Taught absolute non-dualism. The ultimate reality (Brahman) is formless, attribute-less (Nirguna), and impersonal. The individual soul (Atman) is entirely identical to Brahman, and the empirical physical world is an illusion (maya). Salvation comes through rigorous intellectual knowledge (Jnana Marga).
  • Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita (11th Century): Taught qualified non-dualism. While the soul is connected to Brahman, it retains an individual identity. Brahman possesses divine attributes of infinite love and grace (Saguna), personified as Lord Vishnu. Salvation comes through heartfelt, loving self-surrender (Bhakti and Prapatti).
  • Why Ramanuja had greater popular appeal: Shankara's abstract monism was difficult for common people to grasp emotionally. Ramanuja validated human feelings of love, prayer, and devotion, opening spiritual liberation to all people regardless of caste through divine grace.
