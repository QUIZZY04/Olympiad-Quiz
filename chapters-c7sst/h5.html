H5

Tribes, Nomads and Settled Communities

📜 Our Pasts – II (Chapter 5) — Pastoral migrations, the Banjaras, Gond kingdom of Garha Katanga, and the Ahom state

Chapter Summary & Key Historical/Geographical Concepts
While caste-based societies structured by the Varna system dominated river plains, vast areas of forests, hills, and deserts were inhabited by autonomous tribal societies. Unlike jati-based orders, tribal societies preserved egalitarian kinship ties, shared land collectively, and lived through hunting, gathering, shifting cultivation, or pastoralism. Pastoral nomads like the Gujjars and Gaddis moved across seasonal pastures trading milk, ghee, and wool for grain, cloth, and utensils. The Banjaras were the most vital trader-nomads, moving in giant caravans called 'tandas' transportinng grain for military armies under Alauddin Khalji and the Mughals. Over centuries, interactions between tribes and agrarian states caused mutual transformation. In Central India, the Gonds practiced shifting agriculture in the vast forested region of Gondwana. The kingdom of Garha Katanga became wealthy through exporting wild elephants; Rani Durgawati led heroic resistance against Akbar's general Asaf Khan in 1565. In the north-east, the Ahoms migrated from present-day Myanmar in the 13th century into the Brahmaputra valley. They built a powerful state using firearms, relied on forced labor called 'paiks', organized society into clans ('khels'), and composed unique historical chronicles called 'buranjis'.
🎯 Central Theme: The interaction, conflict, and integration between caste-based agrarian societies and autonomous tribal communities.

Intext Question — Page 69
Who were the Banjaras, and why were they crucial to the medieval Indian economy and military?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
The Banjaras were the most prominent nomadic trader-transporters of medieval India:
  • They traveled in massive caravans called tandas, which sometimes numbered over 20,000 to 100,000 bullocks loaded with grain, pulses, and salt.
  • Commercial Link: They purchased grain cheaply in rural farming villages and transported it to high-demand urban markets, evening out regional food supplies.
  • Military Supply Chain: During major campaigns under Alauddin Khalji and the Mughal emperors, Banjaras were hired to transport provisions, rations, and weapons for the entire imperial army marching into hostile enemy territory.

Let's Discuss — Q3
Were the Banjaras important for the economy? Explain.
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
Yes, the Banjaras were vital for the medieval economy:
  1. Inter-Regional Trade: They linked remote agricultural villages with busy city markets by transporting massive bulk commodities (grain, salt, iron) that ordinary sedentary merchants could not transport across long distances.
  2. Mitigating Scarcity: During localized droughts or crop failures, Banjaras quickly moved food grains from surplus zones to famine-struck regions, stabilizing market prices.
  3. Military Lifeline: Emperor Jahangir noted in his memoirs that the Banjaras carried grain on bullocks from different areas and sold it in towns, feeding royal armies of up to 100,000 soldiers on campaign.

Let's Discuss — Q4
In what ways was the history of the Gonds different from that of the Ahoms? Were there any similarities?
5 Marks
Detailed Solution
Key Differences:
  • Location & Origin: The Gonds lived in the vast central Indian plateau of Gondwana (Garha Katanga) practicing shifting agriculture. The Ahoms migrated from Myanmar in the 13th century and settled in the fertile Brahmaputra river valley of Assam.
  • Military Technology: The Gonds relied on traditional weapons and wild elephants. The Ahoms adopted cutting-edge gunpowder technology early (by the 1530s) and manufactured high-quality gunpowder and cannons.
  • Administrative Labor System: The Gonds organized territory into garhs (each of 84 villages called chaurasi, subdivided into barhots of 12 villages). The Ahom state was built on the paik system—forced labor where each village had to send a rotational quota of men for state works, army, and dam building.
  • Historical Chronicles: The Ahoms maintained meticulous chronological court histories called Buranjis (written first in Ahom language, then in Assamese), a tradition not found among the Gonds.
Key Similarities:
  • Both were originally autonomous tribal societies that evolved into powerful, centralized monarchies.
  • Both kingdoms absorbed Hindu influences, adopted Hindu religious deities, and patronized learned Brahmanas with royal land grants.
  • Both kingdoms eventually came into armed conflict with the expanding Mughal Empire and suffered military invasions (Gonds under Asaf Khan, Ahoms under Mir Jumla).

Socio-Historical Analysis: Tribal Transformation
How did the emergence of large tribal kingdoms like Garha Katanga and the Ahom state fundamentally transform the traditional egalitarian structure of tribal society?

The growth of centralized tribal kingdoms transformed egalitarian clans into stratified, hierarchical class societies:

  • Erosion of Clan Equality: Originally, tribal clans shared land, cattle, and resources communally without rich or poor divisions. Centralized kingship concentrated wealth, land ownership, and coercive power in the hands of the royal lineage.
  • Varna Integration: Tribal kings invited Brahmana priests and granted them fertile lands (brahmadeya). In return, Brahmanas fabricated royal genealogies linking tribal chiefs to ancient Rajput dynasties (e.g., Gond king Aman Das adopting the title Sangram Shah).
  • Subordination of Ordinary Tribesmen: Common tribespeople were relegated to lower occupational jatis or subjected to compulsory forced labor (as paiks in Assam), mirroring the caste hierarchy of mainstream plains society.
