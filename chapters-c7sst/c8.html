A Shirt in the Market

⚖️ Social and Political Life – II (Chapter 8) — The journey of a cotton shirt: Swapna the farmer, Erode weavers, garment export, and unequal profits

Chapter Summary & Key Historical/Geographical Concepts
This chapter traces the complete journey of a commodity—a cotton shirt—from its origin in a farmer's field to a luxury shopping mall in the United States, exposing how market dynamics reward some while exploiting others: 1) Cotton Farmer in Kurnool (Swapna): Swapna cultivates cotton on her small landholding. Because she had to borrow 2,500 rupees at exorbitant interest from a local moneylender-trader to buy high-cost seeds, fertilizers, and pesticides, she was bound by contract to sell all her harvested cotton to him. The trader pays her far below the competitive open market price, leaving her with barely 3,000 rupees for four months of backbreaking labor—barely more than a daily laborer earns; 2) The Cloth Market of Erode (Tamil Nadu): One of the largest textile markets in the world, operating on the 'Putting-out System' (Merchant-Weaver arrangement). The wealthy cloth merchant distributes raw cotton yarn to weaver families and gives them instructions on what cloth to weave; the weavers weave cloth in their home looms and deliver it back to the merchant. While weavers avoid purchasing yarn and finding buyers, they are completely dependent on the merchant, who dictates low piece-rate wages while earning massive profits; 3) Garment Exporting Factory near Delhi: A garment exporter buys cloth from merchants, cuts and stitches shirts using low-wage female laborers working long hours under intense pressure, and exports the finished shirts to a foreign multinational retail chain in the USA at 300 rupees per shirt; 4) The US Department Store: The US corporation brands, advertises, and sells each shirt for 1,800 rupees (26 dollars), pocketing immense profit margins. The chapter proves that markets do not offer fair opportunities to all: the multinational retailer and garment exporter make huge profits, while the small cotton farmer and weavers receive pitiful compensation. It highlights Weavers' Cooperatives as a vital solution to break dependence on middlemen.
🎯 Central Theme: The global commodity chain: agrarian indebtedness, the putting-out system, exploitation in garment factories, and market cooperatives.
📖 Intext Questions & Activities
Intext Question — Page 96
What is the 'Putting-out system' (Dadan system) between the merchant and the weavers? What are its advantages and disadvantages for weavers?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
The Putting-out System: An arrangement where the merchant supplies raw material (yarn) to weavers and purchases the finished woven fabric from them at a pre-agreed piece rate.

Advantages for Weavers:
  • Weavers do not have to spend capital to purchase expensive raw yarn.
  • They do not face the problem of finding buyers or selling the finished cloth in distant markets.
Severe Disadvantages for Weavers:
  • Total economic dependence on the merchant gives the merchant overwhelming bargaining power.
  • Merchants pay extremely low wages for the cloth while selling it at huge markups.
  • Weavers invest their own life savings and loans to buy expensive electric looms, yet earn barely enough to survive.

Definition: Merchant supplying yarn and receiving finished cloth 1 Mark
Advantages: No raw material capital and assured marketing 1 Mark
Disadvantages: Asymmetric power, low piece rates, and financial exploitation 1 Mark

Exercises — Q1
What made Swapna sell the cotton to the trader instead of selling at the Kurnool cotton market?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
Swapna sold her cotton to the local trader because at the beginning of the cropping season, she had taken a loan of 2,500 rupees from him at a high interest rate to purchase expensive seeds, fertilizers, and pesticides. A strict condition of the loan agreement was that Swapna was legally bound to sell all her harvested cotton exclusively to him, leaving her with no choice to seek higher competitive prices at the Kurnool open market.

Exercises — Q2
Describe the conditions of employment as well as the wages of workers in the garment exporting factory. Do you think the workers get a fair deal?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
1. Conditions of Employment & Wages:
  • Temporary / Hire-and-Fire Basis: Workers are employed temporarily on contract; if there is no work or if an order is completed, they are summarily dismissed without notice.
  • Long Working Hours: Workers endure 10 to 12-hour shifts under relentless pressure to meet tight export shipping deadlines.
  • Low Wages: Tailors receive around 3,000 rupees a month, while female helpers (who cut threads, button, iron, and pack) are paid the lowest wages in the factory (around 1,500 to 2,000 rupees).
2. Do Workers Get a Fair Deal?
No, workers absolutely do not get a fair deal. They are denied job security, overtime allowances, healthcare benefits, or fair living wages, while the factory owner and the foreign brand reap massive profits from their grueling labor.

Temporary contract, hire-and-fire conditions, and long 10-12 hour shifts 1 Mark
Low wages, especially for female helpers doing thread-cutting and ironing 1 Mark
Unequivocal judgment that workers do not receive a fair deal 1 Mark
Exercises — Q3
Think of something common that we use. It could be sugar, tea, milk, pen, paper, pencil, etc. Discuss through what chain of markets this reaches you. Can you think of the people that help in the production or trade?
4 Marks
Detailed Solution
Example: The Journey of a Packet of Milk:
  1. Primary Producers: Rural dairy farmers and cattle rearers feed, care for, and milk cows/buffaloes in villages.
  2. Village Milk Collection Center / Cooperative: Farmers deliver fresh milk daily to the local village collection center, where fat content is tested and farmers receive payment.
  3. Chilling Plant & Processing Dairy: Insulated tankers transport milk to large cooperative dairy processing plants (e.g., Amul, Mother Dairy) where milk is pasteurized, standardized (toned/full cream), and sealed in sanitary plastic pouches.
  4. City Wholesale Cold-Chain Distributors: Refrigerated trucks deliver thousands of pouches before dawn to regional wholesale depots.
  5. Neighborhood Milk Booths & Grocery Retailers: Local vendors purchase daily crates and stock them in refrigerators.
  6. Consumer: We purchase the milk packet in the morning from the neighborhood booth for our household consumption.
People Who Help: Cattle rearers, veterinary doctors, collection staff, refrigerated truck drivers, factory dairy technicians, quality control inspectors, and local retail booth operators.

Exercises — Q4
Compare who gains the most and who gains the least in the market chain of a cotton shirt.
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
The commodity chain of a shirt exhibits staggering profit inequality:
  • Who Gains the Most: The foreign multinational business corporation (US department store owner). They purchase the stitched shirt for approximately 300 rupees (USD 4) from the exporter, spend 300 rupees on shipping and advertising, and sell it for 1,800 rupees (USD 24), pocketing an immense net profit of 1,200 rupees per shirt!
  • Who Gains Moderate Profit: The garment exporter in Delhi, who earns a modest profit of 50 to 100 rupees per shirt, though carrying financial risks of canceled export orders.
  • Who Gains the Least: The small cotton farmer (Swapna) and the weavers and factory helpers. After four months of relentless labor and chemical exposure, Swapna earns barely enough to buy food, while factory women stitchers receive meager daily wages without job security.

💡 Competency-Based Case Study & HOTS (CBSE 2026-27 Pattern) Applied Social Science
Competency-Based Economic Reform Case Study: The Power of Cooperatives
How do producer cooperatives (such as Weavers' Cooperatives or Dairy Cooperatives like Amul) successfully break the exploitative grip of private middlemen and ensure fair market returns for primary producers?

Producer cooperatives provide an effective institutional counterweight to market exploitation:

  • Eliminating Middlemen Dependency: In a weavers' cooperative, weavers band together collectively. The cooperative buys yarn in bulk directly from spinning mills at wholesale prices and distributes it to members, eliminating the predatory merchant in the putting-out system.
  • Collective Marketing Power: Instead of competing against one another and being forced to accept rock-bottom prices, the cooperative sells finished cloth directly to government retail organizations (like Co-optex in Tamil Nadu) or export markets, securing top prices.
  • Equitable Wealth Sharing: All profits generated are redistributed democratically among the producer-members according to their labor contributions, transforming impoverished artisans into self-reliant, prosperous entrepreneurs.
