Eighteenth-Century Political Formations

📜 Our Pasts – II (Chapter 8) — The decline of the Mughals, successor states (Awadh, Bengal, Hyderabad), Rajputs, Sikhs, and Marathas

Chapter Summary & Key Historical/Geographical Concepts
The first half of the 18th century witnessed the decline of the mighty Mughal Empire. The decline was precipitated by Aurangzeb's long, exhausting war in the Deccan (depleting treasury and army), the breakdown of the mansabdari-jagirdari system, and the devastating sack of Delhi by Nadir Shah of Iran in 1739, followed by Afghan raids by Ahmad Shah Abdali. Out of this crisis emerged three broad categories of states: 1) Old Mughal successor provinces (Awadh under Burhan-ul-Mulk Saadat Khan, Bengal under Murshid Quli Khan, and Hyderabad under Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah) who maintained formal nominal allegiance to the Mughal emperor while seizing autonomous hereditary control, revenue contracting (ijaradari), and forging alliances with wealthy bankers and moneylenders; 2) Watan Jagirs of the Rajputs (such as Sawai Raja Jai Singh of Amber who founded Jaipur and built astronomical observatories called Jantar Mantars); 3) States that seized independence through armed rebellion: the Sikhs under Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa and Banda Singh Bahadur, organizing into military bands called 'jathas' and 'misls' under the grand assembly 'Sarbat Khalsa' (eventually unified by Maharaja Ranjit Singh); the Marathas under Shivaji (using guerrilla warfare and collecting 'Chauth' and 'Sardeshmukhi' taxes), expanding across India under the Chitpavan Brahmana Peshwas; and the Jats under Suraj Mal establishing the kingdom of Bharatpur.
🎯 Central Theme: The fragmentation of imperial authority in 18th-century India into autonomous regional states, warlords, and confederacies.

Intext Question — Page 116
What were 'Chauth' and 'Sardeshmukhi' collected by the Marathas?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
  • Chauth: A land tax consisting of 25 per cent (one-fourth) of the land revenue claimed by zamindars, collected by the Marathas in territories outside their direct kingdom (e.g., in the Deccan and Mughal provinces) in exchange for protection against Maratha raids.
  • Sardeshmukhi: An additional fiscal levy of 9 to 10 per cent of the land revenue paid to the head revenue collector (Sardeshmukh) of the Deccan, a title claimed by the Maratha ruler Shivaji.

Let's Discuss — Q4
What were the offices held by Saadat Khan in Awadh?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
Burhan-ul-Mulk Saadat Khan held the combined offices of:
  1. Subadari (political governorship of the province).
  2. Diwani (chief revenue and financial administration).
  3. Faujdari (supreme military command).
By combining political, financial, and military authority in his own hands, he became the virtually autonomous sovereign ruler of Awadh.

Let's Discuss — Q5
Why did the Nawabs of Awadh and Bengal try to do away with the jagirdari system?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
The Nawabs of Awadh (Saadat Khan) and Bengal (Murshid Quli Khan) dismantled the traditional Mughal jagirdari system because:
  • Curtailing Imperial Influence: Imperial jagirdars appointed by Delhi were loyal to the Mughal emperor, limiting the Nawabs' autonomous authority.
  • Preventing Embezzlement & Corruption: Jagirdars often extracted arbitrary revenues from peasants without properly maintaining royal records or troop quotas.
  • Replacing with Direct Revenue Farming (Ijaradari): The Nawabs transferred most imperial jagirs into crown lands (khalisa) and reassigned revenue collection to ijaradars (revenue contractors) who guaranteed fixed lump-sum cash revenues, backed by wealthy local bankers (like Jagat Seth in Bengal).

Let's Discuss — Q6
How were the Sikhs organized in the eighteenth century?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
Following the martyrdom of Banda Singh Bahadur in 1716, the Sikhs established a remarkable democratic military confederacy:
  1. Jathas & Misls: Sikh fighters organized themselves into numerous bands called jathas, which later coalesced into twelve larger territorial confederacies known as misls.
  2. Dal Khalsa: The combined military army of all the misls was known as the Dal Khalsa ('the army of the pure').
  3. Sarbat Khalsa: The Dal Khalsa met biannually at Amritsar on Baisakhi and Diwali to formulate collective resolutions termed gurmatas ('resolutions of the Guru').
  4. Rakhi System: Introduced a protective system called rakhi, offering security to cultivators from external plunder in return for a tax of 20% of the produce.
  5. Unification: At the close of the 18th century, Maharaja Ranjit Singh unified all the misls and established a sovereign Sikh kingdom with Lahore as capital.

Competency-Based Geopolitical Analysis: Nadir Shah's Invasion
Why did Nadir Shah's sack of Delhi in 1739 deliver a mortal psychological and financial blow to the Mughal Empire, accelerating British colonial expansion?

Nadir Shah's invasion exposed the military paralysis and financial bankruptcy of the Mughal imperial core:

  • Economic Devastation: Nadir Shah looted an astronomical sixty crore rupees, the world-famous Koh-i-Noor diamond, and Shah Jahan's jewel-encrusted Peacock Throne. This stripped the imperial treasury clean, leaving the emperor unable to pay troops or subadars.
  • Destruction of Imperial Prestige: The massacre of 30,000 citizens in Delhi shattered the myth of Mughal invulnerability. Regional governors (Nawabs of Awadh, Bengal, Hyderabad) realized Delhi could no longer defend them and operated as de facto independent kings.
  • Colonial Opportunity: The resulting power vacuum and internal warfare allowed the English East India Company to play fractured regional states against one another, paving the way for the British victory at Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764).
