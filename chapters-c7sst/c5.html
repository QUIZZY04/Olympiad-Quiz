Women Change the World

⚖️ Social and Political Life – II (Chapter 5) — Gender stereotypes, breaking barriers, pioneers of literacy, and the Women's Movement

Chapter Summary & Key Historical/Geographical Concepts
Society frequently confines women to stereotypical professions (presuming women are suited only for gentle caring roles like nurses and teachers, while men are destined for technical and leadership positions like pilots, engineers, and scientists). Brave women continually smash these stereotypes: Laxmi Lakra, a young tribal woman from Jharkhand, defied all odds to become the first female engine driver for Northern Railways. In the past, learning to read and write was strictly forbidden for women, believed to bring bad luck or early widowhood. Yet extraordinary pioneers fought for literacy: Rashsundari Devi (1800–1890) of Bengal secretly taught herself to read the Chaitanya Bhagavata by candlelight in her kitchen, authoring 'Amar Jiban'—the first full-length autobiography written in the Bengali language by a woman; Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain (1880–1932) mastered English and Bengali, penned the revolutionary feminist utopia 'Sultana's Dream' (picturing 'Ladyland' where women run society using solar technology while men remain secluded), and founded a school for Muslim girls in Kolkata. Over the decades, literacy rates for women rose from 9% in 1951 to over 65% today, though glaring gaps remain among SC, ST, and Muslim girls due to rural poverty, distant schools, and discrimination. The chapter documents the Women's Movement, which employs diverse strategies: Campaigning (leading to the 2005 Domestic Violence Act and Supreme Court sexual harassment guidelines), Raising Awareness (through street plays, songs), Protesting, and Showing International Solidarity.
🎯 Central Theme: Challenging patriarchal occupational stereotypes through education, trailblazing pioneers, and organized collective agitation.

Intext Question — Page 56
Who was Laxmi Lakra, and how did she break occupational gender stereotypes?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
Laxmi Lakra was a 27-year-old woman from a poor tribal family in Jharkhand who broke traditional gender stereotypes by becoming the first woman engine driver (loco pilot) for Northern Railways. Overcoming poverty, studying diploma in electronics, and ignoring social discouragement that railway driving was exclusively a man's job, she proved that women possess the technical skill, courage, and stamina for any demanding career.

Exercises — Q1
How do you think stereotypes, about what women can or cannot do, affect women’s right to equality?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
Gender stereotypes severely undermine women's right to equality in the following ways:
  • Deterring Education & Ambition: Stereotyping women as caregivers leads families to prioritize boys' education, viewing girls' schooling as a temporary stop before marriage.
  • Occupational Segregation: Stereotypes restrict women to low-paying 'caring' jobs (nurses, primary teachers, domestic workers) while discouraging them from pursuing lucrative technical fields like engineering, aviation, surgery, or corporate leadership.
  • Destroying Self-Confidence: Constant social conditioning that women lack rational decision-making or technical aptitude demoralizes young girls, preventing them from exploring their full potential and asserting equal dignity.

Exercises — Q2
List one reason why learning the alphabet was so important to women like Rashsundari Devi, Ramabai and Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain.
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
Learning the alphabet was transformative because literacy opened the door to intellectual liberation, independent self-expression, and freedom from patriarchal subjugation:
  • For Rashsundari Devi, it enabled her to read sacred scriptures independently without male mediation and author her inner struggles in Amar Jiban.
  • For Pandita Ramabai, learning Sanskrit enabled her to critique the anti-women biases in Hindu classical texts and establish the Mukti Mission to shelter and educate widows.
  • For Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain, learning English gave her the tools to author visionary feminist literature (Sultana's Dream) and establish modern educational institutions for Muslim girls.

Exercises — Q3
"Poor girls drop out of school because they are not interested in getting an education." Re-read the chapter and explain why this statement is not true.
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
The statement is completely false; poor girls do not drop out due to lack of interest, but due to severe structural and socioeconomic barriers:
  1. Poverty & Sibling Care: Impoverished parents cannot afford uniforms, books, and transport fees for all children; they choose to invest scarce funds in sons while girls are kept home to care for younger siblings or perform domestic chores.
  2. Lack of Basic Infrastructure: Many rural government schools lack separate, functional, hygienic toilets for girls. As girls reach puberty, the absence of privacy and sanitary facilities forces parents to pull them out of school.
  3. Distance & Safety Hazards: Schools are often located far from remote hamlets with no safe public transport. Fear of sexual harassment and physical violence on deserted rural roads compels parents to discontinue girls' education.

Exercises — Q4
Can you describe two methods of struggle that the women’s movement used to raise issues? If you had to organize a struggle against a particular issue, which method would you choose and why?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
1. Two Methods of Struggle Used by the Women's Movement:
  • Campaigning: Systematic collective lobbying through petitions, rallies, and public hearings to demand new legal protections (e.g., campaigns that led to the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act 2005 and Supreme Court guidelines on sexual harassment at the workplace).
  • Raising Awareness: Spreading messages through creative media—such as street plays (nukkad nataks), inspiring songs, poster exhibitions, and community meetings to educate the public on gender equality.
2. Personal Choice & Justification:
If I had to organize a struggle against child marriage, I would choose Raising Awareness through street plays and social media. Laws alone cannot eliminate deep-seated social taboos; changing public hearts and mindsets through emotional, relatable performances in rural communities directly persuades parents that girls deserve education and freedom.

Competency-Based Literary Analysis: Sultana's Dream
Why is Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain's 1905 story 'Sultana's Dream' considered a groundbreaking work of feminist science-fiction in world literature?

Written in English in 1905 by a self-taught purdah-observing woman, 'Sultana's Dream' reversed global patriarchal paradigms:

  • Reversing the Purdah System: In 'Ladyland', men are confined indoors in the 'mardana' because they cause wars, violence, and destruction, while women govern society with empathy, peace, and reason.
  • Pioneering Green Technology: Decades before modern solar energy, Rokeya imagined women scientists capturing solar power to run air-conditioned flying carriages, using condensation cloud-traps to harvest rain, and cultivating lush organic gardens without chemical pollution.
  • Subverting Gender Roles: She proved that women's minds are not inferior in science or administration; when given freedom, women can construct a peaceful, technologically advanced, and harmonious civilization.
