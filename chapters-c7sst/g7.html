Life in the Deserts

🌍 Our Environment (Chapter 7) — Contrasting biomes: The Hot Desert (Sahara) vs The Cold Desert (Ladakh)

Chapter Summary & Key Historical/Geographical Concepts
A desert is an arid region characterized by extremely low rainfall, extreme temperature fluctuations, and scarce vegetation. Deserts are classified into Hot Deserts and Cold Deserts: 1) The Sahara (Hot Desert): The world's largest desert, spanning 8.54 million sq. km across eleven North African countries. It features vast sand seas (ergs), gravel plains (reg), and rocky plateaus. Temperatures can exceed 50°C during scorching days, dropping close to 0°C at night. Vegetation includes drought-resistant species like cacti, date palms, and acacia. Fauna consists of camels ('ship of the desert'), hyenas, jackals, scorpions, and vipers. Nomadic pastoral tribes like the Bedouins and Tuaregs rear goats, camels, and sheep, wearing heavy robes to shield against sandstorms and searing heat. In oases (e.g., Tafilalet Oasis in Morocco), people settle and cultivate dates, wheat, and barley. The discovery of oil in Algeria, Libya, and Egypt is transforming the Sahara into a modern commercial landscape; 2) Ladakh (Cold Desert): Located in the Great Himalayas in eastern Jammu & Kashmir / Ladakh, bounded by the Karakoram Range in the north and Zanskar mountains in the south. High altitude (3,000 m to 8,000 m) creates freezing, thin air where solar heat feels burning hot while shadows remain bitterly cold. Because it lies in the rain shadow of the Himalayas, annual rainfall is less than 10 cm. Vegetation is sparse, featuring willows (used for cricket bats), poplars, and alpine pastures. Fauna includes yaks (yielding milk for butter/cheese and wool), sheep, Tibetan wild ass (kiang), and the endangered Tibetan antelope (chiru, hunted for prized shahtoosh wool). Inhabitants are primarily Tibetan Buddhists and Muslims. The landscape is dotted with famous Buddhist monasteries (gompas) like Hemis, Thiksey, and Shey. People cultivate barley, potatoes, and beans during summer and participate in vibrant cultural festivals.
🎯 Central Theme: Human resilience and biological adaptation to extreme aridity, scorching heat, and sub-zero alpine cold.
📖 Intext Questions & Activities
Intext Question — Page 49
Why do the nomadic Bedouins and Tuaregs of the Sahara wear heavy robes?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
The Bedouins and Tuaregs wear loose, heavy, multi-layered woolen robes as a vital climatic adaptation:
  1. The heavy fabric blocks searing solar radiation and prevents intense heat from penetrating to their skin during 50°C daytime temperatures.
  2. It protects their eyes, nose, and body against violent, blinding desert sandstorms and hot desert winds.
  3. At night, when temperatures plummet toward freezing, the thick robes trap body heat, keeping them comfortably warm.

Detailed Solution

(iii) Climatic Conditions of Ladakh:

(iv) Tourist Attractions in Ladakh:
  • Famous Buddhist monasteries (gompas) such as Hemis, Thiksey, Shey, and Lamayuru with their sacred festivals.
  • Trekking across dramatic alpine meadows, glaciers, and mountain passes (Khardung La).
  • Witnessing the vibrant cultural ceremonies and music of Ladakhi people.

Exercises — Q4 (Give Reasons)
Give reasons:
(i) There is scanty vegetation in the deserts.
(ii) People of the Sahara desert wear heavy robes.
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
(i) There is scanty vegetation in deserts because:
Deserts suffer from extreme climatic aridity—rainfall is negligible (under 10 to 25 cm per year), and the rate of evaporation greatly exceeds precipitation. In hot deserts, the searing heat dehydrates plants, while in cold deserts, the sub-zero freezing temperatures and permafrost restrict biological growth. Only specially adapted xerophytic plants with deep taproots, thorny leaves, and fleshy stems can survive.

(ii) People of the Sahara desert wear heavy robes because:
The thick, loose robes act as an insulator. They shield the skin from scorching daytime solar rays, prevent sunburn, protect eyes and respiratory tract against blinding, dusty sandstorms, and retain body warmth during bitterly cold desert nights.

Competency-Based Ecological Analysis: Flora-Fauna Adaptation
Compare the physiological adaptations of the Camel in the Sahara with the Yak and Chiru in Ladakh.

Animals in extreme deserts have evolved remarkable physiological specializations for survival:

  • The Camel (Hot Desert - Sahara): Features broad padded hooves that walk over shifting sand dunes without sinking; long eyelashes and nostrils that seal tight against sandstorms; can survive weeks without water by conserving moisture (concentrated urine and dry dung); and stores fat in its hump to metabolize energy and metabolic water.
  • The Yak (Cold Desert - Ladakh): Possesses thick, shaggy, dense outer wool with a soft undercoat that traps air to insulate against –40°C blizzards; large lungs and heart to extract oxygen from thin alpine air; and sturdy hooves to negotiate precipitous icy cliffs.
  • The Chiru (Tibetan Antelope): Evolves the world's finest, lightest, and warmest underfur (shahtoosh) to survive extreme Himalayan windchills, highlighting natural adaptation to freezing aridity.
