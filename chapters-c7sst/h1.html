Introduction: Tracing Changes Through a Thousand Years

📜 Our Pasts – II (Chapter 1) — Mapping medieval history, shifting vocabularies, scribes, jatis, and historical periodization

Chapter Summary & Key Historical/Geographical Concepts
Between roughly 700 and 1750 CE, the Indian subcontinent underwent profound historical transformations. Cartography evolved dramatically, as seen by contrasting the 12th-century map of Arab geographer Al-Idrisi (which placed South India at the top and Sri Lanka as an island above) with the 18th-century atlas of a French cartographer (which reflects modern coastlines utilized by European sailors). Terminology also shifted: the word 'Hindustan' was used by 13th-century chronicler Minhaj-i Siraj to designate Punjab, Haryana, and the lands between the Ganga and Yamuna; Babur used it to describe the geography, fauna, and culture of the subcontinent; and today it designates the modern nation-state of India. Similarly, 'foreigner' (pardesi/ajnabi) once meant any stranger from another village, whereas today it denotes a non-Indian citizen. Historical sources diversified from coins and inscriptions to voluminous paper manuscripts copied by scribes. Society grew differentiated with the rise of Jatis (sub-castes) governed by their own jati panchayats and village chieftains. Finally, British historian James Mill divided Indian history into 'Hindu', 'Muslim', and 'British' periods based on rulers' religion, a simplistic categorization modern historians reject in favor of economic and social parameters.
🎯 Central Theme: How historical contexts, geographical perspectives, political terminologies, and social institutions evolved across a millennium (c. 700 to 1750 CE).

Intext Question — Page 2
Look at the maps of the subcontinent by Al-Idrisi (Map 1) and the French cartographer (Map 2). What striking differences do you notice?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
Key Differences Between Map 1 and Map 2:
  • Orientation: Al-Idrisi's map (c. 1154 CE) places South India at the top where North India should be, and Sri Lanka is depicted as an island at the very top. In contrast, the French cartographer's map (c. 1720s) follows modern cartographic conventions with North at the top.
  • Language of Place Names: Place names in Al-Idrisi's map are inscribed in Arabic (with famous cities like Kanauj spelled 'Qanauj'), whereas the French map uses European script.
  • Coastal Accuracy & Detail: The French map shows extraordinarily accurate coastal outlines and river details because it was drawn after centuries of European navigational voyages, and was actively utilized by Portuguese, Dutch, and English sailors.

Intext Question — Page 4
Why did the copying of manuscripts lead to discrepancies and different versions of the same original text?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
1. Printing presses did not exist in the medieval period; manuscripts had to be copied by hand by professional scribes.
2. While copying, scribes occasionally struggled to decipher the author's original handwriting and introduced small revisions, substituting words or sentences they could not read.
3. Over centuries of repeated copying from copies of copies, these cumulative minor errors grew into substantial variations. Consequently, historians today often encounter vastly differing versions of the same text without possessing the author's original manuscript.
CBSE Step-by-Step Marking Scheme
Hand-copying by scribes due to lack of printing presses 1 Mark
Cumulative scribal modifications over generations causing divergent texts 1 Mark

Let's Recall — Q1
Who was considered a 'foreigner' in the past?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
  • In the medieval past: A 'foreigner' was any stranger who appeared in a given village or town—someone who was not an integral part of that local society, culture, or kinship network (termed pardesi in Hindi and ajnabi in Persian). For example, a city-dweller might view a forest-dweller as a foreigner, but two peasants living in the same village were not foreigners to each other, even if they belonged to different religions or castes.
  • In the modern era: A 'foreigner' refers strictly to someone who is not a citizen of India (i.e., an alien national from another country).

Medieval definition: Any stranger outside the local community/village 1 Mark

Let's Discuss — Q4
List some of the technological changes associated with this period (700 to 1750 CE).
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
Major technological innovations introduced into the subcontinent between 700 and 1750 CE include:
  1. The Persian Wheel (Rahat): Revolutionized agricultural irrigation, lifting water from wells to irrigate vast arid fields.
  2. The Spinning Wheel (Charkha): Transformed the textile weaving industry by drastically accelerating the production of yarn.
  3. Firearms in Warfare: Gunpowder technology and cannons were introduced and widely deployed in military sieges and battlefield combat.

Let's Discuss — Q5
What were some of the major religious developments during this period?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
Key religious developments between 700 and 1750 CE include:
  • Evolution of Hinduism: Worship of new deities, the construction of massive royal temples by monarchs, and the growing prestige of Brahmanas as custodians of Sanskrit literature and rituals.
  • The Bhakti Movement: Emergence of the idea of Bhakti—a loving, personal relationship between a devotee and their chosen deity without the mandatory mediation of priests or elaborate sacrificial rituals.
  • Arrival of Islam: Introduction of Islam through Arab merchants and conquerors, venerating the Holy Quran and one God (Allah). Emergence of two major sects: Shia (revering the Prophet's son-in-law Ali) and Sunni (accepting the authority of the early Caliphs/Khalifas), alongside the growth of Sufi mysticism.

Let's Discuss — Q6
How were the affairs of jatis regulated?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
The affairs of jatis (sub-castes) were regulated through internal community governance:
  1. Jati Rules & Regulations: Each jati framed its own detailed rules and codes of conduct to enforce discipline among its members.
  2. Jati Panchayat: These regulations were enforced by an assembly of elders, referred to in some areas as the jati panchayat.
  3. Adherence to Village Norms: Jatis were also compelled to follow the general civil and customary laws of their respective villages. Villages were typically governed by a village headman (grama pradhana or chieftain), and several villages together formed a basic administrative unit.

Let's Discuss — Q7
What does the term pan-regional empire mean?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
A pan-regional empire refers to a vast imperial state whose political sovereignty and territorial boundaries span across multiple distinct geographical, linguistic, and cultural regions.
Historical Examples: The empires established by the Cholas, the Khaljis, the Tughluqs, and the Mughals were pan-regional, integrating diverse ecological and linguistic zones under a centralized administrative framework.

Let's Discuss — Q8
Why do modern historians reject James Mill's division of Indian history into 'Hindu', 'Muslim' and 'British' periods?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
Modern historians reject James Mill's tripartite religious categorization because:
  • Ruler's Religion is an Inadequate Metric: Mill assumed the religion of the monarch was the sole defining feature of an entire civilization, ignoring economic systems, trade networks, peasant livelihoods, and scientific progress.
  • Coexistence of Multiple Faiths: A variety of religious traditions (Buddhism, Jainism, Sufism, Bhakti cults, folk beliefs) coexisted simultaneously alongside the ruler's faith in every era.
  • Prejudiced Colonial Lens: Mill portrayed the ancient and medieval periods as stagnant, superstitious, and despotic, falsely projecting British rule as the sole dawn of enlightenment and modernity.

Flaw of reducing entire society to the religion of the ruling monarch 1 Mark

Competency-Based Case Study: Evolution of Cartography
How does the comparison between Al-Idrisi's map (1154 CE) and Guillaume de L'Isle's map (1720s) demonstrate that history must be studied in context with contemporary science and navigation?

Cartography is not static; it mirrors the scientific tools, trade routes, and geopolitical priorities of an era:

  • Al-Idrisi's Era: Arab merchants dominated overland and Indian Ocean trade. Maps prioritized conceptual trade nodes and pilgrimage routes over geodetic precision, aligning with Arab nautical worldviews.
  • 18th-Century French Cartography: Emerged during European maritime colonial expansion. European trading companies (East India Companies) required exact coastal triangulation, soundings, and river estuaries for armed galleons, producing maps recognizable to modern geography. This proves that historical documents reflect the material needs of their creators.
