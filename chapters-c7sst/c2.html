Role of the Government in Health

⚖️ Social and Political Life – II (Chapter 2) — Public healthcare vs private healthcare, Hakim Sheik's case, Kerala's decentralization, and Costa Rica

Chapter Summary & Key Historical/Geographical Concepts
Health is not merely the absence of disease; it encompasses physical, mental, and social well-being, depending directly on clean drinking water, adequate nutrition, pollution-free shelter, and sanitation. India presents an acute healthcare paradox: on one hand, it possesses the largest number of medical colleges in the world, produces over 30,000 doctors annually, and attracts global medical tourists for advanced surgeries; on the other hand, millions suffer from preventable water-borne diseases like diarrhea, malaria, and tuberculosis because basic public hygiene is lacking. Healthcare is divided into Public Health Services (PHCs, Community Health Centers, and District Hospitals run by the government funded by taxpayers' money, providing universal low-cost or free treatment) and Private Healthcare (for-profit hospitals, private nursing homes, specialized diagnostic clinics charging exorbitant fees, concentrated in affluent urban areas). The tragic plight of the poor is illustrated by the story of Hakim Sheik, an agricultural laborer who sustained severe head injuries after falling from a train, was turned away by eight government hospitals due to lack of beds or specialized staff, and had to spend his meager life savings in a private hospital. He subsequently sued the state, and the Supreme Court ruled (Article 21) that the Right to Life includes the Right to Health, ordering the government to reimburse his expenses. Exemplary positive models include the 1996 Kerala Experience (devolving 40% of the entire state budget to village panchayats to prioritize clean water, anganwadis, and public clinics) and Costa Rica (which abolished its standing army in 1948 to divert military funds into universal public health, clean water, and sanitation, becoming the healthiest nation in Central America).
🎯 Central Theme: Health as a fundamental human right, the paradox of Indian healthcare, and democratic models of public health provision.
📖 Intext Questions & Activities
Intext Question — Page 24
What was the landmark judgment of the Supreme Court in the case of Hakim Sheik (Paschim Banga Khet Majoor Samity vs State of West Bengal, 1996)?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
In the landmark 1996 judgment, the Supreme Court of India held that:
  1. The fundamental Right to Life (Article 21) guaranteed by the Constitution includes the inherent Right to Health and timely medical treatment.
  2. It is the constitutional obligation of the State to safeguard the life of every citizen. Government hospitals cannot deny emergency medical care under the pretext of shortage of beds or specialized equipment.
  3. The Court ordered the Government of West Bengal to pay financial compensation to Hakim Sheik for the medical expenses he incurred in the private hospital and directed all state governments to establish effective emergency healthcare protocols.

Exercises — Q1
In this chapter, you have read that health is a wider concept than illness. Look at this quote from the Constitution and explain the terms 'living standard' and 'public health' in your own words:
"An important part of the Constitution says it is the duty of the state to raise the level of nutrition and the standard of living and to improve public health."
4 Marks
Detailed Solution
1. Standard of Living:
Standard of living refers to the overall quality of material and physical life enjoyed by a person or community. It encompasses not just income, but access to clean, safe drinking water, nutritious and balanced food, hygienic and secure housing, clean sanitation facilities, a pollution-free environment, and opportunities for education and leisure.

2. Public Health:
Public health refers to the physical and mental well-being of the entire community, achieved through collective societal measures. It includes disease prevention, immunization drives, sanitation, pest control (eliminating mosquito breeding), provision of clean water, and accessible, affordable government healthcare facilities (PHCs and district hospitals) that treat and prevent illnesses for all citizens without financial ruin.

Exercises — Q2
What are the different ways through which the government can take steps to provide healthcare for all? Discuss.
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
The government can ensure universal healthcare through comprehensive measures:
  • Strengthening Public Healthcare Infrastructure: Increase national budgetary spending on health to construct and equip Primary Health Centers (PHCs), mobile medical vans, and district hospitals, especially in remote rural and tribal areas.
  • Ensuring Preventive Health & Sanitation: Provide safe, piped drinking water, universal sanitation (toilets), and garbage disposal to eliminate water-borne epidemics like cholera and typhoid before they require hospitalization.
  • Regulating Private Hospitals & Drug Prices: Enforce strict price caps on essential life-saving generic medicines and medical devices (stents, implants), and regulate private hospitals against unnecessary surgeries and predatory billing.
  • Decentralized Democratic Planning: Emulate the Kerala model by empowering local panchayats to manage community health clinics, anganwadis, and mid-day meal nutrition directly.

Exercises — Q4
'Improvement in water and sanitation can control many diseases.' Explain with the help of examples.
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
Over 21% of all communicable diseases in developing countries are water-borne:
  1. Water-Borne Epidemics: Contaminated drinking water carries lethal pathogens causing cholera, acute diarrhea, hepatitis (jaundice), dysentery, and typhoid. Supplying clean, chlorinated drinking water eliminates these pathogens at the source, preventing millions of infant hospitalizations.
  2. Vector-Borne Disease Control: Poor sanitation and stagnant wastewater pools create prime breeding grounds for Anopheles and Aedes mosquitoes, spreading malaria, dengue, and chikungunya. Proper underground drainage prevents mosquito proliferation.
  3. Economic Benefit: Preventive health through water and sanitation saves families from catastrophic medical debt and loss of daily wages, proving far cheaper than curative hospital treatments.

Competency-Based International Policy Case Study: The Costa Rican Approach
In 1948, Costa Rica made the historic constitutional decision to abolish its military army and reallocate its defense budget to public health and education. What governance lesson does this offer developing democracies?

Costa Rica offers a revolutionary model of human security over militarization:

  • Budgetary Realignment: Instead of spending billions on fighter jets, tanks, and standing armies, Costa Rica channeled its national revenue into universal safe drinking water, sewage treatment, free public clinics, and primary education.
  • Outcomes: Despite having a modest GDP, Costa Rica achieved health indicators (life expectancy, infant mortality rates) comparable to the wealthiest European nations and boasts the highest literacy and happiness indices in Latin America.
  • Policy Lesson: Genuine national defense lies in the health, nourishment, and education of citizens; spending on weapons while citizens die of preventable water-borne diseases is a distortion of democratic priorities.
