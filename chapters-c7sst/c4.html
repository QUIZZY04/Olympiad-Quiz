Growing up as Boys and Girls

⚖️ Social and Political Life – II (Chapter 4) — Gender socialization, childhood in Samoa and Madhya Pradesh, valuing domestic housework, and equality

Chapter Summary & Key Historical/Geographical Concepts
Being male or female is not merely a biological fact; it is a complex social identity constructed by the society in which we grow up. Society teaches boys and girls distinct behaviors through toys (cars and guns for boys; dolls and cooking sets for girls), clothing, and verbal conditioning. Anthropological studies show that gender roles are not universal: on the Pacific island of Samoa in the 1920s, children did not go to school; young children were looked after by older siblings, and by age nine, boys learned outdoor fishing and coconut planting while girls took over home-based tasks, experiencing significant freedom as teenagers. In contrast, growing up in Madhya Pradesh in the 1960s revealed spatial segregation: boys attended open schools and played boisterously in public streets, viewing streets as spaces to stand idle and play; girls attended schools with enclosed, secluded courtyards and walked directly home in tight, purposeful groups for fear of being teased or attacked. Furthermore, patriarchal societies universally devalue women's domestic labor—cooking, cleaning, washing, child-rearing, and caring for the elderly—treating it as 'natural' work that does not require financial compensation. In reality, household labor is physically exhausting, mentally draining, and extremely time-consuming (surveys show women work significantly longer total hours than men when unpaid housework is combined with paid employment). To fulfill the constitutional mandate of equality, the state must intervene through affirmative policies like establishing anganwadis, crèche facilities, and legal maternity benefits.
🎯 Central Theme: How patriarchal societies construct gender roles, devalue women's unpaid domestic labor, and the constitutional mandate for gender equality.

Intext Question — Page 45
How did boys and girls view the streets differently in 1960s Madhya Pradesh?
2 Marks
Detailed Solution
  • For Boys: The street was a recreational public space—a place to loiter, play games, try out bicycle tricks, and stand around idly with friends without any apprehension.
  • For Girls: The street was strictly a hazardous transit corridor. Girls walked in tight, silent, purposeful clusters, eyes fixed straight ahead, treating the street purely as a means to reach home safely without being harassed, teased, or attacked.

Exercises — Q1
Are the statements given below true or false? Support each with two sentences of your own:
(a) All societies do not think similarly about the roles that boys and girls play.
(b) Our society does not make distinctions between boys and girls when they are growing up.
(c) Women who stay at home do not work.
(d) The work that women do is less valued than that of men.
4 Marks
Detailed Solution
(a) True: Anthropological studies prove that gender roles vary across cultures. For example, in 1920s Samoa, teenagers had significant freedom and boys took over domestic cooking in special cooking houses, unlike traditional patriarchal setups.

(b) False: Society begins distinguishing between boys and girls from early infancy. Boys are gifted cars, mechanical tools, and taught to be tough and not cry, whereas girls are given dolls, cooking toys, and conditioned to speak softly.

(c) False: Homemakers perform strenuous, non-stop labor from dawn to midnight—including cooking, sweeping, fetching heavy pots of water, carrying firewood, and caring for infants and sick elders. While this labor is unpaid, it is vital, exhausting work.

(d) True: Society culturally devalues domestic housework, assuming it is an innate biological duty rather than real economic work. Paid jobs performed predominantly by men receive public status, money, and social prestige, whereas domestic labor remains economically invisible.

Exercises — Q2
Housework is invisible and unpaid work. Housework is physically demanding. Housework is time-consuming. Write in your own words what is meant by these three terms. Give one example of each based on the household tasks you see around you.
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
1. Invisible & Unpaid Work:
Work that is taken for granted, lacks financial compensation, and is culturally presumed to happen automatically without being recognized as economic contribution.
Example: A mother preparing three fresh meals daily, cleaning dishes, and ironing school uniforms without receiving a salary or formal acknowledgement.

2. Physically Demanding Work:
Labor that requires intense bodily exertion, heavy lifting, strenuous bending, and long hours of standing in heat.
Example: Carrying heavy earthen or metal pots of water over long distances, lifting heavy bundles of firewood, scrubbing floors by hand while kneeling, and washing thick blankets.

3. Time-Consuming Work:
Chores that span continuous hours throughout the day from before dawn until late night without fixed shifts or breaks.
Example: Cooking meals requires planning, shopping for groceries, peeling, chopping vegetables, boiling, roasting, and post-cooking cleanup—consuming five to six hours every single day.

Exercises — Q3
What steps has the government taken to address the burden of domestic work and childcare on women?
3 Marks
Detailed Solution
Recognizing that the burden of domestic care hinders women's education and employment, the government has instituted several policies:
  • Establishment of Anganwadis & Crèches: Set up thousands of village anganwadis and childcare centers (crèches) across the country to provide daycare for infants, freeing mothers to seek paid employment and young girls to attend school.
  • Mandatory Workplace Crèche Law: Enacted labor laws making it mandatory for all organizations and companies employing more than 30 female employees to provide on-site crèche facilities.
  • Maternity Benefit Act: Expanded paid maternity leave to 26 weeks for working women to ensure job security during childbirth and infant care.

Competency-Based Feminist Economic Analysis: Unpaid Care Economy
How does the non-recognition of women's unpaid domestic work in National Gross Domestic Product (GDP) calculations perpetuate gender inequality?

The exclusion of unpaid domestic labor from national economic statistics reinforces systemic patriarchal bias:

  • Statistical Invisibility: GDP calculations measure only market transactions where money changes hands. If a family hires a chef or cleaner, GDP increases; if a woman performs the exact same labor for her family, it counts as 'zero' economic value, falsely categorizing millions of hardworking women as 'non-working dependents'.
  • Policy Distortion: Because macroeconomic planners do not see care work in national accounts, public budgets underfund social services (daycare, clean water, cheap domestic gas), expecting women to absorb the unpaid shortfall.
  • Remedy: Time-Use Surveys and satellite accounting that monetize domestic care work prove that women contribute up to 30% of actual economic welfare, validating demands for joint property ownership and state caregiver pensions.
